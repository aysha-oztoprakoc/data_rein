Mobile Armored Riot Police
Enter Japan’s Newport City. The year is 2027, a time 
when people in First World countries have become 
cyborgs with prosthetic bodies. Play elite operatives 
at the formation of the Public Security Section – a 
team of specialized agents employing advanced in-
filtration tactics and espionage to attack or neu-
tralize enemy threats and uphold national security. 
Unlike typical law enforcement or military personnel, 
your special operations unit is an independent an-
ti-terrorism offensive force that answers only to the 
Chief Director of the Public Security and the Prime 
Minister of Japan. 
Most information about the Section remains highly 
classified; the Ministry of Home Affairs does not 
comment on the details of its activities. The people 
are unaware that the Section even exists, though 
the National Diet and other security forces sec-
tions are generally aware of them as a black oper-
ations unit. This allows the Section to operate in-
dependently from governmental oversight, cutting 
through red tape and bureaucracy.
The Public Security Section was established to 
meet the demands of an uncertain and constantly 
changing world characterized by invisible enemies 
who can destabilize entire countries from a simple 
reach of their keyboard and by a blurring of the lines 
– between war and politics, combatants and civilians, 
crime and terrorism.

Welcome to Newport City
Learn from your mistakes? We’re only human.
 Despite having experienced several conflicts through-
out its history, humanity has become embroiled in a 
new cycle of worldwide wars. Needless to say, the 
conflict has shaken the world to its foundations for 
the umpteenth time. But if there is one exquisitely 
human characteristic, it is the ability to pick oneself 
up, to start again, to rebuild what has got lost.
In this post-war context lies Newport City, a metrop-
olis representing the new beating heart of Japan. 
Located in Niihama Prefecture, on the northern side 
of Osaka Bay, this metropolis has quickly become the 
epicenter of life, of army forces, of politics, of techno-
logical upgrading in this part of the world. 
The metropolis, built according to a model remi-
niscent of an ancient fortress, is subdivided into 
circles, or rings. The large, soaring guild buildings 
illuminate the city with their neon lights, with their 
promise – they are located in the center of the city, 
protected by a wide waterway that separates this 
area from the middle one. The second ring, on the 
other hand, is a large loading and unloading area, an 
expanse of shipping piers and warehouses. Beyond 
this area is the so-called Old Town, a place that stub-
bornly still seems to cling tightly to the tradition of a 
time long lost, and one that will not return. Although 
the war wiped out many lives, Newport City is one 
of the most populated cities in the world, as well as 
one of the cities with the most efficient transport 
network in existence.

ATTRIBUTES
When players perform an action, they refer to their character’s two formative attributes: their Ghost or 
their Shell attributes. 
Each action is plugged into one side of the cyberbrain. Actions on the left align with Ghost, while those on 
the right align with Shell. Observe is mainly aligned with Ghost, but it is placed in the middle, between Ghost 
and Shell – when a player uses Observe, it is the GM’s ultimate decision to declare whether Ghost or Shell 
should be used. 
	
▶SHELL: The PC’s attribute that aligns with 
physical actions. It is the physical body their 
Ghost is within and can be taken away, battered, 
or destroyed. Determines the number of Cyber 
Enhancements a PC may equip. 
	
▶GHOST: The PC’s attribute that aligns with 
social and mental actions. It is the soul of the 
character, their free will personified. The “soul” 
inside the Shell. Determines the number of 
Natural Traits a PC may equip.

ACTIONS
Ghost in the Shell ARISE RPG is about acting, reacting, 
and solving problems. Each basic action is plugged 
into the cyber-brain, and each of these stems into an 
action wires that allow other more specific actions 
to be plugged in.
Your characters act in the world through action 
rolls. When making an action roll, the player de-
scribes what their PC is doing and applies the use of 
each action plugged along a wire that is coherent to 
the description. Each applicable action adds 1d6 to 
an action roll, increasing the chances for success. 
THE BASIC ACTIONS AVAILABLE TO 
ALL THE PLAYBOOKS ARE:
	
▶INTERFACE: The ability to interact with the 
Net to find information.
	
▶SWAY: The ability to influence another with 
charm or words.
	
▶CQC: The ability to fight in close quarters.
	
▶TRACK: The ability to aim, follow, or stalk. 
	
▶OBSERVE: The ability to notice details or 
intuit things you see, hear, smell, or feel.
Multi-Wired 
Actions
Initially, action wires appear to stem from their ba-
sic actions and to expand in an individual track, each 
action along the wire further specializing the basic 
action in one way or another. That is the basic design, 
but playbook abilities, enhancements, and different 
Shell types – which will be available in the final version 
of the game – can tie different action wires together 
with new actions, even move actions entirely.

ROLLING 
DICE
Players roll dice to test their characters against a 
task. In the game, we only use 6-sided dice or d6s. 
You generally roll 2-4 dice.
PLAYERS MIGHT ROLL TO: 
	
▶Fight in close quarters with state-of-the-art 
cyborg bodies.
	
▶Interrogate a witness who has had their 
memories falsified.
	
▶Rip the hatch off of a spider tank with their 
“bare” cyborg hands.
To make a roll, players must build their dice pool: 
	
▶A single starting d6 that represent the 
attribute in use. 
	
▶Plus a number of d6s equal to the number of 
applicable actions along the same wire.
	
▶The highest single die value determines  
the result. 
Die Results
	
▶If the highest die is a 6, it’s a full success or a 
good outcome. The PC achieves their goal and 
the player describes how things go well. If you 
roll multiple 6s, it’s a critical success, and the 
player describes how things go much better 
than planned.
	
▶If the highest die is a 4 or a 5, that’s a success 
at a cost. The PC does what they’re trying 
to do, but the GM also describes a single 
consequence: complications, worse position, 
lost opportunity, and/or damage.
	
▶If the highest die is a 1, a 2 or a 3, it’s a failure 
or a bad outcome. The GM describes how 
things go poorly. The PC doesn’t achieve their 
goal, and the GM describes the full extent  
of the consequences.
ACTION ROLL
An action roll is what a player rolls when their PC 
does something dangerous or reacts to threats and 
challenges. When players roll dice at the table for 
their PC’s actions, that is an action roll. 
When a player makes an action roll in the game, they 
must describe what their character does by clearly 
identifying their goal and their actions to achieve it. 
They describe what their PC does before they roll 
the dice. The player must link their character’s in-fic-
tion actions to the appropriate actions along a single 
action wire on their character sheet.
The player announces what actions best match their 
description. In response, the GM announces how 
they judge the action’s situation (the risk involved) 
and effect (the potential effectiveness) given the cir-
cumstances, starting the Negotiation. 


12
THE NEGOTIATION
The player determines their course of action, knowing the potential risk and reward before they roll the dice. 
This way, the player and the GM have similar expectations, or time to negotiate before finding out what happens.
We call it a Negotiation because the player and the GM can explain why they think the situation and effect 
are or are not fitting. Maybe the GM didn’t account for a Playbook ability, or the player thought their character 
had more time to act. No matter the reason, the player can continue their action or suggest something else 
before rolling the dice. 
This back-and-forth conversation may take moments or minutes. The Negotiation doesn’t end until the dice 
hit the table.
GHOSTS IN THE DARK
At its core, this game uses the Forged in the Dark system popularized by Blades in the Dark, that works to sit-
uationally adapt to whatever actions the player can imagine for their character and forge a path for success, 
providing adequate narrative justification for the use of given actions. In the Negotiation, the GM assesses 
all actions on a relative scale of threat (situation) and effectiveness (effect).

create new dangers, escalate current ones, worsen

PLUSES & MINUSES
Besides the narrative (in-game fiction) advantages 
or disadvantages that affect the situation and/or 
the effect of an action roll, PCs often have Special 
Abilities, derived from their Playbooks, or enhance-
ments that directly benefit them as well.
	
▶We add a plus (+) before the term (+situation 
or +effect) when beneficial. The situation or 
effect scales up by one level. For example,  
a (+) effect would indicate a Standard effect  
is now Great. 
	
▶We add a minus (-) before the term when 
something has a negative effect on the PC’s 
actions. The situation or effect scales down 
by one level. For example, a (-) situation would 
indicate a Risky situation is now Desperate. 
TEAMWORK
When a PC helps another PC in an action roll, they 
influence the situation and the effect of the roll. 
For example, when one character is distracting the 
guards while another is infiltrating. When that’s the 
case, the effect or situation is improved. 
You have to declare the helpers before rolling the 
dice (and before the Negotiation).But when they do, 
if you help, and they fail the roll, both PCs suffer the 
consequences as if they acted alone.

SOME COMMON SHORTHAND USED:
	
▶+effect, +situation, +action, or +attribute are abbreviated ways of stating that there is a 
positive or beneficial shift in the PC’s favor. They make it easier to achieve their goal. 
	
▶Similarly, -effect, -situation, -action, or -attribute are abbreviated ways of stating that there 
is a negative shift or added difficulty that makes it harder for the PC to achieve their goal. 
	
▶+action improves the effect but it only applies when using a specific action. 
	
▶+attribute is a numerical increase in value (5 to 6, for example). 
	
▶-attribute is a numerical decrease in value (4 to 3, for example).

CLOCKS
A progress clock is a simple tracking tool for mea-
suring success or failure over time. Think of them like 
timers that trigger when something is finished (like a 
health bar in a fighting game) and let us know when to 
move on narratively to the next thing. The GM should 
frequently create these to define the challenges the 
PCs face, but also players can suggest making them 
– mainly to track their own goals.
To create a clock, you simply draw a circle usually divided 
in 4, 6 or 8 segments. The name of a clock describes 
its purpose or what it triggers when it completes. 
EXAMPLE: The GM draws a 6-segment clock, 
named Protestors Are Shot, to measure the grow-
ing threat of the Riot Police.
If an action or task can be completed in a single 
roll, it doesn’t need a clock. Use the rules of the 
effect only for its narrative component and for any 
damage inflicted.
OTHER CLOCK EXAMPLES:
	
▶Hacking a Barrier Maze.
	
▶Disarm a cyber-enhanced hit man.
	
▶Tracking an armored truck that disappears due 
to optical camouflage.
Clocks track outcomes. They don’t determine how 
the outcomes are achieved. Roleplaying games are 
all about solving problems. Half the fun is in creating 
your own unique solutions.
More on creating clocks at page 24.

ACTION ROLL SUMMARY
To resolve action rolls in the game:
1.	 The player describes what their PC intends to do, plugging in one or more actions along the same action 
 wire (Interface, Sway, CQC, Track, Observe). 
2.	 Starting with a single d6 for their Ghost or Shell (based on the chosen action wire), the player collects 
 an additional d6 for each applicable action on the same action wire. This is your dice pool. 
3.	 If they haven’t already, the player describes the PC’s special abilities, situational advantages, or signature 
 gear they believe improve their situation or effect. 
4.	 After that, the GM determines the actual situation and effect. The player can adjust their description in 
 fiction before the roll is made. This is called the Negotiation. 
	
▶EFFECT LEVELS & NUMBER OF SEGMENTS MARKED 
	
No Effect, Limited (1), Standard (2), Great (3), Critical (4)
	
▶SITUATION LEVELS & NUMBER OF CONSEQUENCES 
	
Extreme (4), Desperate (3), Risky (2), Controlled (1), No Threat
5.	 The player then rolls their dice pool The highest single die value determines the result.
a.	With multiple 6’s, it’s a critical success, everything goes significantly better than planned.  
	
The action has +effect. 
b.	On a 6, it’s a success, everything goes according to plan, if not a little better. 
c.	On a 4-5, it’s a success at a cost or success with only one consequence no matter the situation level. 
d.	On a 1-3, it’s a failure and the PC suffers the full extent of consequences.
6.	 The GM describes the outcome based on the effect and determines the consequences based on the 
 situation of the action roll. This may include ticking or filling clocks, creating new clocks, or damaging 
 characters. On a roll of a 6 or multiple 6’s, the GM should invite the player to describe the successful or 
 perfect outcome.

AT ALL COST
A PC can push beyond their physical limits, adding 
one die for each Shell point spent. This happens after 
the initial roll but before the GM describes the out-
come. From a fictional standpoint, these additional 
dice represent the PC tearing apart their own cyber-
netic body to achieve what they want. 
Similarly, you can spend Ghost to overextend PC’s 
mental limits but are limited to just one die per ac-
tion roll unless it concerns hacking or viruses where 
brains can literally be fried. Only in the latter case you 
can spend as much Ghost you’d like. 
THE 
DAMAGE
Every successful action roll has the potential to 
cause damage. When applied directly to a target, the 
result shown on each die matters. 
Tracking damage is essential in some scenes, such 
as when clashing with particularly powerful enemies, 
the Nemesis, when PCs take damage from the con-
sequences of their own action roll, or when the PCs 
confront each other. 
	
▶The Shell attribute becomes the target number 
to beat when dealing with physical damage. 
	
▶The Ghost attribute becomes the target 
number to beat when dealing with mental 
damage or social situations. 
After success is determined, the damage assess-
ment phase starts: the roll is looked at again to de-
termine the damage dealt. Each die that shows a 
result higher than the target attribute damages it 
and reduces its value. A 6 always damages the tar-
get, no matter the attribute value. 
EXAMPLE: If a CQC action roll succeeds with 
a roll of a 3, 4, 5, and 6 against a target with a 4 
Shell, that target would take two damage based on 
this roll alone. The 5 and 6 are greater than the 
target’s 4 Shell. 
Before rolling the dice, the GM can always 
ask a player to enact one of their Ghost 
Memories (see p.23). If the player agree, 
they mark one box of their Ghost Memories 
Clock and add one die to their pool.

Combat
That’s if it’s a fair fight… but who wants that? You 
call yourselves experts, right? You always exploit 
the advantage, and you have the most cutting-edge 
technologies… Each ranged weapon has an Attack 
Value. When fighting hand-to-hand, a PC’s Shell is 
their Attack Value, instead. 
While assessing damage, if the Attack Value of a 
character is higher than the Shell of the opponent, 
the attacker may reroll one die for each value of 
difference. A rerolled die cannot be rerolled again. 
EXAMPLE: Using our example from earlier… If a 
combat CQC action roll succeeds with a result of a 3, 
4, 5, and 6 against a target with a 4 Shell, that target 
would take two damage based on this roll alone. If the 
PC had a 6 Shell (6 Attack Value), they would keep the 6 
and the 5 rolled before and reroll the other two dice to 
see if their superiority yielded more success. They rolled 
a 4 and a 5 to replace the 3 and the 4 rolled before. 
With a roll of 4, 5, 5 and 6, the PC inflicts 3 damage.
If an action is a success, but the roll yields no damage, 
the obtained successes can still be used to mark or 
create new clocks (see Creating Clocks, p.24).

CONSEQUENCES AS DAMAGE: 
PC VS NPC
The GM rolls a damage die for each conse-
quence targeting a PC’s Ghost (for mental or 
social attacks) or Shell (for physical attacks). 
The GM rerolls just as a player would due to 
a difference in attribute ratings or attack val-
ue between PC and NPC. 
DEFEATED
When a Shell attribute is reduced to zero, the us-
er’s physical body can no longer act. If a PC’s cy-
berbrain is still intact and in working condition, their 
mind may be later revived in a full cyborg body (as 
long as they aren’t working with an outdated cyber-
brain model). In the final game there will be specific 
rules for handling Shell transmigration, which can 
make changes even to action wires and the way ac-
tions are plugged in. 
INITIATIVE
The GM can typically choose the initiative order 
based on how they believe the order of events would 
occur. If questionable, the initiative should be deter-
mined in order of Ghost attribute. It gives hackers a 
slight edge over brutes and lets talkers get a word 
out before the actions go down. 
BROKEN
Instead of taking damage directly to a PC’s attri-
butes, a player can also choose to take them to 
the actions they used. Each action can absorb one 
point of damage: by marking it on your sheet you 
can no longer use it for the rest of the Mission.

TARGET ACQUIRED
Sometimes it can be very interesting to have 
PCs act to stop an enemy operative from dam-
aging an allied NPC with Shell, Ghost or both. 
For example, a sniper on a rooftop targeting a 
minister who is speaking on a stage. The sniper 
could act after a specific number of PCs have 
acted and the GM could assign a level to the 
sniper, just like a Nemesis, and roll, also taking 
into account the Attack Value of the weapon 
they are wielding to hit the minister’s Shell. 
NEMESIS FIGHTS
Sometimes you need powerful oppo-
nents, someone who can really put the 
PCs in danger: these are the Nemeses. 
The Ghost Memories (p. 23) are a way 
to introduce a Nemesis in play, but re-
member that the GM can also freely in-
troduce them. 
The GM can make action rolls for a 
Nemesis, just like a player for a PC. 
The dice pool rolled by the GM for the 
Nemesis is equal to the number placed 
beside the Nemesis name - their level. 
Each Nemesis action generates new 
consequences, in form of damage or 
a clock that represent a specific weak 
spot of them.
A Nemesis can try a roll At All Cost

OPPOSING CLOCKS
When dealing with opposing factors or goals, create 
clocks for each. The clock that completes first wins. 
Individual clocks can be marked in parallel. Danger 
clocks are generally negative, as they tick like a time 
bomb toward a new consequence. Progress clocks 
are generally positive, tracking toward completing a 
positive goal.
EXAMPLE: The GM draws a 4-segment The Sep-
aratists Burn Down the Lab danger clock and a 
6-segment Reveal the Separatists Falsified Mem-
ories progress clock.
However, start with a greater number of clocks, al-
though more complex to manage, makes it possible 
to keep each one simple and crystal clear. 
Creating 
Progress 
Clocks
The more segments a clock has, the longer it takes 
to mark and complete. So, the GM must consider 
the complexity of the task and how much game time 
they want to allocate to the scene (battle, chase, re-
search, etc). 
The number of PCs involved in a scene is also import-
ant because each player deserves an opportunity to 
act. When few players are involved in a clock, a larg-
er clock can give them access to many more actions, 
but that can also equate to more opportunities for 
consequences, increasing the chance of failure. 
Use the following as a guide:
NUMBER OF PCS 
	
▶1 PC = 4-segment clock (the minimum after 
segments modifiers)
	
▶2-3 PCs = 6-segment clock
	
▶4+ PCs = 8-segment clock
COMPLEXITY (SEGMENT MODIFIERS)
	
▶Easy = -2 segments
	
▶Difficult = No change
	
▶Complex = +2 segments
As GM, build on what players want to do through 
their characters to open progress clocks. Each plan, 
each set of possibilities, that players name can result 
in a goal that should also name the progress clock.

Danger Clocks
Danger clocks are introduced as complications (see 
p.28) of an action roll, prior action, or challenges pre-
sented by the GM. A single complication spawned by 
a success at a cost can generate a danger clock. To 
pressure players, these should be 4-6 segments in 
length. Longer and the threat of danger clocks lose 
their value. The PCs do not want danger clocks to 
complete. Each danger clock creates a race for PCs 
to mark an opposing progress clock before the dan-

EXAMPLE: Escaping a stealth military submarine 
before it sinks to the bottom with our heavy android 
bodies (that can’t swim).

Instead of creating massive clocks due to the com-

consider breaking large clocks into several small-

together complex goals. 
EXAMPLE: The GM decides to break the spider tank 
challenge up into a series of clocks that must be com-
pleted to defeat it. The GM draws a 4-segment clock ti-
tled Squashing the Spider Tank with four linked clocks 
attached. The linked clocks each have 6 segments and 
are labeled Avoid the Cannon, Avoid the Miniguns, 
Get in Close, and ultimately, Explode the Core. In this 
example, the four linked clocks must be completed to 
unlock the final objective (Squashing the Spider Tank).

CONSEQUENCES
Enemy actions, bad circumstances, and the outcome of a roll can all inflict consequences on a character. The 
situation determines the number of consequences dished out. The GM can choose to assign each type of 
consequence individually or stack them together for increased severity of a single typology. 
SITUATIONS & NUMBER OF CONSEQUENCES: Controlled 1, Risky 2, Desperate 3, Extreme 4. 
There are 4 types of consequences that result from player actions:
1.	 COMPLICATIONS: New threats or mounting danger (creating and ticking clocks).
2.	 WORSE POSITION: Losing control of the situation as the threat rises.
3.	 LOST OPPORTUNITY: An opportunity slips your grasp.
4.	 DAMAGE: Injuries to your character’s Ghost, Shell, or Actions.

Complication
This consequence represents new threats or mount-
ing danger due to a PC’s actions. Complications can:
	
▶INTRODUCE A NEW THREAT: The GM 
introduces an immediate problem or obstacle 
resulting from the PC’s action or trouble, 
altering the fiction.  
 
EXAMPLE: You’re disarmed, you’re  
framed for a crime you didn’t commit,  
you lose credibility with your employer, the 
target evades you, and now it’s a chase, 
reinforcements arrive, etc.
	
▶MAKING THE DANGER MOUNT:  
The GM ticks a danger clock tracking a growing 
complication. Tick a number of segments on 
a danger clock determined by the situation: 
Controlled 1, Risky 2, Desperate 3,  
Extreme 4.
	
▶CREATE A DANGER CLOCK: The GM 
creates a new danger clock related to a failed 
action or a PC or NPC in trouble. 
Worse Position
This consequence represents losing control of the 
situation as the threat rises. The current action car-
ries you into a more dangerous situation.
EXAMPLE: Evading gunfire only to roll or drive into 
oncoming traffic.
Often the PC does not fail at what it was doing – this 
consequence fits perfectly for a success at a cost re-
sult – but it left them vulnerable, making the circum-
stance more dangerous. As the action plays out, a 
situation could spiral from controlled to risky to des-
perate as the PCs become mired in consequences.
Lost 
Opportunity
This consequence represents a shift in circumstanc-
es; an opportunity slips your grasp. The PC had an 
opportunity to achieve their goal with an action roll, 
but it’s gone. To try again, the PC must wait until con-
ditions change or employ new tactics. 
An explosion clouds Saito’s target as he aims to fire. 
He fires, but his target is gone. 
This consequence may result in prohibiting all PCs 
from using a given action a second time on a given 
clock until the end of the current scene.

Damage
This consequence potentially deals harm to the PC. 
The GM rolls a damage die for each consequence al-
located to Damage targeting a PC’s Ghost (for men-
tal or social attacks) or Shell (for physical attacks). 
The GM applies the enemy Shell as Attack Value in a 
melee situation, and using the weapon Attack Value 
in a ranged situation. 
Choosing 
Consequences
GM, whatever the consequences, a success is a suc-
cess. On success at a cost, the single consequence 
never negates or twists a success into a failure.
When there is failure, you determine the conse-
quences you think best match the severity of the 
situation you set with the player in the Negotiation.
There are no absolute right or wrong ways to as-
sign or stack consequences. You’re on the right 
path if you intend to keep things interesting, chal-
lenging, and enjoyable. All damage, all the time, isn’t. 
Diversify consequences to layer the difficulty and 
keep players guessing. 
RECON
Providing Information about the fictional world to 
the players is important. Typically, the GM tells the 
players what the PCs perceive, suspect, and intuit. 
To help catch any other information they want, the 
players can roll Recon.
If it’s common knowledge, the GM simply answers the 
questions. If something stands in a PC’s way, make 
an action roll. If there’s no obstacle but the quality of 
the recon is in doubt, a simple d6 rolled by the GM 
determines the outcome.
The player asks a question, and the GM answers hon-
estly, with a level of detail that depends on the effect.
	
▶GREAT OR MORE (6 GM’S ROLL): 
You get exceptional details. The information is 
complete, and follow-up questions may expand 
into related areas or reveal more than you 
hoped for.
	
▶STANDARD (4-5 GM’S ROLL):  
You get good details. Clarifying and follow-up 
questions are possible.
	
▶LIMITED OR LESS (1-3 GM’S ROLL): 
You get incomplete or partial information.  
More recon is needed to get all the answers.
Each attempt to recon takes time. If the situation al-
lows, you can try again if you don’t initially get all the 
information you want. But often, the opportunity is 
fleeting, and you’ll only get one chance to roll for that 
particular question.

ways a good solution: something is going on behind 
the scenes, but what? Who are the enemies really? 
You do not have to answer these questions right 
away, as players will make assumptions all the time, 
and because of this the narrative will take unexpect-
ed and exciting turns. Let your players surprise you 
and build on their expectations. 
Downtime
After each unknown is resolved (whether stopped 
or allowed to go off), the PCs are given a moment 
to regroup, and the players are given downtime to 
digest what has happened so far. This is a phase of 
play where players can apply game time toward their 
own PC needs or objectives. 
For the purposes of this Quickstart, or most short 
one-shot adventures, this is time for the team to 
speak to one another, heal Ghost, and repair or re-
place Shell. The Downtime phase will be discussed in 
detail in the Corebook.
