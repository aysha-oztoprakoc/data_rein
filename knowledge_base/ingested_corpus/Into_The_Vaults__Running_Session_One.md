# Into_The_Vaults__Running_Session_One.pdf

 
Into The Vaults!: Session One 
An SCP-inspired tabletop gaming framework (v2.1) 
by Jamie Wahls 
 
This game is about survival horror, abstract concepts, and puzzles. I didn't think it was 
very complicated until I tried to write down everything I knew about Vaults, including all the 
weird edge cases and plot spackle that I've developed since v0.1 in 2013.  
I quite like this as a system for players new to tabletop gaming. The rules are very easy 
to learn, all roleplaying is voluntary, your backstory can be “I woke up in this dark future with 
no memory of my past”, and that's entirely fine.  
This document is a combination of prose, DM notes, and FAQ's about how Vaults 
work. For the players, Vaults should be like stepping onto a terrifyingly fast, deadly, sci-fi 
puzzle treadmill while blindfolded—which means that I must strongly recommend YOU read 
through at least the D-Class section of this documentation before you run your first game of 
Vaults.  
Enjoy.

0. Being a D-Class:  
So you're a clone slave in the portalpunk future  
 
You awaken with a start, beneath a bright blue glare. You are lying flat inside a stone 
sarcophagus, the inside of which is carved with runic symbols whose origin you do not 
recognize. The lid slides off, and you rise. 
Seated on a stone bench to your left are four identical copies of you, looking 
bewildered. Each wears a plain gray jumpsuit, like you. They observe you, and you them.  
To your right stands a massive man, blue-skinned, taller by a foot than anyone you've 
ever seen and wide as a door.  His hair is long, black and straight, and his eyes are perfectly 
dark pits in his face. He is profoundly muscled, and sports an absurd mustache which curls 
out to his cheekbones. He examines your face disinterestedly, and waves for you to take a 
seat on the bench.  
A woman of similar size as the moustachio'd man enters the chamber through a door 
at the back of the room, pulling a rope into the room. Her eyes, too, are perfectly black, and 
she observes you and your doubles without curiosity.  
She continues to pull in the rope, and addresses you. 
“We are Draka. 
You are human, and are inferior. We have conquered your race and exterminated 
them. We have given your personal phenotype life because we believe that you will serve the 
needs of the Draka adequately. You exist because we will it. Any Draka may kill any slave at 
any time without consequence.  
You five will be assigned as D-class expeditionaries, and will explore the vaults of the 
 


 
Keepers. You will report all findings and bring any useful information or artifacts to your 
handler.  
If you serve adequately, you will have an adequate life, and your children may be free.” 
She finishes reeling in the rope, and at the end of it is a rope sack holding five corpses. 
She places the first of the corpses into the sarcophagus, and regards you impassively. 
“Follow me,” she says. 
She leads you down a long stone hallway, the sides of which are perfectly smooth. 
There are runes carved into the wall, higher than you could reach, shifting as you pass. You 
don't recognize them. 
You arrive at a ramshackle shantytown, and the odor of many unwashed people 
assails your nostrils. The Draka woman turns and departs without a word. 
The town is a squalid collection of decrepit buildings in haphazard rows. Between 
them, makeshift tents are erected. Spray-painted onto an old, rusted street sign, you see the 
name ​Topside.  
On the few streets you can see, dozens of people sit in an eclectic collection of chairs. 
They seem calm enough.   
A rotund and merry-looking man waddles towards you. 
He dabs his sweaty bald head with a dollar bill. He's wearing a bow tie, a cummerbund, 
suspenders, a blue, ripped shirt, and originally-they-were-white pants. He introduces himself 
as Bertolo, mayor of all humanity. He hands you a coupon for a million billion dollars. And 
then he rather abashedly hands you ten small, reddish tokens, which he informs you are the 
basic unit of Draka currency and can be exchanged for one slaved copy of yourself. 
“Just drop them into your Hammerspace,” he says, pantomiming dropping something 
 


 
behind his head... 
 
 


 
1. Ethos 
 
Vaults is about the fear of the unknown.  
It's a very strange experience for me to try to make sound-bite pitches of Vaults. The 
process of the players struggling to make sense of the world they're in, and piece together 
what happened since the fall of humanity, is such a large part of the game.  
“Imagine that you—you, fully you; with your thoughts, your dreams, your skills and your 
tragedies, wake up in a terrifying future enslaved by arbitrary, inscrutable aliens, who send 
you into uncannily staged terrorboxes to die and die again, in a hunt for powerful supernatural 
artifacts...” 
...but saying it like that cheapens it! Naming a thing gives you power over it, and words 
are tools, and I want the players to build their own tools like this is a goddamn lightsaber 
ritual. If, by session five, they have a sense of familiarity and security... 
Well, it's because they've ​earned​ it, paid their dues in red blood and blind terror.  
 
 
 
Do you want to lead your players through bizarre new adventures, from puzzle solving 
to horror? In a system that allows for unique play—different every week, but still building 
satisfyingly towards a triumphant climax? Do you want a game that challenges you and your 
players with complex situations, overarching meta story and strategic superpower use?  
 
Into The Vaults! 
 


 
 
2. The Rules: An Introduction to Ultralight  
 
The rules system is a profoundly simple framework I call ​Ultralight​, and there are only 
three stats: 
Muscle​, which decides rolls relating to strength, toughness, melee combat, and the 
use of heavy weapons.  
Moxie​, which decides agility, speed, dodging, smooth-talking, ranged weapons, and 
self-deception.  
Mind​, which dictates perception, memory, maintaining sanity, and the use of science 
and skills.  
 
Each player picks one stat they're best at—their class—and one thing they're worst at. 
When making skill checks with their best stat, roll two d20 and take the best roll. (DnD calls 
this ​advantage.)  ​When making skill checks with their worst stat, roll two d20 and take the 
worst roll. (DnD calls this ​disadvantage.)  
 
You'll need to assign a lot of dice checks on the fly. Assume 10 for an easy check and 15 for 
a moderately difficult check.  
 
And that, my friends, is Ultralight. 
 


 
 
2a. Classes 
 
There's only one choice that a player must make before starting vaults: ask them 
whether they're playing a ​brute​, ​rogue​, or ​scholar​.  
 
Brute (advantage on muscle): ​Damage Reduction 1.  
Rogue (advantage on moxie): ​33% faster than the other classes!  
Scholar (advantage on mental): ​(​advantage​ on their chosen ability tends to come up more 
often than those of the other classes).  
 
(These are very loose classes! They'll rapidly become under- or over-powered as you 
houserule where certain abilities lie, so keep an eye on game balance!) 
 
 
2b. Combat: At Low Levels, Pretty One-Sided 
 
This combat system is a really barebones hybridization of Warhammer and 
Weaverdice. The system is, frankly, more designed for exploration and survival horror than it 
is for mutual battery, and it's entirely possible to run multiple sessions of vaults without so 
much as touching the combat rules.  
...but here they are, if you need them.  
When a monster (or Inquisitor, or sentient and hateful law of physics, whatever) attacks 
 


 
a player and the player is aware of that, there are two options: they can ​dodge ​or they can 
brace​. Dodging means that they roll a dice against whatever the check of the attack is, and if 
they succeed, they didn't get hit. Bracing means that they opt to tank the damage—a 
successful brace check gives them a bonus Damage Reduction 1 against the attack. It is 
possible and desirable to reduce an attack to zero damage this way.  
Note: This does indeed stack with the Brute's innate DR1, which is entirely necessary. 
Basically only Brutes should be routinely bracing against damage.  
 
Okay but what even is damage tho:  
Damage basically isn't tracked unless the player is getting hit hard enough to 
meaningfully wound them, along the lines of a broken bone. D-class start with two Wounds 
(except for Brutes, who have 3), and each player gains an additional wound per level as they 
level up. A player reduced to zero wounds is incapacitated or killed outright, whichever is 
more dramatically satisfying. 
Note: Assume monsters are invulnerable. Unless the players have specialized 
equipment for defeating a specific adversary, they most likely won't be able to do anything to 
it that'll matter.  
You'll notice that this system uses a dynamic defense system—the player rolls 
whenever they're attacked, using their best stat, so most kinds of PvP combat will even out to 
a 50% hit/miss chance. If desired, you can tweak this somewhat by adding flat modifiers onto 
certain skills, but it probably won’t come up at low levels. 
 
 


 
3. ​The Setting: Vaults? Artifacts? Templates? HUDs?  
Vaults are a series of self-contained universes—think of them like levels in a video 
game. They vary widely in type and difficulty; some contain horrible monsters in their natural 
environment, some contain valuable artifacts which must be recovered through completion of 
a puzzle or ritual, and yet others are containment units for stupendously vile superpowered 
evil. The labels for the Vaults were lost long ago—if such a thing ever existed—and neither 
the players nor their handlers will know which vaults are which.  
The players are going to be forced into these, time and time again, to train, to gain 
information, and to collect valuable Artifacts.  
The three classifications of Artifact—borrowed wholesale from SCP wiki—are:  
 
Safe​: This is no more dangerous than a live nuclear bomb which will go off if you look 
at it.  
Euclid​: This thing is sentient, particularly escape-prone, or otherwise very hard to 
predict or control. 
Keter​: This thing will cause massive catastrophic destruction ​unless​ you do specific 
things, and those things are hard.  
 
What's an Artifact tho 
General term for an item or entity with wyrd properties. Like...headphones that make 
you ​go mad​, with their ​music from beyond the stars!​ Or a weird knobbly ball that, when you 
leave it inside a house, makes the house get bigger-on-the-inside. Or a giant demon; the 
things I've been calling “monsters” are artifacts too.  
 


 
 
Okay but what is all this about? Can you just tell me the real story?  
I encourage you to wait.  
Your players don't get the real story until they've played through “Case Colorless 
Green”, the first real tipping point in the story. You can read that right now, if you want, but 
you'll have better InfoSec if you don't know. 
 
3b. Life, Death, and Templates 
So here's the deal with death; players should die a bunch in this game. Death is 
cheap...they start with four spare lives and that'll probably actually carry them through to 
B-class. Not that they should know that.  
Whenever they die, they wake up in a familiar vat/coffin, with a B-class medic (Faction: 
Midwives) standing over them, sympathetically asking them how they died and offering them 
candy. The players retain all of their memories—their last moments were uploaded to the 
vault network and printed onto this new body—but lose any gear that wasn't part of their 
template​.  
 
Template?  
I had never really noticed how much easier this is to grok if you're familiar with Git  
Each player's phenotype (​not ​genotype​) ​is stored as a template in the Midwives' 
archives. Whenever a player dies, a new body is printed off of this template. This includes 
both their body and mind, with up-to-date memories, as well as any gear that they've 
purchased​. It doesn't include their artifacts...but a player can access their hammerspace 
 


 
regardless of whether they're the original body who put things into that hammerspace.  
 
3c. What’s a Hammerspace? 
 
A Hammerspace is a arbitrarily-sized pocket universe which can only be used to 
contain a single Artifact. This Hammerspace is accessible in Vaults, and remains so unless 
you're disconnected from the vaults network (which usually only happens when you're at risk 
of having your template corrupted). Every human printed off for the purposes of exploring the 
Vaults has a Hammerspace.  
A Hammerspace can contain only one Artifact at a time. No non-Artifact items can be 
placed in the Hammerspace. No, you can't test whether something is an Artifact by attempting 
to store it in your Hammerspace; you need to recognize it as an Artifact before you can drop it 
in. Good work being clever though! You're getting into the munchkin spirit of the game.  
 
3d. The HUD (That’s Heads-Up-Display, for all y’all NAU’s out there)  
 
All printed humans have HUDs, an information dashboard superimposed over their 
vision. These work topside, but shut off in Vaults.  
You’ll see your own information in your HUD:  Lives, Money, and XP. These track how 
 


 
many lives you have, how much money you have, and how much XP you have. Duh.  
Additionally, your HUD gives you information about most people that you look at, 
including each other. In the beginning, you'll all tag as things like D-2103 or D-2134, because 
having names is reserved for free humans and humans of rank B or higher.  
You can use the HUD interface to safely transfer coins to others by briefly 
focusing on the expandable interface above their heads.  
 
3e. Conscription and Trickle-Down-Brutality 
This expandable interface also contains a “kill disobedient vassal” button, which you 
can use to terminate a lower-ranked human if they aren’t willing to go along with your mission.  
There ​is​ oversight on this. People who abuse conscription—using it to steal Artifacts 
from lower classes, or coerce vassals in non-mission-related ways—are themselves demoted 
to D-class and sent on extra-suicidal missions. Embezzling gear or making low ranks take the 
riskier jobs is perfectly acceptable, though.  
The HUD will let you know when a higher-ranking human has conscripted you, and 
provide a heads-up countdown timer if that human is going to exercise their “kill disobedient 
vassal” option.

4a. Some Locations in Topside:  
 
The D-Class shantytown:  
Topside is not a safe place for D-class.  
Here are some heuristics— 
You, the DM, want to communicate a prison/scavenger mentality; D-class are hoarding 
sharp shards of pottery and making shoes out of duct tape. The aesthetic is 
shantytown-haphazard; blue tarps set up across antique furniture and ancient corrugated 
metal signs. ​ ​As you watch, a grimy child of about six runs through the streets, splashing in 
dirty puddles. Things are really ​hardscrabble​, as a D-Class, and there's not much kindness or 
generosity to go around.  
 


 
People will sell them two-chip glass shard knives, or offer to sew a pocket into their 
jumpsuit for that much money. No one will buy from them though, and anyone selling is really 
shifty about it—the prison economy is disrupted whenever a C-class comes through and 
demands their items.  
C-class can ​conscript​ them—select them in the HUD and give them orders under pain 
of immediate death—and often do, for really terrible suicide missions to deadly vaults, during 
which the C-class will usually not do very much work.  
C-class can’t use their rank to demand artifacts or money, and there’s oversight in 
place to make sure C-class don’t flagrantly abuse D-class. But the criterion for “abuse” is 
pretty high.  
B-class tend to be magnanimous—they’re sufficiently secure in their position that they 
don’t need to belittle D-class.  
They won't meet any A-class until they're C-class and you want to start introducing 
faction leader NPCs. 
 
The Vault Elevators: 
 
Studded throughout topside are Vault elevators, sticking out the ground like batteries 
stuck into the dirt. These all look the same on the outside. 
On the inside, they’re the same basic model, but all distinct. They don’t have 
physics-consistent internal space; they’re bigger on the inside, and some are sized for people 
and some are sized for elephants. Often they have marks from the vaults that they most 
recently went to; deep claw gouges in the golden metal interior, or a faint brimstone smell, or 
puddles of acid and blood on the floor.  
 


 
They have a small control panel; players should have to figure out what this does 
without assistance.  
Keypad​: Players may input any number of digits and the elevator will take them to that 
vault. Feel free to respond “out of range” if the number the players give you doesn’t lead to an 
inspiring SCP.  
Triangle​: This takes the players to a “random” safe vault of your choosing. They may 
not use this twice in a row.  
Weird triangle​: This takes the players to a “random” euclid vault of your choosing.  
Weirdest triangle​: This takes the players to a “random” Keter vault of your choosing. 
This will likely kill them. 
 
The elevator will descend for a variable amount of time, corresponding to how dangerous the 
Vault it’s pairing with is. Its ten seconds for safe, twenty for Euclid, and thirty for Keter.  
Once the elevator has paired with a vault, the interior door will change to reflect that 
vault. A vast underground cavern may have a paired door more of rough, barely-hewn stone. 
A door to an innocuous backyard may be a simple domestic sliding door. A door to a derelict 
spaceship may be a huge pressurized airlock, sized for an entire dropship and spattered with 
alien blood... 
NB​: Once the door to the Vault opens, all players are ejected into the Vault and the 
elevator is inaccessible until the terminus condition of the Vault is met.  
 
The DM may deliver further information about the Vault while inside the elevator, but 
this is optional: 
 


 
 
 
Lefty's Saloon  
There's a ramshackle bar right next to the vault elevators that all D-class know well. It's 
the frequently-destroyed tavern where Lefty, a one-armed free human, runs his hot dog 
charity.  
All D-class get a free hot dog. Also, this is a bare-minimum-kinda-safe space where 
D-class can swap information, make trades, and quietly sit and cry.  
Carved into the wood of the wall are a few vault numbers—none of them particularly 
great—and some information about specific C-class who are assholes.  
Also a couple of apparently well-intentioned pieces of advice, like ​don’t eat anything 
from a Vault unless you have to ​and ​always have a means of suicide​.  
This is probably where the players will be when they get conscripted for the Plague 
Doctor.

A C-class (let’s call him Bruce, even though C-class are forbidden to have names) grabs the 
players from the Lefty’s Bar and informs them that he can kill them with his mind. He also 
grabs a redshirt—a scholar-type with a severe speech impediment who is mildly abrasive to

Their HUD has a popup—“now owned by C-1278”—and Bruce orders them into the vaults.  
 
He ​can​ kill them with a thought, but doesn’t, unless really obstinately provoked.  
 
They get in the elevator. There are buttons for “random safe, random Euclid, random keter” 
as well as a keypad which lets them punch in specific numbers. He punches in the numbers 
for the Plague Doctor—049.  
 
The vault elevator rumbles down for 20 seconds. The inner door opens, revealing a second 
door, a lacquered one with brass hinges.  
 
“Go on,” orders Bruce.

elevator then disappears until they meet the terminus condition of the vault.)

4c. Topside, Party, Cake 
 
When they arrive topside after their first vault, some Midwives applaud them and give them 
cupcakes. I recommend you ​play this song in celebration​. 
 
Bruce thanks them for their service and bids them farewell.  
 
Later, in the tavern, they see Ricky again. He looks pissed, and no longer wants to interact 
with them. He goes to make other friends—a brute called Brutus and a rogue named 
Red—and they'll be joining the Inquisitors later to make life quite hard for the players.  
 
How you get the players into the next vault is largely up to you. I recommend having Lefty 
casually mention that Worthless Child No Hope has a history of telling people vault numbers 
in exchange for cupcakes. Failing that, they could mash the random button on the elevator, or 
they could see the number carved in a table somewhere, or they could see a bunch of B-class 
coming out of a vault elevator and receive a friendly tip...all are fine. Most important is that 
you get them in a vault without a C-class handler. Let them feel that fresh autonomy.  
 
 
 


 
PsychChairs 
434 
Safe 
http://www.scp-wiki.net/scp-434 
 
End Condition: The players name all seven Shards and leave with the artifact.  
 
Reward: XP, The PsycheChair 
 
The players emerge out of an elevator into a 1950's psychologist's office. It consists of a 
reception area, a conference room, and a break room. The break room contains a few snacks 
but is unimportant; the Artifact is in the conference room.  
 
The conference room contains a large conference table, with a single old, mahogany 
chair seated at the head of the table. There are no other chairs present.  
 
Once a player sits down in the chair, they and the chair disappear, and seven identical 
chairs materialize around the table, with seven identical actors in them.  
 
 
 
Hand your volunteer actor the following sheet:  
 
--------​------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- 
 
Your psyche has just fractured into seven constituent goal threads, and each thread now has 
its own body! Lucky you! 
 
Here are the personalities of your new seven bodies. They correlate strongly but not perfectly 
with the seven deadly sins, but...their implementation can be virtuous!  
 
 
Good luck! Act big!  
 
 
 
1 will not tolerate criticism and will not accept the subject has ever been at fault. (Ego)  
 
 
2 will act and advise the subject for their own benefit, regardless of the requirements or 
well-being of others.  (Self-preservation)  
 
 
3 rarely speaks and does so only to counsel the avoidance of activities or effort on the 
subject's behalf. (Laziness)  
 
 
 


 
4 will often fixate on something (an object, or personality quality) possessed by someone else 
known to the subject and advise its acquisition. (Obsession)  
 
 
5 will advise the subject to explore their whims and desires regardless of practicality or the 
presence of other requirements. (Hedonia)  
 
 
6 will advise aggression, active or passive, to counter all difficulties suffered by the subject. 
(Defiance)  
 
 
7 will advise the subject to what the subject believes to be their best long-term gain, 
regardless of the needs of others.  (Strategy)  
 
 
 
 
If the players are having trouble figuring out what to do with the Shards, they should discover 
a sticky note in the reception area. It's written by Dr Bright, and it reads, "NAME THEM". 
Whenever a player names one of the Shards, that shard disappears.  
 
If they kill a shard, the player, when reconstituted, will not possess that part of their psyche.  
If two players sit in the chair, they both fragment, and when reconstituted, form a single body.  
This can temporarily be used as an item duplication cheat, but note that all duplicated items 
disappear when the shard is named or reconstituted.  
 
This ends session one. Hopefully the players are invigorated thinking of the shenanigans 
they’re going to get up to with their new person-duplication chair.  
Note: It is meaningfully less useful in combat than they’re thinking it is. Few of the shards are 
actually useful, and if the player’s Wrath shard gets killed, then the player can never commit 
violence again once reconstituted. 
 


 
Injuries to the shards persist when the player re-splinters. The player should technically try to 
communicate psychological damage when one of their shards gets beaten up, but I’ve never 
seen a player do that.
