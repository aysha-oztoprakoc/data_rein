THE RULEBOOK 
 
This book is devoted to establishing the rules of the game for both Players and the Guide, the 
person most responsible for running game sessions. We highly recommend that all participants 
in a session be familiar with the information contained within the following pages. 
Aysha Öztoprak (Order #52163445)


Introduction
WITCH DOCTORS is a tabletop roleplaying game about the supernatural forces that vie for 
control of our minds, lives, and modern world, and the unique few capable of defying 
them for the sake of those who can’t.  
This small subset of people are called Witch Doctors. Born different. Uniquely aware of 
those forces – some routine, some not – that fight for dominion and obedience. That prey 
upon the unsuspecting in unpredictable, often horrifying ways, using magick as a lever to 
twist human perception and physical laws to benefit someone with both the knowledge 
and ambition to do so.  
In such cases, some Witch Doctors seek out a way to fracture the spell or spells victimizing 
innocent lives.  
The process of uncovering the truth and bringing harmful magicks and those behind them 
to an end is called a Mystery. But the more Players dive into a Mystery, poking and 
prodding at its secrets, the more likely their characters will enter the line of fire of the 
twisted and depraved souls that unleashed the pain in the first place.  
Welcome to our world. From bustling metropolis, to mountain village. 
Only now, open your eyes.  
In WITCH DOCTORS, Players create a Witch Doctor to portray. They will think and make 
choices on the character’s behalf, doing their best to act from that character’s perspective. 
Most things they do will simply happen. Words will be exchanged, implied abilities will be 
used, and their decisions will shape consequences sometimes in and out of their favor or 
line of sight.  
Managing all of this is the Guide, another person who populates the world around the 
Player Characters, builds the Mysteries they encounter, and shifts the agendas of forces 
both benign and malignant in nature in response to the Players’ choices. 
On occasion, the Players will attempt to do something – or encounter something – that 
represents substantial risk or opportunity to tilt the story being told in a new direction. A 
pivotal moment that dictates what’s to come. When that occurs, the Guide will ask the 
Players to roll three 20-sided dice to determine the outcome. 
Once the roll is resolved, the story continues – with the outcome of the roll, itself a 
function of the Players’ choices and good fortune, informing what comes next.  
Structure of this Book
This book is split into two sections: The Rulebook and The Guidebook. 
The Rulebook is where all true rules of the game live. Some rules manage
processes and procedures of the gameplay itself, while others are concerned with
highlighting aspects of the setting key to the game’s intended experience. Each
participant sitting down to play the game should read The Rulebook. 
The Guidebook is intended to support Guides’ efforts in managing the
game’s mechanics, as well as providing methods by which to create Mysteries,
respond to the Players’ choices and roll outcomes, and do so with little prep.
Everything included is a suggestion; an attempt to help Guides direct the chaos of
Players’ choices and magick’s influence.

What is a ‘roleplaying game’? 
A roleplaying game is one in which Players are 
most often tasked with using the game’s rules 
to create a character, whom they control to 
undertake a series of challenges. A challenge 
may be death-defying or not, belong to one 
genre of story or setting or another. Further 
rules exist to inform Players on how to deal 
with the challenges they are presented.  
In WITCH DOCTORS, the setting is an ‘eerily 
similar facsimile of our world’, but one in 
which magick is occasionally, even pettily, 
used to victimize innocent people who are 
unaware of its existence. 
What sets Players’ characters apart isn’t their 
capacity for destruction, but rather an innate, 
extrasensory ability that better enables them 
to sense and overcome the effect of harmful 
magick compared to an average person.

Coming from Another Game? 
This section is to help acclimate people who are familiar with other tabletop roleplaying games to 
WITCH DOCTORS. Whether this applies to you or not, peruse ‘Getting Started’ to the right.  
You may find that WITCH DOCTORS does not ‘behave’ like other games you’ve played. 
Though this may be confusing at first, it is nonetheless intentional to reflect the design 
goals for the game. Below, we will address some key differences.  
1| There is no catch-all Perception or Perception-like ability. There is no Skill for 
determining what you see or hear when entering a new room or environment. 
You describe what your character does.  
The Guide must honestly respond to you with perceivable details. 
You roll when something you ‘see’ may benefit from your character’s translation, 
but some things are obvious enough once you’ve ‘seen’ them that no translation is 
required.   
You are free to spot something in the room, such as an assassin hiding behind the 
door if you happen to check, without rolling dice of any kind. Once a threat takes 
action, your character’s Saves come into play. Identifying a threat early ordinarily 
should give you an advantage unless some form of disadvantage exists.   
2| There is no ‘balance’ between two characters or other Player choices. As Players 
are able to identify their characters’ special powers with a high degree of freedom, 
one may have the ability to blow things up with their mind while another can 
communicate with birds. One is clearly more dangerous than the other, though 
perhaps not as useful given broader situations other than strict combat.  
Instead, WITCH DOCTORS balances characters by making spellcasting a risky 
proposition. A power that is more lethal by definition is more lethal to you as well 
should a mistake be made while attempting to use it.  
You are far more likely to kill yourself with your blow-things-up power than the 
other character is with their bird-talking power.  
Power is always a double-edged sword worth respecting in the game. 
3| Every roll threatens to change the direction of the story. When a roll is made, 
the Outcome is not simply a reflection of whether XYZ was successful or not, but 
how the story shifts because XYZ was attempted at all. This is intended to create 
encounters that feel dynamic and ensure that the game responds to the choices 
Players make. As such, there is no null result coming from a roll. A roll is made, 
something changes – either right then or later as a direct result, whether positive 
or negative.  
On account of this, you will likely find WITCH DOCTORS to move more quickly 
from a story perspective than you are accustomed to.  
4| Situations don’t stay static while your party discusses strategy. Often, a group of 
Players can get into a discussion about what to do regarding a situation. These 
conversations can get… well, long.  
It’s important to keep in mind that the time spent discussing is reflected in in-
game time as well. This means the situation may change radically, by the Guide’s 
discretion, should such discussions go on ad nauseam.  
Getting Started 
It is common to step into a new game and fall 
back on habits built over the course of years 
in another game or style.  
Below are some tips to help come into WITCH
DOCTORS and play it ‘correctly’: 
Be genuine in your actions. Take what
happens around your character in as
organic a fashion as possible and be ‘real’
about it. If the situation were occurring
in real life, what would a character like
yours do? Commit to doing that.
Rock-Paper-Scissors does not work here.
There is no clear mechanical solution or 
counter to everything that happens. Your 
character’s knowledge, personality, and
approach should be your North Star, not
finding ways to check boxes within the
game’s mechanics.
Play in the world, not in your character
sheet. Describe what your character says
or does, not what they’re rolling. If you
have a good handle on who they are and
what’s happening around them, many of 
your choices can be initiated without any
direct reference to what’s on their sheet. 
The above not only helps with immersion and 
pacing in the game, it mirrors how the Guide 
is intended to make mechanical judgements. 
The more your decisions are centered in your 
character and their situation, the more likely 
you will be mechanically benefitted.

Expectations 
Whether you act as a Player or Guide, there are certain things you should do to improve 
the odds of success in your role and help everyone have a good time. 
 
These are: 
 
 Come to the game prepared. Bring 20-sided dice and the materials you require to 
perform your role. Players should make sure they are at least passingly familiar 
with THE RULEBOOK (what you are reading now), as it contains the core rules 
for the game’s operation, while the Guide should be thoroughly clear on the rules 
and setting as well.  
 
 Participate. Everyone is expected to engage with everyone else involved. Speak, 
laugh, encourage. If a Player Character would involve long periods of time spent 
looking out a window into the night, festering in the sullen pain of their own 
soul, the Player should consider another character.  
 
 Support one another. It is everyone’s job to make sure that each person is 
engaged in a positive way and having a good time. Yes. Everyone. Not so-and-so. 
Not just the Guide. Everyone. Be mindful of others and protect them. Each of you 
is more important than any story you will ever tell in the course of playing a game. 
 
 Don’t be a jackass. Everyone involved should put forth a genuine effort to play 
the game and be an active participant. Else, they should not be playing. It is 
always better to have an empty spot at a table than a black hole.    
 
 The Guide is not the enemy. Players should not treat the Guide as one; the 
Guide should not behave as one. The Guide’s job is to create situations which are 
engaging – whether dangerous or not – to provoke Players to do things and be 
immersed in the setting. Beyond that, the Guide should administer the rules fairly 
while also trying to find ways to say ‘Yes’ rather than ‘No’ to Players’ ideas.  
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Disclaimer 
In most RPGs, your plucky group of characters run into everything from reality-
warping wizards to fierce dragons, but the threat of violence is muted by the dull thud 
of attacks and spells as they land, march along their polite circuit, and ultimately fall 
away without too-detailed of consequences as the healing potions and spells start flying. 
 
Violence in WITCH DOCTORS is different. Violence is something you engage in when 
you cannot avoid it. A poor roll or tactical blunder could lead to your character being 
different from that point on. Sometimes, things will get nasty. Detailed. Brutal. 
 
If you choose to play this game, it’s with that understanding.

The World of WITCH DOCTORS 
The following are key aspects and themes of the world that distinguish a game of WITCH 
DOCTORS from other titles. They are included in THE RULEBOOK – where rules live – to 
showcase how intrinsic they are to the intended experience of playing the game.  
 
 Witch Doctors live in an eerily similar facsimile of our own world. As such, it 
should be assumed that all historical events that occurred in our own world did in 
theirs as well, unless otherwise noted by the Guide. Details related to these events 
may vary due to the existence of Witch Doctors, but the larger brunt of history 
would remain the same.  
 
 Though WITCH DOCTORS is presumed as a ‘modern’ title, Witch Doctors have 
existed across the entirety of human history. As such, it is possible to portray 
characters in any historical period or place desired. Just confirm with your group 
from the beginning when and where the game will take place. 
 
 Though WITCH DOCTORS is about the supernatural, there are no real fantasy 
creatures in the world. No vampires, no werewolves, no angels or demons or 
Cthulhu. Anything that presents as such, and in fact all supernatural encounters, 
are driven by the thoughts and intentions of human beings.  
 
 Witch Doctors are born with an innate sensitivity to changes in the Grimoire 
underlying all beliefs, perception, and reality. When a spell is invoked (‘activated’) 
near them, they perceive the change in the form of a Precursor, a premonition 
encoded into symbolic or haunting spectral images or sensations. This gives 
Witch Doctors enough warning to defend themselves from the spell’s effects. In 
addition, they are immediately aware of where the invoked spell originated from, 
be it a person, object, or location. 
 
This special sense is not the same as a danger sense. Thus, Witch Doctors are as 
vulnerable to mundane forms of violence – such as knives and guns – as any other 
person. 
 
 Being born a Witch Doctor is largely hereditary. Outside of some existing family 
history, the odds of being a Witch Doctor are 1 in 10 million and, somehow, 
getting worse over time.  
 
 With their heightened perception, Witch Doctors are naturally more resistant to 
the influence of cultural attitudes, traditions, or beliefs due to their constant 
awareness of the Grimoire and the role it plays to conform humankind towards 
shared principles. Witch Doctors can be an angry teen who feels tortured by the 
constant assault of the Grimoire’s influence against their senses, a hippie who 
perceives their gift as a source of freedom from conformity, or someone in 
between. Most will land somewhere in the middle.  
 
 Being a Witch Doctor does not make someone a mage and some may go their 
entire lives without learning how to cast spells purposefully.  
 
 There is a secret alliance of Witch Doctors families called the Demiurge that 
controls much of the formal education in magick. The Demiurge uses its gifts to 
manipulate the political and socio-economic spheres largely for its own benefit. 
 
 Token, the dominant school of magick in the modern era, is the latest in a line of 
interpretations of the Grimoire. Compared to predecessors, its interpretation is 
structured, easy to learn, and largely predictable.

Game Resolution
WITCH DOCTORS operates on a resolution system – i.e. a gameplay process that outlines 
what happens – that proceeds along the following path: 
 
A roll occurs because a Player Character has either decided to do something or is provoked 
to defend themselves from a possible threat.  
The Guide should aspire to have Players roll only when a good and/or bad result would 
bring an interesting shift in the story, not out of some sense of obligation to roll ‘X number 
of times’ in a given session.  
Following the roll, one or two Attributes are added to the result depending on whether a 
Skill or Save is being attempted.  
A Skill is used when the Player wants to make something happen leveraging their
expertise in a field or practice. Only one Attribute is added to a roll for a Skill.
A Save occurs to stop something from happening that otherwise would. Saves can
be triggered by both the Player and the Guide. Two Attributes add to the roll in
the case of a Save.
Adding the roll result and the Attribute(s) together gives one the Action Score. Since the 
Action Score is the main driver of what Outcome applies to a given situation, the intent is 
most often to generate an Action Score that is as high as possible.  
For every action, there are thresholds the Action Score must achieve that determine the 
Outcome of their action.  
Outcomes are not strictly about whether the action undertaken succeeds or fails in 
isolation. Rather, they determine the tone and direction of the story coming out of the 
decision the Player made. The action is simply a manifestation of their decision that 
triggers the roll. It isn’t, in truth, what the Outcome cares about.      
For example, it is quite possible for a character to attack someone, generate a horribly low 
Action Score, and yet deal a lethal blow… because killing that particular person is far worse 
than just missing them.  
In the following pages, we will dive deeper into each of these components, as well as 
some additional features that affect how Players use them.  
An Underlying Principle 
Where WITCH DOCTORS’ machinery would lead to results in the narrative that are 
ridiculous or prove derisive to the Players' immersion in the game's world, the Guide 
should go with being sensible to the story and allow the system to take a backseat.  
There will be extreme cases that the system is incapable of modeling well without 
bastardizing the most sensible consequences of such a situation or having hundreds of 
edge-case rules that muddy things up outside of such rare occasions. 
A gun firing a bullet into someone's forehead would almost certainly kill them and 
definitely knock them unconscious. Falling from an airplane without a parachute would 
likely result in the same, as would magically tearing someone's heart out of their chest.  
Where machinery and story conflict, story should win.

When to Roll & How 
Players roll whenever the Guide feels that the action they initiated or are responding to 
carries with it enough risk or potential for the story to change direction, immediately or in 
due time. In other words: Is the situation rife with potential whether the Players get what 
they want or very clearly don’t?  
 
Players should always communicate what their characters are doing by describing their 
actions within the context of the game and refrain from referencing specific Skills. More 
“I’m going to climb the fence to get onto the campus” versus “I’m going to use my Skill in 
Mountain Climbing to scale the fence.” 
 
There is a good chance the Guide may simply allow the action to happen without a roll. 
Mentioning a specific Skill is only asking for a roll to happen when it may have otherwise 
not occurred.  
 
When rolls are requested, they are done by rolling three 20-sided dice simultaneously. In 
most cases, the die with the middle-most value – called the Standard Die – will be used.  
 
Based on the situation, or an existing boon or handicap due to an item, ability, or spell, the 
Guide may elect to have a person rolling use the lowest or highest of the dice rolled. The 
Low Die and High Die, respectively, should be identified in advance. The explanation for 
either’s use should be human legible, clear and concise.  
 
 
Apparatuses 
An Apparatus is a bundled mechanic that helps capture actions swamped in complexity in 
as abstracted a manner as possible to align the Players and Guide on both what’s possible 
and how difficult what they want to do is.  
 
A tell-tale sign of an Apparatus is a list of Features with several options to choose from, 
with each option separated by a pipe (|). Each pipe represents growing complexity as one 
moves between the options available. The number of pipes crossed dictates the difficulty 
associated with performing the task well.  
 
Two example Features with options might be for Damage and Range increments associated 
with a weapon, as in:  
 
Damage  
+3 | +5 
Range (feet) 
100 | 200 | 300 | 400 | 500 | 600 
 
In the above example, trying to hit a target in a more vital area 375 feet away would require 
crossing 4 pipes. First, to increase the Damage from +3 to +5; then, moving to a maximum 
Range of 400 feet. 
 
The number of pipes crossed is then compared to the level of Skill the character has with 
the action in question. If their Skill level is equal to or higher than the number of pipes, 
they default to using the Standard Die; else, they default to the Low Die.   
 
Apparatuses are used in WITCH DOCTORS to manage Weapons and Spellcasting, with a 
single Apparatus dictating how spells are custom-built regardless of their complexity.   
 
  
 
 
 
 
 
 
To be clear, Apparatuses exist to preempt the 
sort of debates that can occur in which Players 
and the Guide ‘see’ situations differently.  
 
If an Apparatus is naturally engaged by what’s 
happening, it defines the default difficulty 
and full range of possibilities – not the Guide.

Attributes 
Attributes are innate aspects of a character that grant them a greater affinity for certain 
actions and combine to form Saves that protect them from harm. In WITCH DOCTORS, 
there are four Attributes: 
 
 ATHLETICISM represents a character’s capacity to perform actions which lean 
most heavily on physical strength, prowess, or muscle memory. 
 INSIGHT reflects a character’s gift for interpreting information from the details 
in the environment around them, as well as in others’ behavior.  
 INTELLECT is the character’s sense for logic and reason, as well as their ability 
to comprehend and put to use complex or mechanical concepts or ideas. 
 WILL supports actions driven predominantly by cunning, creativity, or the power 
to provoke or manipulate others directly or indirectly.  
 
Attributes add individually to Skill rolls – ones in which a character’s expertise in a field of 
study or activity is being tested – based on what is being done with the Skill in question.  
 
 
Saves 
A Save is a natural defense mechanism that triggers automatically to avoid a threat of one 
kind or another to the character. Saves are instinctual. Primal. The result of thousands of 
generations’ worth of evolution.  
 
Saves are composed by summing two Attributes together. As such, they tend to be stronger 
than an individual Attribute on its own. 
 
There are four Saves in WITCH DOCTORS:   
 
 DEFIANCE | Intellect + Will 
Defiance triggers to address threats to a character’s beliefs, ideas, or emotions. 
Anything that attempts to ‘change their mind’ will run up against Defiance.  
 INTUITION | Insight + Intellect 
Intuition triggers against any threat to a character’s sensory perception regardless of 
which sense is specifically being targeted.  
 REFLEX | Athleticism + Insight 
Reflex deals with external threats to the character’s physical body, from a punch to 
the head or fireball flying towards them, to a gunshot or oncoming car.  
 RESOLVE | Athleticism + Will 
Resolve contends with internal threats to a character’s Health or capacity to take 
action, including poisons, bleeding, or other Injuries. Resolve also represents the 
amount of Health a character recovers following 6 hours of sleep.  
 
 
 
PROGRESSION 
To increase a single Attribute’s score requires spending Experience equal to the sum of all 
a character’s Attribute scores plus one. If a character’s Attribute scores are 2, 3, 4, and 1, 
for a total of 10, it would cost 11 Experience to move a single Attribute 1 higher.   
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
Attributes range from 1 to 5, with 5 
representing peak human potential, but 
may drop to 0 to simulate the character 
straining themselves to the point of 
exhaustion (see below). An average person 
has a 2 for each Attribute.   
 
 
Straining Attributes 
A character may strain their Attribute(s) 
associated with a forthcoming roll as a 
means of improving their odds for a good 
Outcome.  
 
 The Player must state that they are 
straining on the roll prior to rolling. 
 Any strained Attribute(s) double 
their value for that single roll. Thus, 
an Attribute with a 3 would act as 6.  
 If the roll’s Outcome is worse than a 
Critical, any strained Attribute is 
lowered by 1 from its current score.  
 On a Critical, the Attribute’s score 
stays where it is.    
 All such reductions remain in place 
until the character can sleep for 6 
straight hours.

Action Score 
The Action Score is a number indicating the viability of the action being taken in concert 
with the character’s goals and the state of the world around them. That is:  
 
Does what they’re doing truly serve their goals given the circumstances? 
 
It isn’t about whether the action succeeds or fails in a binary sense. It’s about whether the 
intent or belief underlying the action taken is accurate and sensible – and the repercussions 
if not. Whether it is or isn’t is determined based on the resulting Outcome associated with 
the Action Score generated.  
 
 
Outcomes 
There are three standard Outcomes (Disastrous, Passable, and Optimal) and one special 
Outcome (Critical). Each has a threshold that a roll’s Action Score must hit to qualify for. 
A Critical occurs when the die result on its own qualifies for an Optimal.   
 
The Outcomes’ default thresholds and impact upon the narrative are as follows: 
 
Disastrous 
<13 
Bad things happen as a result of your choice.  
Passable 
13+ 
You technically get what you want but pay a price.  
Optimal 
20+ 
You get what you want. Period.  
Critical 
20+ 
You get what you want and then some.  
  
Saves always operate off the default thresholds above. Skills, on the next page, do not.  
 
When a Player makes a roll, it is customary to report both the Action Score and resulting 
Outcome, as in: “22. Optimal.” There are times, such as with Versus Tests below, in which 
both pieces of information are vital for Guides and the smooth operation of the game.   
 
 
Versus Tests 
A Versus Test may occur when two or more characters act in opposition to one another, 
whether they are Player Characters or Guided Characters. The purpose of a Versus Test is 
to walk away from it with a single Outcome to satisfy within the story. This is achieved by 
operating off a participating Player Character’s Outcome, as Guided Characters do not 
create Outcomes on their own. Instead, their Action Scores ‘promote’ or ‘demote’ the 
Outcome the Player Character would have normally achieved.  
 
 When an Outcome is ‘promoted’, it improves by one step. From Disastrous to 
Passable; from Passable to Optimal; from Optimal to Critical. This occurs when the 
Player Character’s Action Score is the highest.

Turns 
A Turn is a period of time in which the ‘camera’ zooms in on a specific character and that 
character becomes the focus of the game for a bit. As WITCH DOCTORS is meant to be 
quite cinematic in nature, it’s easy to picture how and when to move from one character to 
another. Films and TV shows, after all, have been educating us all on how to do it for over 
a century.  
 
The length of a Turn may vary dramatically depending on what’s happening. From a few 
seconds to minutes at a time.  
 
At minimum, whosever Turn it is should be given the chance to make a Move or two 
Steps, use a Skill to do something, and perform miscellaneous activities like talking, 
looking, listening, etc. Saves trigger off of others’ actions towards the character and do not 
need to happen on the character’s Turn. 
 
 A Move is meant to quickly move a character from one place to another. Though 
this can largely be abstracted, an approximation of distance a character can travel 
in a single Turn is equal to (Athleticism + 1) x 10 feet.  
 A Step is a subset of a Move, allowing other activities to be taken – such as aiming 
a weapon, concentrating on a spell, opening a door, etc. – at the expense of 
traveling one’s full distance. One Step can be used to travel half one’s maximum 
distance, if desired. 
 
It is common to take more truncated Turns, such as the above, during combat or other 
fast-paced situations in which lots of things are happening all at once.  
 
 
 
Turn Order 
The game operates on a narrative Turn Order that is set at the beginning of a game session 
and never revised without permission from the Players. It is meant to ensure that each 
Player gets the opportunity to do something with their character, rather than have the most 
extroverted and vocal Players take up the majority of the time.  
 
An easy way to determine the Turn Order is to put the Player Characters in order of their 
Reflex Save, with the highest value going first.  
 
Player Characters who are in a scene together share a Turn Order and are able to do as 
they please while their scene is active, though the Guide should use the Turn Order to 
prompt quieter Players by asking, “And what’s Ella doing or thinking about right now?” 
 
Guided Characters do not have Turns in a traditional sense. The Guide simply decides 
when a Guided Character should move and take actions, so long as this does not lead to 
the Player Characters’ Turns being truncated or marginalized in any way. The decision to 
have a Guided Character act may be based on dramatic timing, to illustrate the back-and-
forth nature of a situation, or the feeling that certain conditions have been met that the 
Guided Characters would choose to act upon them then and there.  
 
Further, if the Players take too long adjudicating what their next step(s) should be, the 
Guide is within their right to have Guided Characters change the situation in some way. In 
this respect, in-game time moves forward at a fractional rate of real-world time, but it does 
not stand still.  
 
The world should always respond to Players, but it isn’t enslaved by them. It will feel more 
alive when it doesn’t ask for permission to do something.

Health 
First and foremost, Health is a measure of one’s capacity to take action as well as a means 
to track at which point Damage leads to lesser performance, a loss of consciousness, and 
even death. All characters in WITCH DOCTORS have a maximum Health of 50. As it goes 
down, Injuries occur at set intervals, with these Injuries threatening long-term risk to the 
character’s future. 
 
Health is restored over time, equal to the value of the character’s Resolve Save at the time 
they go to sleep for a minimum of 6 hours. There is no way to simply grant back Health to 
a character, via magic, medicine, or any other fashion. They must heal naturally, though 
magic and medicine may be used to overcome Injuries or Conditions (to be covered later) 
that would otherwise hamper the character’s ability to heal or act.  
 
 
Injuries 
An Injury occurs at every interval of 10 Damage to the character’s Health. Thus, an Injury 
is sustained when the character goes below 40 Health, 30 Health, 20 Health, etc. In most 
cases, these Injuries will directly reflect the effect that caused the Health to go below the 
interval in the first place. There is a location on the Character Sheet for each interval in 
order to notate the Injury resulting from hitting that Health.  
 
For example, if a character were hit by a baseball bat on their left arm and this took their 
Health below 40, they might write ‘Left Arm’ on the line for @40. Until the Injury is 
resolved, each attempt to use their left arm to perform a Skill or Save would force the use 
of the Low Die.  
 
 Injuries can be removed by a Skill or action that logically leads to their removal. 
Saves can only offer a temporary reprieve from the Injury and its effects.  
 Injuries can also be removed by the character’s Health going above the interval 
that originally triggered the Injury in the first place. [In the ‘Left Arm’ example 
above, the character’s Health hitting 40 or higher would remove the Injury.] 
 
There are two special Injuries that are identified on the Character Sheet: Unconscious @ 0 
and Dying @ - (negative). 
 
 An Unconscious character cannot strain on rolls, nor can they use Skills. On each 
Turn, they roll their Resolve Save to temporarily waken before blacking out again.  
 A Dying character whose Resolve Save proves Disastrous will die at the start of their 
next Turn unless another character uses a Skill or action that would logically lead 
to the removal of the Dying Injury.   
 
 
Damage 
The Damage generated by an attack or event will usually default to the Attribute value that 
was used for the roll. If the Attribute was strained, the Damage resulting from a successful 
Skill will also be double the Attribute’s normal value. On a Critical, the Player may elect to 
double the current Damage or apply a Condition of some kind. 
 
 Weapons will most often add to the default Damage generated by an attack.  
 Armors feature levels of Protection that can absorb or outright deflect the total 
Damage generated by an attack. Any Damage that goes beyond their current level 
of Protection will not only cut into the armors’ durability but also push through 
to the character’s Health.

Supports & Conditions 
Through the course of play, there is a high chance that Player Characters will be exposed to 
spells, abilities, items, or otherwise that will in some way help or hinder their ability to do 
things. Those which help are called Supports; those which hinder or harm are called 
Conditions.  
 
These effects are applied explicitly by the use of a Skill, either their own or someone else’s; 
either by magick, medicine, or some other means. Contrary to Injuries, which are tied to 
Health, Conditions can be overcome and removed with a successful Save.  
 
 When a Support is in effect, any roll which would obviously be impacted by it 
should use the High Die by default. If a Passable occurs with a Support present, the 
Guide may choose to remove the Support as part of the Outcome’s ‘price’. On a 
Disastrous, it will almost certainly go away.  
 When a Condition is present, any roll which would obviously be impacted by it 
should use the Low Die by default. On a Critical result, the Condition should 
automatically go away.  
 
When a Condition is applied, the Action Score that created it should be written in 
parentheses beside a name for the Condition, as in ‘Numb Left Arm [23]’. The Action

anyone else to remove it via a Skill or Save.  
 
Conditions which cause Damage over time, such as a poison, should include the Attribute 
alongside the Action Score to provide all information needed: ‘Poison 4 [21]’, for example.

Solving/Resolving a Mystery
The following are not strict procedures for playing the game, but rather guidelines for how 
playing through a Mystery generally looks. One or more of these may not occur. One or 
more of these may occur in a different place or happen more than once.  
1| You discover a problem. 
By the time Witch Doctors get involved, something bad has already happened or 
is still happening. You might get a phone call from someone who has retained 
memories of what you can do. You may hear something on the news, through 
your workplace, or feel something aggressive on the periphery of your senses as 
you walk to a store or take a subway train. 
Like a contagion, it – whatever it is – is already out there. Uncontained, and 
affecting people without their knowledge or consent. 
2| You apply a band-aid fix where possible. 
Most often this is about mitigating the damage as much as possible. Could be 
magical, medical, or even psychological in nature. You stop someone from 
bleeding to death, administer an anesthetic to knock someone out, use a spell to 
offset suicidal depression or seal someone to a location so they can't unwittingly 
hurt anyone. 
3| You speak to witnesses, seek out leads. 
Based on your patient or client's symptoms, you determine how to dig deeper into 
the cause. This may involve interrogating or casually questioning family, friends, 
or witnesses. Maybe you're reminded of something/someone in particular that 
you can follow up on. 
You are trying to narrow options. Is the threat personal or environmental? 
Malicious or benign, even accidental? 
4| You expose yourself to potential risk. 
Whether the effects were applied by malice or ignorance, as magick can have 
unpredictable effects, be targeted or incidental, at some point you must invariably 
step into the line of fire to discover the truth.  
Jumping headfirst into the threat, this step brings your natural gifts as a Witch 
Doctor to bear, allowing you to gather vital clues of what the hell is happening. 
5| You confront the source. 
Once you've identified what is happening and why, it's time to ensure it doesn't 
impact innocent people further. This may involve fighting a twisted magician, out-
strategizing an organization, or fracturing a harmful spell.  
This is the part of the story where rubber meets road and you attempt to 
permanently settle things. To resolve the Mystery, not simply solve it.  
That said, due to the nature of magick in the game, it is sometimes not going to be possible 
to save everyone. To fix everything. You might have to cut your losses or settle for the lesser 
of two evils. An innocent you promised to help may go unaided; lives may be lost.  
You operate on a tether of what is possible within the hard structure of magick and the 
forces you confront each time you step into the path of the storm.

Token  
Magick in the world of WITCH DOCTORS has gone through dozens, perhaps hundreds, of 
interpretations over the many millennia in which humans have been aware of its influence. 
Many schools of thought have come and gone, their interpretations of the Grimoire fallen 
aside in the face of more stable, productive methods.  
 
What is Token? 
Token Magick, often shortened to simply “Token”, is a formal training regimen and 
method that enables spellcasting with a high degree of sophistication, versatility, and 
success. Elements of Token have existed in one form or other since the early Renaissance 
but were not synthesized into a single theory and practice until the dawn of the 19th 
Century.  
 
Its name is derived by its use of Tokens, in-born and learned themes by which a caster is 
able to change themselves, others, and the world around them. Its interpretation of the 
Grimoire is centered around this and the use of Articles, or grammatical structures, to 
create spells using whatever Token is ‘slotted’ into them. 
 
According to Tokenists, spellcasting’s historical frailty came from spells that were cast 
incomplete, missing Articles necessary to properly define the spell’s intended effects. 
Without these, it was like writing a sentence without using all of the requisite nouns or 
verbs to be understood. What nouns and verbs were implied were left to chance, often 
straying from the mark by a wide margin. In addition, they claimed their predecessors often 
attempted to cast spells that were simply impossible with the casters’ available Tokens, 
leading to unintended effects or none at all.  
 
Some earlier schools of magick practice, such as Panentheism and Mesmerism, argued 
against Token’s use for decades until the turn of the 20th Century – at which point earlier 
schools fell into disarray and disuse and Token Magick became a veritable monolith.  
 
It has enjoyed universal dominance as a magick practice since.  
 
Token’s Role in the Game 
Most Mysteries that Player Characters confront will be driven by a spell or errant magick 
effect that causes physical or psychological harm to innocent people who have no idea 
what’s happening to them. Whether a Player Character is a mage or not, they must think 
through steps to overcome the challenge these rogue spells present.  
 
Ultimately, Players are expected to solve the Mysteries. Yes, their characters will provide an 
avatar within the world, as well as an array of Skills alongside considerable natural gifts, but 
Players have to use what they gather through their characters and solve things themselves.  
 
The Grimoire 
Tokenists interpret the Grimoire as a combination of grammatical structures called Articles 
that mirror concepts we learn as children. This helps considerably with magick instruction. 
Some terms and concepts you will come across as you dive into Token are: 
 
 ‘Source’ always refers to the original caster of the spell. 
 ‘Locus’ represents the individual (other than the Source) the spell’s effects 
were invoked, or activated, by; ‘Loci’ is the plural form of Locus.  
 Token’s Articles feature pipes (|) that serve to define first, what is possible, 
and second, how hard the resulting spell is. Together, these comprise an 
Apparatus called the Spellcasting Worksheet featured on the back of each 
Character Sheet for ease of reference.

 “Invoke on Sunday at 3pm.” 
 “Invoke 2 hours from now.”

the time identified to what the caster’s intention is.

must be satisfied for the spell to invoke. The criteria provided can be as simple or detailed

 “When someone opens this door, invoke.” 
 “When my plane lands in Singapore, invoke, but only if someone has opened my

The Default Form, Here, acts as one might expect with some slight nuance: 
 
 It invokes within 10 feet of the location it was cast at; 
 It invokes upon the Source, themselves, wherever they are.  
 
A caster may use the 2nd Form, Near, in order to invoke a spell at a location within their 
sensory perception but outside of 10 feet from the place they cast it. Any location they can 
see, hear, feel, etc. at the time of casting the spell is within the range of Near.  
 
There is a catch-all Form, as it allows the spell to invoke at any location. It is traditionally 
used in combination with WHEN’s Trigger Form to allow a spell cast on a person or object

a Teleportation spell in which the target location the caster is trying to reach is outside

Speaks to motive. Intent. The Target. Who or what is the target of the spell? 
 
Though the above looks complicated, it’s really simple in practice. The Default of the

The distinguishing characteristics that have to be considered are:

 Is the target living or non-living? The You Forms would cover the former; That or

All of this gives casters the flexibility to affect living things or non-living things only, which

The Token Effect is what the spell actually does to its target. This is the fire in Fireball; the

perception or belief, to a transmutation of matter or disruption of natural law itself.  
Contrary to the others, HOW has no impact on the complexity or difficulty of the spell. 
Rather, it defines the nature of the change that the spell is meant to affect, and ties directly 
into the Token or Tokens that the character has at their disposal.  
 
Suffice to say, the only real crux of this Article – as mind-blowingly diverse as it is – is the 
following: 
 
 To create an effect, there must be a Token that can logically generate it.  
 Each Token is capable of creating Subliminal, Material, and Astral spells. 
 Each Token is leveled separately, as if its own Skill, to represent the character’s 
level of expertise and familiarity with that individual Token.  
 
It's easy to imagine that WHO, WHAT, WHEN, WHERE, and WHY form a box that one's 
Token fits within. They dictate how it's delivered. HOW is simply what happens when the 
delivery is made. This combination of effect and delivery compose spells that can be built

Sigils 
Sigils have been a mainstay of magick practice since long before Token’s inception. To 
some extent, Tokenists resisted the use of sigils out of a stubborn interest to toss out 
traditions but ultimately found them too useful to ignore. Today, most sophisticated users 
of Token are versed in the creation and use of sigils. 
A sigil is a material object that has been embedded with elements of a caster’s power, be 
it a spell or a single Token. A sigil can take virtually any form, from old-fashioned 
inscribed sheets of paper to elaborate clothing or weapons, so long as it is material in 
nature and can be interacted with as such.  
By possessing a sigil, casters gain the following benefits: 
A sigil embedded with a caster’s own Token makes casting spells using that
Token more potent. By simply having the sigil in their possession, the caster’s
Skill level in that Token is increased based on their Skill level in Sigils. That is,
they are added together and eat into the Passable range. This is a powerful option
for casters, as it notably increases the likelihood of casting without side effects and
heightens the effect of their spells (via Criticals).
A sigil embedded with another’s spell or Token makes casting with that spell or
Token possible. Thus, spells that would normally be outside of the caster’s ability
are able to be cast as part of their usual repertoire. In practical terms, when
casting based on another’s power, the Outcome thresholds act off the sigilist’s
level for that Token and the caster’s Attribute.
A sigil does not cast a spell or invoke a spell’s effects. It is a tool that makes casting spells 
easier and broadens the Tokens available to a caster. That said, nothing is perfect. Should a 
person using a sigil fail in their attempt to use it, getting Disastrous on a Token roll, the sigil 
will tear itself apart in a dramatic display reflective of its materials.  
A metal ring may melt as if under extreme heat or unbind itself in sharp
filaments that slice into the caster’s fingers.
A piece of clothing’s thread may unravel itself bit by bit in a desperate rush to
free itself from the caster’s body.
Whatever form the deconstruction takes, the sigil no longer exists at the end of it and the 
material components are ruined beyond repair or reuse. Tainted.  
Beyond that, there are economic payoffs to being a sigilist depending on the Tokens at 
one’s disposal. Sigils make it possible to grant others access to a Token or spell without 
revealing a Token’s True Name. This fact has led to a large black market known as the 
Caravansary, which has been in existence since the early 1600’s but is principally managed 
digitally in the modern era for obvious reasons.

As with most things that require the character to step beyond the routine, Weapons 
operate off Skills. To keep from bogging things down too severely, the Skills that operate 
them are meant to be somewhat generalized: Handguns, Shotguns, Rifles, Knives, Swords, etc. 
If Players want to gear up their characters with more specific types of pistols, they are 
welcome to – but it would still operate off the more general Skill of Handguns.  
Mechanically, Weapons add to or multiply the Damage generated by an attack, which 
defaults to the Athleticism added to the Skill roll. Obviously when straining Athleticism 
to support an attack, the character will hit harder.  
Before we dive into the Weapons and their stats, let’s discuss some basic concepts so that 
they are understood:  
Dual-Wielding. The majority of people have a main hand and an off-hand. That
is, they are generally left- or right-handed. Characters are free to use a weapon in
each hand, but there are some rules around this as trying to do both splits the
character’s focus.
•
The Weapon in the off-hand always operates off the Low Die roll.
•
The Weapon in the main hand can only use the Standard Die, even if
circumstances would have normally allowed it to use the High Die. 
•
If circumstances are notably disadvantageous for the character, then the
Passable threshold expands to cover the Optimal range as well.
Reach. Melee-based Weapons have a Reach stat that generalizes to Short,
Medium, Long, and Far. If the Player Character’s Weapon is at its preferred

Armor
Armor acts to deflect or absorb the Damage generated by a successful attack; that is, if an 
attack bypasses a character’s Save attempt, the weapon definitely hit the character. The 
question is whether the armor can mitigate the Damage and protect the character’s 
Health, as armor has its limits on how much it can truly absorb before some impact is felt 
by its wearer and will itself degrade over time.  
Protection 
Protection represents the amount of Damage in a single attack that the armor can 
deflect and absorb, as well as how near it is to being broken. This is communicated 
through a visual shorthand using boxes that are filled over time, attack after attack.  
A small box – k  – represents an increment of 5 Damage. Thus, Protection with a small 
box is able to deflect Damage less than 5 or absorb up to 5 Damage stemming from a 
single attack. Anything beyond 5 would pass on to the wearer’s Health.  
If an attack against the armor generates up to 4 Damage, the attack is deflected.
This means that no portion of the attack gets through and the armor was not hurt
by the impact. The small box is not filled in and Health remains as-is.
If an attack against the armor generates 5+ Damage, the attack is absorbed. This
means the armor sustained 5 Damage on behalf of the character. Any remaining
Damage hits the character’s Health. The small box is filled in; any forthcoming
attack hits the next box in line, if there is one.
A large box –        – represents an increment of 10 Damage and works in the same fashion 
as the small box, except it is more able to deflect attacks. It appears in more robust armors, 
often with metal components at their core.  
It is common to see even the strongest armor degrade in performance and durability as it 
absorbs more and more Damage. An example would look like this –  
– in which the armor becomes less able to deflect attacks over time. Once all squares are 
filled in, the armor is broken to such an extent that it no longer offers any Protection and 
would need to be replaced. 
Coverage
Coverage identifies what portion of a character’s body the armor protects. For example, a 
Ballistics Vest would protect the Torso, but leave the rest of the character’s body exposed. 
As most attacks default to the Torso, this may be good enough in most cases but not all.   
Areas of Coverage are: 
Head
Torso
Arm / Arms
Leg / Legs
Vulnerabilities 
Many armors are strong against certain types of Damage but see their ability to protect a 
character erode against others. For example, Chain Mail was great against weapons with 
the Slashing Property but faced issues against those with Piercing.  
If an armor goes up against a weapon that it has a Vulnerability to, its current increment 
of Protection is reduced by 1 size. Thus, a large box would count as a small box; a small 
box would count as nothing at all.  
All attacks default to hitting center mass; i.e. 
the Torso. If an Arm or Leg is targeted, it acts 
as an additional pipe (|) being crossed. 
Hitting someone in the head doubles the 
Damage that affects Health but acts as two 
additional pipes (||) being crossed.  
When Ballistics is noted as a Vulnerability, 
this refers to projectiles fired from a gun of 
some kind, be it a handgun, rifle, machine-
gun, etc. Most armor is weak against guns.

Character Evolution 
As characters struggle in pursuit of their interests and the Mysteries beset upon them, they 
evolve. Some of this evolution is linear; some is not. Sometimes it’s an easy sense of pure 
mechanical progression, marked by making a number or two on their Character Sheet a 
notch higher.  
Sometimes, it redefines aspects of the character’s identity. 
This evolution can come in whispers or in screams. 
A single, numeric step or an explosion.  
In this section, we will discuss both types of evolution – Sequential and Traumatic. 
Sequential Evolution 
Sequential Evolution is about Experience in the pure mathematical sense. As a character 
resolves Mysteries and pursues Private Goals, they acquire Experience that can be used to 
progress their Attributes, Tokens, or Skills.  
When a Mystery is resolved, the character receives 3 Experience. This should be added to 
the Experience boxes on their Character Sheet. This can either be used immediately to 
progress an Attribute, Token, or Skill or held onto for later use. 
When a Private Goal is completed, the character receives 3 Experience. As before, this can 
be held onto or immediately used. To complete a Private Goal, the story revolving around 
it must reach a point where the Guide can ask the Player, “Did your character get what 
they wanted from that?” This forces the Player to honestly assess what the character wanted 
from the Private Goal in the first place and if what has happened satisfied that drive – that 
innately personal pursuit – or not.  
If it did, the Experience is awarded. 
If it didn’t, the Private Goal is revised. Pivoted towards something that makes more sense 
or will satisfy the yearning the character feels.   
Traumatic Evolution 
The events in WITCH DOCTORS can have horrifying and life-altering effects for the 
characters involved. They may cause innocent people to die; their loved-ones may be 
seriously harmed, either at their own hand or another’s. They may experience something 
that is beyond human comprehension; something that forces them to think of the world 
and their place in it in an entirely new way. When one or more of these things occurs, it is 
an example of Traumatic Evolution.  
Where Sequential Evolution is about stepping a number up, Traumatic Evolution is 
about witnessing the transformation of a human being’s attitudes and beliefs. And this 
transformation requires that the Player portray the character differently going forward.  
To that point: Traumatic Evolution is the only way to get new Tokens. Period. 
Because, in the end, the character isn’t the same anymore. They cannot be. And the new 
Token is a reflection of that core change in who the character is.  
When a character experiences something that is traumatizing in a session that the Player 
feels could change the character’s beliefs or attitudes in some clear way, they should attempt 
to define both the trauma and the result as near to the event as possible.  
Capturing the Event should be straight-forward.

THE GUIDEBOOK 
 
Though principally intended as a resource for Guides, THE GUIDEBOOK can be read by anyone 
participating in the game. Unlike content in THE RULEBOOK, everything that follows is merely

The Guidebook
WITCH DOCTORS was designed to make guiding the game as easy as possible without 
dumbing down what Players could do within the setting. It should be possible for Guides 
to spend a minimal amount of time preparing for sessions without those sessions feeling 
empty of content or fully removing initiative from the Players.  
The foundation for guiding stories within WITCH DOCTORS is that the world should feel 
alive. Lived-in. Structured less as a backdrop and more as a character unto itself that will 
ebb and flow, twist and turn, bend and break.  
It doesn’t wait for the Player Characters, but it does respond to them. 
THE GUIDEBOOK is intended to provide Guides strategies and methods to help make the 
world feel as real and responsive as possible, with people who behave like people and 
antagonists who do more than sit on a throne waiting to be brought to heel.  
None of what follows are rules. Those live in THE RULEBOOK. These are just ways to 
make your life as a Guide more livable and fun.    
Structures of Play 
There are two primary drives a Guide uses when running WITCH DOCTORS: Mysteries 
and Private Goals. 
A Mystery is a story the Guide devises that inevitably pits the Player Characters
against a person or group leveraging magick to victimize innocent people for their
own gain. Any standard mystery fiction setup should work, from theft and murder
to arson and blackmail, only tying back into the unique features of the setting.
Some examples might be:
o
A celebrity inexplicably starts writing death threats to herself while
asleep; the Player Characters are asked to dig into the cause and to
ascertain whether the threats are legitimate.
o
A man who recently won a large settlement from a medical college is
found dead, somehow killed using a blade of grass.
o
A mysterious book is stolen from a Demiurge member’s home
without triggering any alarms, magical or otherwise.
A Private Goal is something uniquely personal to each character; something that
reflects their own aspirations, hang-ups, or concerns that they are compelled to
address with or without anyone else’s help. It may stem from incidents taking
place within a Mystery or speak more specifically to the character’s background
and own personal, evolving narrative.
o
The character recently found information about their birth mother,
who was believed to be dead for the last twenty years.
o
The character’s ex seems to be under the influence of a secret
benefactor, causing them to be uncharacteristically negligent
towards their son.
o
Following a recent Mystery, the character wants to develop new
Tokens to keep from being caught off-guard again.
It is usually helpful to intersperse Mysteries with sessions devoted to Private Goals, to give 
Players the chance to direct where things head and only introduce Mysteries where the 
Player Characters begin to lose steam.

Guiding Mysteries
A Mystery acts as an effective way to get new Player Characters up and running or to rev 
the story engine of more longstanding characters by forcing them out of their comfort zone 
and into circumstances that challenge them. This adds drama and excitement, but it also 
grounds (or reaffirms) who the characters are and what they are willing to do when pushed 
into a corner. 
What and who do they care about?
What drives them to get involved in the first place?
What are they willing to do to satisfy one or both of the above?
Often, Mysteries act as launching points for more Player-directed sessions in the future, in 
which their characters’ Private Goals drive the story forward until it’s time to shake things 
up again. Which can, if desired, lead to other Mysteries, other Private Goals.  
So on and so on. 
Key Terms & Concepts for Mysteries 
WITCH DOCTORS is meant to respond to the Players’ actions; to illustrate that nothing 
operates in a vacuum or is completely without risk. Bad rolls have consequences, but so do 
the choices Player Characters make. Antagonists will rarely, if ever, sit back while the Player 
Characters tear down the world around them. It’s their world, too, after all. They likely 
have reasons to keep it standing.   
With this in mind, there are a handful of concepts that tie into guiding the game that – 
while top of mind – will keep Mysteries moving and bring the setting’s inhabitants to life. 
Agenda 
An Agenda defines the underlying motives and goals for why events occurred that led into 
a Mystery. Agendas have an Owner, who often acts as the antagonist of the game and will 
respond to Players’ intrusion as Player Characters’ activities become more obvious.  
An Agenda is often structured as a simple timeline of events, showing what happened 
before, during, and after the event that led to the Player Characters getting involved in the 
first place. Anything on the timeline that has yet to happen reflects what the owner has 
planned or what would happen if the Player Characters weren’t to derail things.  
Inciting Event 
Ordinarily a pre-written, dramatic sequence that the Guide reads through, the Inciting 
Event is what occurred that triggered the Player Characters’ involvement and serves the 
purpose of immersing Players in the tone, cast, and setting of the Mystery. The Inciting 
Event can take place at any point along the Agenda’s timeline – near the beginning, 
middle, or end. The Inciting Event can be central to the Agenda’s purpose, a stepping 
stone towards a larger goal, or a tangent the Owner indulged in along the way.  
Owner 
The Owner of the Agenda underlying a Mystery is principally the main antagonist against 
the Player Characters. The Owner may be an individual or group, but in either case will 
seek to control possible threats against them via the Player Characters’ activities. Though 
the Owner is set against the Player Characters, it is best when they aren’t cartoonishly evil, 
but rather operating under relatable motives and sometimes even out of desperation. Some 
may be truly bad people, while others may just have gotten in over their head.    
Retaliation 
As Player Characters dive into a Mystery, their activities will inevitably lead to the Owner 
becoming aware that someone is poking into things, questioning people, or disrupting

ongoing elements of the Owner’s work. When this occurs, they may attempt to disrupt, 
distract, or otherwise negate the threat that the Player Characters pose to them through an 
act of Retaliation.   
Such acts can be broad in nature: Destroying evidence, silencing witnesses (or accessories) 
to the crime, causing disturbances at the Player Characters’ workplaces, threatening family 
or friends, offering bribes, or perpetrating violence – lethal or otherwise – against the 
Player Characters themselves. Often, intelligent and resourceful Owners will seek discrete 
methods for throwing Players off the scent or otherwise forcing their focus elsewhere.  
Developing the Agenda’s Owner 
One of the most important things for the Guide to do in preparation for a Mystery is 
grasping the person or people most responsible for it happening: The Agenda’s Owner. 
This is a key step as it helps the Guide intuitively understand the Owner’s mentality and 
attitudes, generate acts of Retaliation that are distinctive from one Agenda Owner to 
another, and know what the Owner wants to protect or is willing to part with.  
A good starting point for developing an Owner is to look at the Player Characters and 
their story up to now. It can be impactful to have elements that connect to the Player 
Characters’ backgrounds or immediate history, or to have the Owner reflect an aspect of a 
Player Character’s attitude. Though it isn’t always possible, stories tend to have more 
resonance when the Player Characters can ultimately relate to the Owner in some fashion 
and recognize ideas or feelings that they themselves share. 
All Owners in WITCH DOCTORS are human beings. 
They may be bent or broken.  
Greedy. Passionate. Lost. Egotistical. Scared.   
The point isn’t to worry about how the Owner mechanically relates to the Player 
Characters. That should take little time. More effort should be spent on developing an 
Owner that sets itself apart and is willing and able to use Retaliation against the Player 
Characters in unique or interesting ways.    
What does the Owner want and why? What are they willing to do to get it?
How does this sort of person or group challenge the Player Characters?
Building the Agenda 
Once you know who the Owner is and what they want, it’s time to construct the Agenda 
that the Mystery builds upon. As mentioned before, this is a timeline of events similar to 
what you might see in a history book that ends with the Owner’s goal being accomplished 
and has points prior to that representing events that somehow contributed to the outcome. 
The number of points on the timeline can vary. A good starting point is 3 or 4 leading up 
to the goal, though the number can grow dramatically based on the goal’s complexity and 
the population of people/groups the Owner has to contend with to make it happen. 
Someone trying to get revenge against a single person with no particular
safeguards or protection? The timeline is probably short.
Someone trying to take over a branch of the Demiurge through blackmail,
murder, and political alliances? Likely a dozen or more points long as there are
so many people to appease or remove who could get in the way.
Keep in mind, the number of points does not necessarily reflect how long or hard solving 
the Mystery is. An Agenda with three points may lead to six sessions; another with a dozen 
points may be solved in two. Don’t feel you need to pad things. Players will do that for you.

Placing the Inciting Event 
The starting point for a Mystery is the Inciting Event. It sets the stage, establishes the 
stakes, and piques the Players’ interest. Point in fact, the Inciting Event is the only 
component of a Mystery the Guide is entirely responsible for. In essence, the Inciting 
Event kicks things into motion. The rest of a Mystery is driven by the Players’ choices, the 
Outcomes of rolls, and points at which the Guide determines that Retaliation should 
realistically occur.  
But where the Inciting Event fits into the Agenda’s chronology matters. It may be one of 
the key, contributing points to the goal, or some tangent that the Owner took for other 
reasons. It may happen after the Agenda was completed that forces the Players to examine 
elements already long passed.  
Nothing about this decision should be based on making it harder for the Player Characters 
to learn information or find leads. Rather, it should be predicated on how the decision 
makes the Mystery more interesting or amps up the likelihood of the Owner using 
Retaliation. 
If the Inciting Event happens near or as the first point in the Agenda, there is a very 
narrow range of options for the Players to explore at first but other events on the Agenda 
spiral out rapidly in real-time. This gives Players the sense that the world is changing with 
or without them; that something is building, powering up; that the stakes already apparent 
are not the only stakes in play. 
It’s a race against time against an invisible opponent. 
If the Inciting Event happens near the middle or end of the timeline, there is a lot more 
for the Player Characters to dive into immediately – to the point of overload, in fact – and 
the stakes have already been raised more than once. Oddly enough, Mysteries with the 
Inciting Event later in the Agenda tend to run longer than those where it occurs early.  
This is due to Players being overwhelmed by the amount of stuff they can dive into and the 
likelihood of the Owner learning of their involvement and pivoting to compensate or even 
wiping out evidence or witnesses as part of Retaliation.  
It’s a fight against a stronger opponent in a sense, as the Owner has had time to spread 
their influence and effort across each of the areas their Agenda touches.  
With these in mind, set the timing of the Inciting Event. 
Writing the Inciting Event 
An Inciting Event can take many forms but is generally written as a script or short story – 
ideally no longer than 2 or 3 pages – that the Guide can either distribute to the Players or 
read aloud as the Guided Character telling the Players the story and asking for their help.  
Key focal points when writing Inciting Event are to: 
Establish the tone and setting of the Mystery
Introduce one or more Guided Characters who will be involved
Set up the stakes for the Mystery and the Players’ involvement
Though most Inciting Events should feel conversational, the tone and setting of the 
Mystery may dictate something different – from a formal letter written by a clergyman a 
decade ago, to an email from a stranger written in binary.  
The kind of story you want to convey and encourage should dictate the form and nature of 
the Inciting Event. It is something that happens in-setting, not out.

Solving the Mystery 
Following the Inciting Event, the game is afoot: The Players are unleashed on the world 
and begin poking and prodding. Powers may be used. They may go here, there, and 
everywhere, either following evidence or some stubborn figment in their own minds. 
 
Let them.  
 
Depending on what they do and how they roll, you may reveal something earlier, later, or 
never at all. Connections you didn’t even know existed may appear as a result of a roll 
forcing you to take the story a new way. This isn’t Agatha Christie. Mysteries in the real 
world are messy affairs, with haphazard clues, witnesses that talk too much or too little, and 
nary a wrap-up scene at the end in sight. 
 
One thing you should be prepared to do is perform a Recap from time to time.  
 
Pause things at the end of a scene and recount what was learned, found, or discussed. 
Sometimes Players can get lost in every detail. The Recap is there to help them prioritize 
without doing it for them.   
 
In the end, however, solving the Mystery is often not enough.   
 
Resolving the Mystery 
Solving a Mystery may take half a session or a handful of whole ones. But that’s only the 
halfway point: Now that you know what has happened or is still happening, what are you 
going to do about it?  
 
The answer gets to the heart of resolving the Mystery.  
 
 Cure the injured person 
 Stop the spell imperiling dozens 
 Capture the criminal and return what was taken 
 Walk away and protect you and yours 
 
Depending on the Player Characters, resolving a Mystery might go in wildly different ways. 
More violent characters may lean on that affinity, while others who abstain from direct 
conflict may have to use guile or spellcraft to bring things to a close. The Players may not 
even be able to help those they promised to. Even others may die or have their lives ruined, 
whether by incident or malice. 
 
Solving a Mystery could be an act of curiosity.  
Resolving a Mystery is an act that defines who a character is.  
 
 Not everyone is a hero prepared to run through fire  
 Not everyone is a coward despite how they may appear  
 Not everyone is as bound by morality as they behave  
 
Characters who act selfishly may surprise you with their willingness to put their lives on the 
line. Others, so gung-ho at the beginning, may falter when faced with something they truly 
know is out of their league.  
 
It is not hard to die in WITCH DOCTORS. Every Player Character may fall trying to resolve 
a Mystery in which they are in over their heads but refuse to stop out of courage, ethics, 
guilt, or stupidity. As the Guide, you have to be willing to let that happen. Where there is 
an in-fiction, clear way to save them, do so. But where there isn’t without eroding any sense 
of danger to the world in which the game takes place?  
 
The dice fell where they did.

Best Practices 
Over the course of many sessions, Guides will develop their own toolbox of methods and 
tricks to employ that pay dividends with their unique group of Players. Prior to that point, 
there are a number of behaviors that are recommended for delivering on the crux of the 
game and getting Players immersed into the story and world. 
 
Outcomes are Storytelling 
Outcome resolution is the process of taking the Outcome of a roll and translating it into 
the story. Not merely from a question of ‘Did they succeed or fail?’, but ‘How does the 
situation change because of the Outcome provided?” In fact, it’s often problematic to think 
of Outcomes as a Yes/No with one specific, transactional result, or as a determinant of 
Success or Failure on the specific action that triggered a roll in the first place. Depending 
on the circumstances, the action may not have been completed or even started based on 
the roll’s Outcome.   
 
Outcomes aren’t really concerned with whether the character’s attempt to jump over the 
fence led them to fall to the ground or gracefully launch themselves to the other side. It 
cares about how the story changes because they attempted to climb it in the first place.  
 
Does it become more favorable or less? Or a bit of both? 
 
In a more traditional way of dealing with the resolution of the example above, you might 
get something like the following: 
 
Guide 
So what is Eris doing? 
Player 
She's going to try to climb the fence and get to the other side. Could I use her rock-
climbing Skill for this?  
Guide 
Sure.  
Player 
[rolls] Oh god. 11. Disastrous. 
Guide 
As you're climbing up, the fence is still wet from rain early in the day, and your fingers 
slip on the links. You feel yourself plummet backwards and land flat on your back. 
You take 6 points of Damage based on how far you fell.   
 
And that’s fine. However, an option with Outcomes may be a bit different:  
 
Guide 
So what is Eris doing? 
Player 
She's going to try to climb the fence and get to the other side. Could I use her 
rock-climbing Skill for this? 
Guide 
Sure. 
Player

guards from the parking lot. They're within 30 feet of you when one shouts,  
"Freeze!", as they reach for the pistols on their hips. 
Player

Guide 
What is Eris doing? 
 
The first clearly centers the Outcome’s resolution on the Success/Failure of the action. As 
a result, the only thing that’s changed at the end is that the character is now down 6

Health. Otherwise, the story hasn’t really changed: Eris still needs to get past the fence to 
move things forward. This isn’t a null result, but it’s close to one as the resolution is purely 
mechanical: A number on her Character Sheet went down.  
 
In the second, the story has progressed. Complications, in the form of the guards, have 
arisen, and Eris now has to navigate those. Will she be captured? Shot? Does this open up 
other avenues for getting to the other side of the fence?    
 
Either is a legitimate choice for the Guide to take, but in the second, the action hasn’t even 
completed. It’s not about whether Eris gets over the fence or not; it’s about consequences 
stemming from the choice to climb the fence at all. The game is about telling an engaging 
story that twists and turns, in which one choice most often leads to another, quite different 
one.  
 
It’s not a war of attrition between the Guide and Players where the Guide defaults to any 
opportunity to make a Player Character’s Health go down.  
 
Quite frankly because that’s boring unless the Player Character is known to be bumbling 
and tumbles and falls are intended for chuckles at their expense.  
 
Move the Agenda Forward 
After giving the Players time to explore all they can up to the point at which they joined 
the Mystery (the Inciting Event), move things forward. The next step along the Agenda’s 
path, whatever it is, comes to fruition. Maybe right in front of their faces; maybe not.  
 
Nonetheless, the world of the Player Characters has changed in some way.  
 
If the Agenda has been completed, progress the pain it has caused in its wake. People 
suffering from spells fall deeper into disarray and ruin. Someone innocent may do 
something stupid out of desperation, trying to find any solution for the cloud hanging over 
their head. The point isn’t to wallow in misery, but rather, to illustrate that the world does 
not wait for the Player Characters to make it turn.  
 
That they have to do something; that things are starting to unravel and even drastic actions 
– once thought too dangerous or crazy – may prove to be a source of hope.  
 
Using Retaliation for Pacing 
Pacing is vital to any story, but it’s even more important for telling a story within a tabletop 
roleplaying game because they can drag with no sense of forward progress or excitement. 
For this reason, the Guide needs to keep an eye on the Players’ body language, responses, 
and other cues indicating whether they’re fully engaged or waning.  
 
Retaliation is a fantastic tool for controlling the pace of a game and reengaging Players 
when the Guide feels they’re running out of steam. Using Retaliation, the same Mystery 
can run for 3 hours or 3 sessions. So long as the Players are doing things, there is some 
causal reason for acts of Retaliation to occur.  
 
Retaliation is effective because it immediately raises the stakes.  
 
It is WITCH DOCTORS’ way of saying, “Hi there. I see you,” through the lens of a possible 
psychopath. Further, the Owner of the Agenda may realize who the Player Characters are 
first, providing a nice jolt of paranoia. Making them question everyone. Everything.  
 
Every flickering light is a cause for panic.  
Every odd phone call or passing stranger a potential threat.  
 
The other benefit of Retaliation beyond just screwing with the Players’ minds is that it 
provides new evidence to act upon. The Owner of the Agenda may not realize that the

lashes out more often than they should. People do this sort of thing all the time when they 
feel pressure or at risk, and it almost always draws attention.

Straining is a Conscious Act 
Characters cannot strain a roll if they are not consciously aware of what’s happening.

If a character is going to attempt something that arguably might tie into using multiple 
Skills to accomplish successfully, have them only roll once using the Outcomes of the Skill 
they have the least expertise in to determine how the overall process goes.  
This is done to save time, effectively using their weakest Skill as the constraint on whether 
things go well or not.  
Another example where a Tandem Roll might make sense is when a Player Character 
unleashes a wave of ammunition or magick that would impact multiple targets. Roll once 
using the Outcomes for the target with the worst Save and approximate the effect across all 
targets based on that one roll.

Session Notes 
WITCH DOCTORS gives an almost unparalleled amount of freedom to Players in terms of 
what their characters can do and where the story goes. This has its benefits, but it also 
creates challenges for the Guide trying to keep all the insanity under control.   
Though this will initially sound like busywork that you would prefer not to do, it is highly 
recommended that any Guide running WITCH DOCTORS keep detailed notes from each 
session. The payoff to doing this is that the world and characters that you’re generating as 
part of the story seem more consistent, with greater connective tissue and deeper meaning; 
but, also, it actually saves you time trying to prep for future sessions. 
I spend 30 minutes or so writing out Session Notes immediately following the session or the day after. 
These are typed in the same layout as this book, with a large, empty margin on the right. The notes 
are often bullet points of things the Player Characters did, heard, or learned in the session. Once I feel 
the key points and details have been captured, from Guided Characters’ names to things like 
descriptions of places, I will print the notes off.  
Then, using a red pen, I go through and reflect on how what happened ties back to the Agenda or has 
larger meaning within the story by writing a sentence or so of ‘Next Steps’. Some of these may be 
Retaliation related; others, just character beats to pay off later on. When the next session is coming 
up, I review the prior session’s notes and the Next Steps written in the right margin. There are times 
these Next Steps won’t happen. They simply don’t organically fit into the story, even if I thought they 
would.  
In all, this process takes about an hour at most and allows the Players to go as far afield or crazy as 
they like within the game.

The Lore
This section is devoted to worldbuilding that is purely optional for Guides to use, where and when 
they need some assistance in touching on the world’s history without having to build it from whole 
cloth. Use at your leisure.  
The world of WITCH DOCTORS is deceptively like our own. If one were to look through a 
history book or spend weeks going down the rabbit hole of Wikipedia in each, they might 
find one – perhaps two – small differences between our world and the world in which the 
Player Characters live.  
Though awesome powers exist within the world of WITCH DOCTORS, the world is not 
defined by them but, rather, the unyielding march of sameness. Of  weighted averages. 
Edges are worn down; visible points of difference swallowed up.  
Where once collective thrust of human conquest employed swords, spears, and arrows to 
destroy all evidence of alien forces, today it wields appropriation, assimilation, and noise. 
God, the noise. The scattershot, frantic death wail of silence.  
How else but by being a Witch Doctor would one ever know? 
In the Beginning…
I. 
Perhaps once, long ago, we were all gods. But those days are gone, and one suspects we 
have grown dumb rather than wise as time marches forward. Lost, outside our own 
thoughts, our own unique senses.  
For those who know what Witch Doctors are, there is s suspicion that all humans were 
once like them. Able to see the twirling, spiraled currents of magick. We experienced the 
world in a way that today would feel euphoric. Even frightening. Shapes of ideas, of 
dangers and periphery, sliding in and out of sight and mind.  
We would have been mad by today's standards. Speaking to notions; screaming at billowing 
clouds of dreamscape. Back then, there was only change. Unfocused and raw; tempestuous 
and all-too-sudden. 
When people use the word 'primal', they do so with some mixture of fear and fascination, 
but we suspect fear and paranoia ruled the early days of our kind. No wonder we built gods 
and spirits and rulers to protect us.  
II. 
As the population grew, as tribes formed, the meek lost their minds. They lost the spark 
that made them powerful; that made magick a natural, innately human extension of 
themselves. Instead, it enfeebled their senses and dulled the spectrum of experience where 
once they knew the power and fearsome aspect of change.   
Their voices drowned out by a single man. A woman. An idea. 
The strong and assertive took the fore. Leaders. Rulers. Tyrants. 
Speaking no when acting on yes. Privileged. Reserving the right to break rules because they 
are the ones who fashion them. The shapers and makers of reality and perspective.  
Transforming themselves into gods, where possible; into children of gods, where not. 
But most people are not exceptional. If a system is built in which only the best may 
succeed, there will be many losers. And there were many, many.

III. 
By the time cities were built, there was a clear separation between Witch Doctors and the 
mundane arm of humanity. Clear to Witch Doctors at any rate. But even for them, some 
of the clarity of change had dulled. Magick had become precious and fleeting. The tipping 
point into the mundane had been crossed and the decline of magick in turn.   
The incessant noise echoed in every street. A chaotic voice bigger and bolder than any one 
person's, save few. The primacy of humankind supplanted by something else, more.  
Though the Grimoire’s influence had always existed, cities changed everything. Its vigor 
encroached upon all aspects of human life: Everything beset by expectation. Rules codified 
into laws.  
Enforced. 
Punishing. 
Ruthless. 
The Grimoire created culture. Literature. Religion. Art. Science. Economics. Not their 
modern equivalents, of course, but with enough texture and fidelity to reinforce behaviors 
and ideas. To enslave the vast masses to the stake and cudgel of their time.  
IV.  
On September 19th, 1350, in Constantinople, an assembly was held between eight of the 
eleven Key Families. It was the first record of such a large number of Witch Doctor families 
intentionally meeting and formulating terms and means for collaboration. Though the 
purpose of the resulting alliance would change in time, this first meeting was to fashion a 
response to the Black Plague cascading across Eurasia, millions dead in its wake.  
After several weeks of discussion, the Demiurge was officially established… and with it, our 
first written records of the Witch Doctors population and its practices. Records from the 
meeting, though edited to remove the names of the Key Families, also include the first 
known reference to the magick practice of Panentheism or 'The Great Tradition'.  
Remarkably, the families from Europe and Asia used similar practices in their application 
of Panentheistic spellcasting despite having never met. This coincidence led people at the 
time to believe that Panentheism must be, in fact, the one true interpretation of the

Setting Options 
Across the wide world, there exist a large number of options and possible locales to explore 
in WITCH DOCTORS. Some notes about locales follow, but never hesitate to create your 
own if the Players want to go there.  
 
Playing with History 
 
Witch Doctors have existed since the dawn of humanity, so the entirety of recorded history 
is at a group’s disposal. Any historical event or time period can be plumbed for inspiration 
or flavor, from the founding of Rome or conclusion of the Persian Wars to the fall of the 
Shogunate or the coup that led to the formation of Korea.  
 
There are only a couple of things to address depending on the time period being visited: 
 
Spellcasting 
Canonically, Token has only existed in the world of WITCH DOCTORS since the early 19th 
Century. As such, any game set in a time that predates its creation would be without a 
built-in spellcasting system. As WITCH DOCTORS was always intended first and foremost 
to be set in the modern era, this is an expected challenge when attempting to go beyond 
that intended setting.  
 
There are two straight-forward options: 
 
 Create your own spell system, leaning heavily in the direction of systems you 
might have experienced in other games. Once upon a time, there might have 
been spellbooks, material components, and the like. Spells specific in their 
effects and difficulties, with different schools owning groups of spells at a time. 
Each school – similar to Token Magick – might have its own unique Skill.  
 
The issue with this option is that it would require more robust prep work on the 
part of the Guide to manage, regardless of whether they use something more 
free-form as in Token or more Vancian and archived like in many other games. 
 
 Toss canon and do what you want with Token’s chronology. Perhaps in your 
world’s history, Token came earlier. Even much earlier. Hell, its creation need 
not even be mentioned or addressed – it simply is present and, perhaps, always 
was. Indistinguishable from the discovery of the Grimoire itself.  
 
The benefit of taking this route is that nothing mechanically changes with the 
casting of spells, allowing the Guide and Players to operate as normal despite the 
drastic change in period and setting.  
 
Weapons & Armor 
Though some primitive weaponry is included in THE RULEBOOK, those samples are a far 
cry from showcasing the breadth of arms and armament that has existed through history. 
That said, what’s proffered may be enough to help the Guide fill in the blanks where they 
deem gaps to exist. Further, there is little information provided about armors dating back 
even to the mid-1900s, never mind beyond. But, again, what has been included may be 
enough to make some assumptions around the capabilities of such gear.       
 
Where possible, the Guide should make their life easy, grab something with stats available 
in THE RULEBOOK and simply oscillate numbers up or down.

Considerations for Your Own Astral Realm  
If you decide to build your own Astral Realm, there are several suggestions we would make 
to support your design.  
 
1| Don’t make it a ‘premium’ experience. In essence, the Astral Realm should not 
be an all-win proposition, even when a character can operate within it effectively. 
Whatever form or function it takes, it’s best when it offers a different experience 
without having the more Material experience be clearly lesser than it. The reason 
it’s harder to cast spells out of the Astral Form is due to how alien it feels relative 
to the Material world, not because it’s somehow elevated in power or potential.  
 
2| Time-scale it similarly to the Material realm. That is, don’t have events on the 
Astral Realm act on a time-scale notably different than the Material Realm. A 
minute in the Astral Realm should be a minute elsewhere, even if what can be 
done in that minute feels or operates differently. By lining up the timing across 
different facets of reality, you avoid logical disconnects that force you to operate 
Scenes differently than you would prefer to and protect each Player’s right to get 
to play.  
 
3| If Players don’t want to create a new, distinct Astral version of their Token’s 
effect, ask them to select the Subliminal or Material to model its use after. Once 
they’ve chosen, it will always operate in that fashion. This tends to save time and 
simplifies things for those Players who ‘want to keep the story moving’.

Beyond The Inciting Event, the Agenda, and Key Guided Characters information that follows, 
there is minimal strict guidance for how to run a specific Mystery. This is for a few reasons: 
1| You are not the only author. Nor am I. The Players’ choices should evolve and 
inform the story. The Agenda is there as a foundation. A starting point. But it is 
not the be-all and end-all. It’s not there to railroad the Player Characters into 
going or doing XYZ. Give them time to explore their characters’ lives; each has a 
spot in the Turn Order for a reason. Morris will march along his own path, with 
some deviations to clear up liabilities or retaliate along the way.   
2| Roll Outcomes will define how things progress and move. This ties into the note 
above about Players’ choices, but the dice are the 3rd author – unbiasedly dictating 
new directions and challenges. It’s your job to roll with them, rather than against 
them, and use their results to create opportunities or drama within the narrative.  
3| As Players question, poke, prod, and otherwise showcase their presence, it 
becomes increasingly likely that Morris will learn about them – and Retaliate. 
How far he takes it is up to you. He may start out with discrete threats; a voice in 
a dark alley or yank people out of their homes with his power. He likes where his 
life is going and is unlikely to let it go without blood being spilled.

Characters can do, and generally get a sense for how Scenes function within the

really cool idea, run with it.

BEGINNING THE SESSION 
 
1| Socialize. It’s good to start casually. If this is an impromptu group, or there is 
anyone new to the group, begin with some quick introductions. Especially if 
someone is new to a regular group, don’t be in a rush. Give folks a chance to talk 
and get to know one another.  
 
2| Set Expectations. As you prepare to start, it’s good to remind people of the 
Expectations at the front of THE RULEBOOK. Two good ones to stress are 
“Participate” and “Support One Another”. The latter is a good segue into 
introducing preferred Safety Tools – whether Lines and Veils, Script Change, etc., 
or some combination thereof. 
 
3| Turn Order. Have everyone read off the total of their Reflex Save without a roll 
and record the numbers. If any number repeats, have those with the matching 
number roll to break the tie. Put the names of their characters in descending 
order based on their Reflex Scores and the outcome of any tie-breaking rolls on a 
piece of paper that’s easy for you to refer back to. This order never changes.    
 
4| Character Introductions. Go through the Turn Order and have each Player 
introduce their character. It’s assumed for this Mystery that the characters know 
one another from before, but that won’t always be the case. Key things to mention 
are Concept, Profession, and anything distinctive about the character’s life or 
situation. Token descriptors – such as ‘the fire in Fireball’ – should be 
mentioned, but no Private Goals should be discussed.   
 
5| Determine the Setting. As Ghosts deals with politics and features characters 
involved with the legal system, it is likely to take place in or around a major 
metropolitan area. Make sure that the Player Characters make sense within such 
an environment before running the Mystery.  
 
6| Confirm everyone is ready. If people need to go to the restroom, do it now. If 
they’ve a pressing text to reply to, do it now. Check off the boxes and make sure 
everyone is ready.  
 
7| Now, turn the page.
