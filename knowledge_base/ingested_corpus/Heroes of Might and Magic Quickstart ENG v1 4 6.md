Chapter 1
Introduction
Step into a world where heroes shape Antagarich’s destiny through daring deeds, strategic battles, 
and personal quests for glory. In Heroes of Might and Magic 2d20, you take on the role of great 
champions—masters of sword or sorcery—leading armies and performing legendary feats to carve 
their names into history.
What is this game about?
You’ll command a diverse roster of units drawn 
from the iconic computer game. Combine strategic 
combat and personal heroics, leading them into 
battles that escalate from small skirmishes - like 
defending a village from a goblin horde - to epic 
confrontations of titans and dragons beneath 
towering fortress walls. Whether you charge into 
the fray, challenge rival heroes to duels, or wield 
powerful spells and artifacts to turn the tide, your 
choices will shape the battlefield.
But Heroes do not just rely on their troops to 
do the heavy lifting. You’ll undertake critical 
solo missions — too important, dangerous, or 
delicate to entrust to even the most faithful 
of subordinates. These quests often involve 
recovering magical artifacts, ranging from 
useful trinkets to world-altering relics.
Collaboration with other Heroes is as 
inevitable as it is crucial. Will you forge bonds 
of trust and friendship or engage in uneasy 
alliances where betrayal lurks? While a shared 
goal unites the party of Heroes, each Hero’s 
motivations, personality, and methods may 
differ, sparking lively debates and difficult 
decisions that will reach their peak intensity 
during your war councils. At times, trust may 
falter, alliances may crumble, and betrayal 
could redefine your story.
Depending on your nation and your character’s 
personal backstory, you can either devote 
yourself to fulfilling missions assigned by your 
superiors or forge your own destiny, seizing 
wealth and power from those stronger than you 
through sheer force of your might and magic.
Whether you fight to save Antagarich or hasten its 
destruction, you’ll immerse yourself in a vibrant 
world full of surprises, dramatic twists, and high 
stakes. This is a realm where grand heroism blends 
with a touch of humour, and every victory—or 
defeat—leaves a lasting impression.
How to play the game?
Role-playing games, also known as RPGs, are all 
about storytelling and shared adventures in an 
imagined world. The concept is simple:
	
¾ All but one of the people playing take on 
the roles of characters who will be their 
avatars in the game’s fictional setting—in 
this case, Heroes in the magical world of 
Enroth. These are our players.
	
¾ The one remaining person doesn’t control 
a character but instead describes the 
environment, narrates situations, and plays 
the roles of various creatures and beings 
that the characters encounter. This person is 
the gamemaster (GM).
	
¾ The GM describes the current situation 
during the game, and players decide what 
their characters do or say. When an action’s 
outcome is uncertain, players refer to the 
game’s mechanics—rules that usually involve 
rolling dice to determine success.


7
	
¾ To start the adventure, the GM often uses 
a pre-written scenario created by someone 
else (such as the one included in this 
Starter) or crafted by the GM. The GM then 
adapts the narrative based on the players’ 
actions and decisions.
	
¾ Even though the GM is in control of the 
enemies that the Heroes face off against, 
they themselves are not the adversary. 
GM’s role is to create dramatic challenges 
that give players a chance to shine and feel 
immersed in an exciting adventure.
	
¾ No one knows how the story will end—it 
evolves as the game unfolds. The goal isn’t 
to win but to have fun and create the most 
engaging and memorable story possible 
collaboratively.
Heroes of Might and Magic 2d20 is a high-
fantasy game, meaning it takes place in a 
magical world filled with extraordinary 
creatures and spellbinding wonders. Both 
the events and challenges the Heroes 
face are epic in scale, offering grand 
adventures and unforgettable tales. That 
being said, let’s be honest - this world 
comes with a knowing wink and a smirk—
especially when more than one scene can 
be kicked off with a gnoll, an ogress, and a 
necromancer stepping into a tavern.
How does the 2d20 system 
work?
As the name suggests, the 2d20 system is based on 
rolling two twenty-sided dice. When a character 
attempts an action with an uncertain outcome, 
the GM sets the difficulty level, and the player 
rolls the dice. The lower the roll results, the 
better. Each roll that matches or is below the sum 
of a Trait and a Skill being tested in this Skill 
check counts as a success. The action succeeds 
if the number of successes meets or exceeds the 
difficulty level. If not, the action fails.
You’ll come across some terms you might 
not yet be familiar with. Don’t worry, 
though—you’ll find their definitions along 
with examples later in this Starter and all 
the details in the core rulebook.


8
Conversely, a high result on a die roll can 
introduce complications—negative consequences 
that make things harder for the characters. 
Regardless of the consequences, if achieved, the 
Skill check is considered a success.
During gameplay, players earn Morale Points, 
which they can use to improve their situation in 
various ways, such as adding extra dice to their 
rolls. The better they perform, the more Morale 
Points the team gains for future use. Meanwhile, 
the GM collects Threat Points, allowing them to 
introduce additional obstacles for the Heroes. 
Players can even increase the threat pool 
themselves, risking future perils in exchange for 
an immediate bonus to their Skill check.
The battle is the most mechanically complex 
aspect of the game. Each Hero commands several 
units, which vary in size, combat effectiveness, 
and unique abilities. The GM assigns specific 
strategic objectives to the party, which they 
must complete within a set number of rounds.
What else is needed to get 
started?
In addition to the Starter, you’ll need a group 
of people poised and ready to dive in. While the 
game can be played with just a set of two —a 
GM and one player—it’s typically more fun with 
a group of three to five.
You’ll also need a few essentials:
	
¾ A set of d20 and d6 dice
	
¾ Printed character and army cards (found at 
the end of the Starter)
	
¾ Some tokens for tracking points
	
¾ Something to write with
	
¾ A quiet space to play
Games usually last two to four hours, but you 
can play longer or pause earlier and pick up 
where you left off next time. To enhance the 
atmosphere, consider playing some fitting music 
in the background!
What even is this 
Starter??
The Starter is just a taste of what the 
full game has to offer. Inside, you’ll find 
descriptions of sample nations, character 
classes, and units, along with a selection 
of ready-to-play Heroes, a straightforward 
scenario, and the rules you’ll need to play 
through it, including your first battle. 
We encourage you to continue your 
adventures with the core rulebook if you 
enjoy it!
The rules presented in the starter may be 
slightly modified in the full version of the 
game.
Safety tools
Ensuring a safe and comfortable gaming 
environment can be achieved through the use of 
safety tools.
Lines and Veils – A pre-game discussion to set 
boundaries on which topics should never be 
included in the gameplay (lines) and which may 
appear only „off-screen,” mentioned briefly but 
not explored in detail (veils).
X-Card – A card marked with an X. A player 
can touch it if any part of the narrative makes 
them uncomfortable (e.g., bringing back 
distressing memories). This pauses the game, 
giving the group an opportunity to adjust, 
rewind, or omit the problematic content as 
needed.

Chapter 2
The World of Enroth
Welcome to Enroth, one of the worlds of Heroes of Might and Magic. Little is known about 
its mysterious prehistory. Some say beings from the stars created it, while others believe the 
Elemental Lords brought it into existence. The truth has been lost to the sands of time, though 
fragments can still be found amidst ancient ruins and in the darkness of the deepest dungeons.
Eons ago, large civilizations composed of humans, elves, dwarves, goblins, and other intelligent 
beings settled the three continents of this world, divided by the Boundless Ocean: Enroth, 
Jadame, and Antagarich. Since then, countless wars have been fought, civilizations have risen 
and fallen, kingdoms have been born and destroyed, and sages and wizards have delved into 
the mysteries of elemental magic. At last, the Ironfist dynasty of Enroth forged an alliance with 
the Gryphonhearts of Erathia through marriage, offering hope for long-desired stability and a 
measure of peace across the two continents of Enroth and Antagarich.
However, a few years ago, seemingly coinciding with a celestial event scholars named the Night 
of the Shooting Stars, a new breed of demons called Kreegans descended upon Enroth. That event 
would announce a time of great wars and desperate struggles for survival that would eventually 
alter the balance of the world. The coming years will prove to be a trial for all who call this 
realm home.
We stand on the brink of events that will go down in history as the Restoration of Erathia. At 
King Ironfist’s court, agents of the Kreegans have infiltrated and are undermining the dynasty’s 
authority among the people. Meanwhile, on the continent of Antagarich, the defeated and exiled 
necromancers of Deyja conspire against King Nicolas Gryphonheart. No one realized that these 
were the first signs of a global catastrophe.
Antagarich
For the core rulebook, we have focused on 
Antagarich, the main stage for the events of 
Heroes of Might and Magic III. This continent is 
divided among eight kingdoms:
	
¾ At its heart lies Erathia, the kingdom 
of beautiful castles, age-old aristocratic 
families, honourable knights, and 
wise clerics who serve King Nicolas 
Gryphonheart. Its capital, Steadwick, is 
considered the cradle of a majorly human 
civilization in Antagarich. Until recently, 
Erathia was at war with the necromancers 
of Deyja but successfully repelled them with 
the aid of unexpected allies.
	
¾ To the north lies AvLee, Erathia’s ally - 
although the recent Timber Wars had them 
at each other’s throat - and a land almost 
entirely covered by the Tularean Forest. It is 
home to a variety of elves, dwarves, humans, 
centaurs, and the mighty Golden Dragons, 
ruled by the elven king Eldrich Parson and 
a conclave of druids. The inhabitants use 
magic to shape the forest in harmony with 
nature and welcome anyone who treats the 
natural world with respect.
	
¾ To the south is Bracada, a nation of 
stateless scholars and wizards and another 
traditional ally of Erathia. It is divided into 
two main regions: an Eastern desert named 
Bracaduun, after the ancient empire that

¾ Between AvLee and Erathia lies Deyja, a 
barren, mountainous wasteland home to the

From there, its king, Finneas Vilmar - who

lush fields, and a political system based on

of Nighon. This kingdom is home to a 
civilization split into two strata. A vast 
majority of surface dwellers and the secretive 
underground society controlling them. The 
rituals of the underground lodges summon 
the dreams of ancient creatures like harpies, 
medusae, or manticores. Minotaurs and other 
underground denizens collaborate with the 
society to increase its control over other 
nations in the mainland. Its inhabitants, 
skilled in espionage, collect knowledge and 
secrets to expand their influence and use 
them as weapons in their pursuit of power.
	
¾ To the west of Erathia lies Krewlod, a 
vast land of steppes and deserts. Once the 
possession of the ruthless Bracaduun empire 
that conquered most of Antagarich, it is now 
inhabited by nomadic tribes of barbarians, 
ogres, and goblins. These heterogeneous 
groups usually act independently but become 
united under a king every time one war chief 
elevates amongst the tribes. Recently, the so-
called “Duke Winston Boragus,” a tyrannical 
ogre lord, has subjugated the many tribes. 
Krewlod’s spirituality, also shared between 
the tribes, holds the ancestors in great esteem 
and believes that part of their souls can be 
preserved through magical trinkets. For a few 
decades, this knowledge has been under the 
scrutiny of mages and necromancers alike.
	
¾ Finally, in the inhospitable swamps of 
Western Antagarich lies Tatalia, a land that 
was once the majestic capital of the only 
empire to rival that of Bracaduun but has 
since endured subjugation by both Erathia 
and Krewlod and shrunk to its former core 
of the mud lands. It is home to lizardmen 
and gnolls, ruled by King Tralossk, who still 
believes in his people’s lost glory. The swamps 
are infamous for their deadly flora and fauna, 
including wyverns, hydras, and carnivorous 
plants.
Who Are the Heroes?
The history of Antagarich is shaped by 
extraordinary individuals who, by choice, birth, 
or fate, achieve epic deeds. These are the Heroes 
of this world—your player characters.
Some, like Sorsha, achieve glory through martial 
prowess, and they are known as Heroes of 
Might. Others, like Jeddite, dedicate themselves 
to mastering the power of the elements, and 
they are called Heroes of Magic.
The Lineages of 
Intelligent Beings
Among the many colourful lineages of 
Antagarich, this Starter features those most 
typical for Erathia and Krewlod.

Humans
Humans can be found almost everywhere 
in Antagarich. In Erathia, they serve as the 
backbone of the army and are known for their 
loyalty and dedication to their queen. Their 
strengths lie in numbers, discipline, and access 
to proven tactics and technologies. However, 
humans are not limited to Erathia. They also 
defend AvLee’s forests, swear fealty to demons 
in Eeofol, study magic and planar mechanics in 
Bracada, engage in secret conspiracies in Nighon, 
or fight as mercenaries from Krewlod. While 
they lack exceptional physical traits, humans 
excel in adaptability and understanding their 
surroundings—a quality some call their “sixth 
sense” for deciphering the rules of the world.
Goblins and Hobgoblins
The most populous inhabitants of Krewlod, 
goblins form the basic infantry of its nomadic 
armies. They hail from villages consisting of large, 
multigenerational families crowded into simple 
huts. Goblins are small, green-skinned, with pointed 
ears and sharp teeth. Goblins live in multi-lineage 
tribal communities. Village councils, led by elders 
and shamanic spell casters, govern their lives, 
while warriors lead armies and prove themselves in 
direct combat. Goblins and hobgoblins wear armor 
adorned with bones of great beasts, paint themselves 
with war colors, and often ride into battle on the 
backs of massive wolves. They differ from other 
Krewlod plains folk in their strong ancestral worship 
and unwavering devotion to their matriarchs.
Ogres
The nomadic ogres are another one of the many 
lineages that compose the tribes of Krewlod. 
Warchiefs and battle mages often come from 
this lineage. Their massive bodies make them 
formidable warriors, allowing them to endure the 
power needed for spell casting using their physical 
mass rather than spiritual energy to control magic. 
Towering over two meters tall, green-skinned, and 
powerfully built, ogres typically wear loose robes 
adorned with steel and bone. When marching to 
war, they don armor that only someone with their 
strength and size could bear—massive metal plates 
studded with barbed spikes and decorated with 
the gargantuan bones of defeated beasts.
Enroth is a world steeped in magic, 
built on the foundation of the four 
elements: fire, air, water, and earth, 
and a more mysterious spiritual 
element divided into light and dark. 
Regardless of class or lineage, every 
Hero can learn the principles of 
elemental magic and wield spells. 
Spiritual magic is more complex and is 
not described at this stage.
Magical artifacts are commonplace in 
Enroth. They are forged by melting 
magical and common materials into an 
intricate matter weaving. Some even 
say they can contain a piece of the soul 
of their bearer! Creating, discovering, 
and using them effectively will be the 
focus of many adventures.
The world is also home to mythical 
creatures, from sirens, nymphs, and 
unicorns to vampires, harpies, and 
minotaurs, all the way to thunderbirds, 
flying serpents, and behemoths. 
Some you can play as, others you’ll 
command, and still more will challenge 
you in battle.
Enroth has no revealed gods. 
Existing religions are more akin to 
philosophical systems exploring the 
world’s origins and universal laws. 
These beliefs are integral to the 
identity of certain cultures, such as 
Erathia’s worshippers of the Light or 
AvLee’s followers of the Cycle of Life.
Borders on Antagarich’s map shift 
constantly, as wars and conquests rage 
across the land. Alliances are formed 
and broken as those with the power—
or ambition—strive to shape the 
world’s fate, amass power, or at least 
secure their wealth. Your Heroes are 
among them.

Elves
Elves are the primary inhabitants and current 
rulers of the forest nation of AvLee. They are 
the descendants of the Snow Elves settlers 
who abandoned their homeland of Vori in 
pursuit of freedom from an authoritarian and 
traditionalist rule and the discovery of new 
lands. Elves of AvLee often make exceptional 
scouts and archers, raised with deep respect 
for nature’s cycle and the untamed wilderness 
of the forest depths. They fiercely defend their 
land, welcoming all who wish to support them 
in this endeavor and mercilessly punishing those 
who dare to raise a hand against their domain.
However, elves are not mere forest recluses, 
even though they often lean toward isolationist 
ideologies. They are capable of engaging 
in politics on par with their neighbors, 
maintaining alliances, and fielding numerous, 
disciplined military units. They live in harmony 
with Nature and pass down their history 
through oral tradition.

Heroes of Might 
and Magic
We have prepared several ready-to-play Heroes for the Starter: a Knight and a Cleric from Erathia, a 
Ranger from AvLee, and a Barbarian and a Battle Mage from Krewlod. The rules for creating Heroes 
from all the nations, lineages, and character classes known from the computer game are extensive, so 
you will find them in the core rulebook.
Traits
Each Hero is defined by eight Traits, 
representing their general abilities tested during 
the game. Trait values range from 2 to 12, with 6 
being the average and 12 considered legendary.
Daring
Represents aptitude with which your Hero 
handles rushing into the fray and acts directly, 
disregarding own safety or doubt.
You will roll for Daring when...
	
¾ ... acting with directness or aggression.
	
¾ ... pressing forward with decisive action 
despite the risk.
	
¾ ... leading by example.
Cunning
Represents the acumen with which your Hero 
manages situations requiring subterfuge and 
stealth or where a straightforward approach is 
not applicable.
You will roll for Cunning when...
	
¾ ... setting up a ruse or ambush.
	
¾ ... deceiving or distracting others.
	
¾ ... concealing your true intentions.
Tenacity
Represents the steadfastness with which your 
Hero endures consequences and adversity, 
describing general resilience and determination.
You will roll for Tenacity when...
	
¾ ... demonstrating relentless determination 
in the face of danger.
	
¾ ... persevering through challenging physical 
activities.
	
¾ ... withstanding intense physical or mental 
hardship.
Adaptability
Represents pliability with which your Hero 
is able to adapt to changing circumstances, 
especially when attempting to avoid danger.
You will roll for Adaptability when...
	
¾ ... dodging or evading.
	
¾ ... finding a way out of trouble.
	
¾ ... seeking a compromise.
Zest
Represents the gravity with which your Hero can affect 
reality through the emotions, what advantage they may 
manifest, and how stalwartly they may be endured.
You will roll for Zest when...
	
¾ ... inspiring or uplifting others.
	
¾ ... pushing past fear and uncertainty.
	
¾ ... intimidating or provoking someone.


1414
Focus
Represents the relentlessness with which your 
Hero is able to operate under pressure and 
handle precision tasks requiring a sure hand.
You will roll for Focus when...
	
¾ ... performing a precise or delicate action.
	
¾ ... carrying out a task that demands precise 
timing.
	
¾ ... providing detailed and exact instructions.
Mind
Represents the acuteness with which your Hero 
is able to learn and utilize collected knowledge 
in practice.
You will roll for Mind when...
	
¾ ... acquiring, internalizing, and retaining 
new information.
	
¾ ... analysing complex situations.
	
¾ ... applying your extensive knowledge and 
expertise.
Insight
Represents the fluidity with which your Hero 
is able to make sense of the situation at hand 
without any time for consideration, read the 
emotions of others, and make adequate first-
glance assessments. 
You will roll for Insight when Heros...
	
¾ ... trying to comprehend a sudden change in 
your circumstances.
	
¾ ... relying on a hunch in an unfamiliar field.
	
¾ ... resisting illusions or attempts to confuse 
your senses.

Might or Magic: Nation 
and Character Classes
A Hero’s chosen nation determines their 
available lineages and character classes. Heroes 
can hail from any of the nations featured in 
the computer version of HoMM3—that is, 
from any of the states of Antagarich described 
previously in Chapter Two. However, since this 
is a role-playing game, their nation does not 
define their personality. A Hero can be whoever 
you envision—brave, cunning, honorable, or 
ruthless—regardless of where they come from.
In the Starter, the pre-made characters belong 
to one of four lineages: humans, goblins, ogres, 
or elves (see page 11 i 12). They are divided 
into five character classes—three for Heroes of

Complications
A roll resulting in a natural 20 triggers a 
complication, representing a shift for the worse.
Complications impact the narrative without 
altering the outcome of the roll itself. Instead, 
they introduce a situation that forces players 
to take additional actions or rethink their 
approach to ongoing tasks:
	
¾ If the roll is successful, we say, „Yes, but...” 
For example, „You manage to break down the 
door, but the noise alerts enemy soldiers.”
	
¾ If the roll fails, we say „No, and...” For 
example, „You fail to find a safe path 
through the swamp, and then you get lost in 
the forest.”
It’s crucial, especially with „Yes, but...” 
outcomes, that the complication does not 
negate the Hero’s success or undo their efforts. 
Instead, it should add new layers to the 
situation, encouraging creative problem-solving 
without invalidating their achievement.

Morale, Luck, and Threat
Three types of points influence the mechanics 
and unfolding story of the game. Players 
have access to Morale and Luck, while the 
gamemaster manages Threat.
Morale
When the number of successes in a Skill check 
exceeds the Difficulty Level (DL), each extra success 
grants one Morale point. You can choose to spend 
these points immediately to improve your current 
result (a list of possible uses is provided below). If 
you don’t spend the Morale Points right away, they 
go into a shared pool that all players can use.
The shared pool can hold a maximum of 6 
Morale Points. This means that if you gain 
Morale, but the pool is already full, any excess 
points are lost unless you use them immediately 
on your turn.
Failing a Skill check DOES NOT generate 
Morale Points.
Using tokens is the best way to track the 
current number of Morale Points.
You can also track Morale Points using 
a six-sided die. Simply set the die so 
that the top-facing number matches 
the current Morale total.
The Fleeting Nature of Morale
High Morale is not permanent. At the end of 
an extended scene—and certainly at the end 
of each phase of the game (see page 30-33)— a 
single Morale point is removed from the shared 
pool. This encourages players to take action 
rather than waste time, keeping the story 
moving at a dynamic pace and ensuring that 
dramatic events unfold one after another.

Using Morale
While performing an action, a player can spend 
any number of Morale Points, using both those 
just generated from a roll and those available in 
the shared pool.
	
¾ Before rolling, spend 1 Morale point to add an 
extra d20 to the roll. The first additional die 
costs 1 Morale, the second costs 2 Morale, and 
the third costs 3 Morale.
	
¾ Spend 2 Morale Points to create a new 
permanent resource and record it on your 
character sheet.
	
¾ Spend 1 Morale point to add Quality to a 
resource. 
	
¾ Spend 1 Morale point to increase the damage 
dealt by 1 point.
	
¾ When the GM is about to roll for an NPC, 
spend 2 Morale Points to increase the difficulty 
level of that roll by 1.

Qualities on page 25 i 26.
Luck
No matter how skilled or heroic a Hero may be, 
sometimes sheer luck can tip the scales in their 
favor. In the game, this is represented by Luck 
Points, a rare and valuable resource that enables 
truly epic effects:
	
¾ Before making a Skill check, spend a Luck point 
to add a d20 and immediately set it to a result of 
one—guaranteeing two successes! 
	
¾ After rolling, spend 1 Luck point to re-roll any 
selected dice.
	
¾ After completing an action, spend 1 Luck point 
to immediately take an extra action in the same 
turn. This can only be done once per round.
A Hero starts the game with 2 Luck Points. You 
can spend only one Luck point per roll.
Threat
Threat Points represent the ever-increasing 
dangers surrounding the Heroes and the 
challenges and obstacles standing in their way.
At the start of the game, the gamemaster 
(GM) begins with 2 Threat Points per player. 
Throughout the game, additional Threat Points 
are generated through:
	
¾ GM Rolls – When the GM rolls for NPCs, 
any extra successes beyond the Difficulty 
Level (DL) automatically convert into 
Threat Points.
	
¾ Buying Dice – Instead of spending a Morale 
Point to add an extra d20 to a roll, a player 
can choose to give the GM 1 Threat Point 
instead. However, this can only be done 
once per roll.
	
¾ Complications—If the GM chooses not to 
introduce a complication immediately, they 
may add 2 Threat Points to the pool instead.
Threat from Army Size
The world does not stand idle as Heroes 
grow in power. Rumors and reports of troop 
movements spread quickly, and enemies prepare 
accordingly. The larger the army under a 
Hero’s command, the more Threat Points the 
gamemaster gains during battle.
The rules for generating Threat Points based 
on the number of units in a Hero’s army are 
detailed in Chapter 6: Battles.

The gamemaster (GM) uses Threat Points

¾ Adding Dice to NPC Rolls
When rolling for an NPC, the GM can 
spend 1 Threat Point to add an extra d20 to 
the roll. The first die costs 1 Threat Point, 
the second 2 Threat Points, and the third 3 
Threat Points.
	
¾ Complicating an Ongoing Scene
The GM can spend 2 Threat Points 
introducing additional challenges that 
logically fit the narrative. This could be a 
story complication or an increase of +1 to

¾ Increasing Damage

Rounds
During a game, there will be moments when 
events unfold rapidly—such as combat or a 
pursuit—and multiple characters wish to act 
simultaneously. In such situations, it helps 
to divide the scene into rounds, which are 
arbitrary units of time. Within a round, every 
character (Heroes and NPCs alike) has the 
opportunity to act, taking their turn.
Actions
An action is a declared attempt to do something 
that impacts other characters in the scene. Each 
character should have the chance to act during 
a round, which almost always involves a Skill 
check (as explained in Chapter 2).
If a character’s action forces another character 
to react or creates a conflict, the mechanics 
of conflict resolution (detailed later in this 
chapter) are involved.
Initiative and Turn Order
Once the initial situation is set and the players are 
ready to take action, it’s important to determine 
the turn order, also known as initiative. By default, 
initiative always belongs to the Heroes, and the 
players decide among themselves who will act first. 
The key rule is that each player must take one turn 
per round.
For 1 Threat Point, the gamemaster (GM) can have 
an NPC act out of turn. This can happen:
	
¾ Before the round begins, allow an NPC to 
act first.
	
¾ Between player turns, after one Hero has 
finished their action but before the next 
begins.
In such cases, the NPC acts normally, following 
standard rules. After their turn ends, initiative 
returns to the players. The GM can spend 
additional Threat Points to allow multiple NPCs to 
seize the initiative.
Space and Distances
During the game, space is divided into zones, 
which serve as a general way to determine where 
the Heroes, their enemies, and key locations 
are. Zones do not have a fixed size or shape. 
At the start of any scene where positioning is 
important—particularly in action sequences or 
battles—the gamemaster (GM) should sketch or 
describe the division of space. Keeping things 
simple is best—the fewer zones, the better. 
Additional zones can always be added later if 
needed.

¾ Adjacent zone → Medium range: You must

Resources
Resources include items owned by Heroes, 
creatures, and units under their command, as 
well as potential allies and contacts.
Heroes are important and influential figures 
who don’t need to worry about trivial expenses 
like buying food or repairing worn-out 
boots. As a result, not every item they use is 
considered a resource—only those that enable or 
significantly aid important story-driven actions. 
For example, a regular cloak isn’t a resource, but 
an elven scout’s camouflage cloak is perfect for 
blending into the forest.
Acquiring Resources
A Hero’s access to resources is based on the 
logic of the world:
	
¾ An Erathian noble likely has fine clothing 
and a pouch full of gold.
	
¾ A cavalry commander can easily demand a 
saddled horse.
	
¾ A leader of crossbowmen has ample bolts 
for their unit.
	
¾ A necromancer commanding an undead 
horde has plenty of rusted cleavers at their 
disposal.
However, obtaining resources becomes more 
complicated during action scenes (e.g., combat, 
battle, or a chase). In such cases, acquiring a 
resource requires both a narrative justification 
and either spending 2 Morale Points or passing 
a Skill check set by the GM.
Resources and Game 
Phases
Later in the Starter, you’ll learn about the 
different phases of the game. For now, you just 
need to know that during the Adventure Phase, 
when Heroes embark on quests and missions 
without their armies, acquiring resources is 
always more difficult.
	
¾ A Hero only has the resources listed on 
their character sheet.
	
¾ If they need something extra, they must 
spend 2 Morale Points or pass a Skill check 
in a narratively justified situation.
A: During a fight, your Hero’s sword 
breaks. You need a new weapon, but your 
dwarves are currently fending off an 
attack from stone gargoyles—you can’t 
simply walk up and order one of them to 
hand over their axe.
	
¾ Spending 2 Morale Points represents 
a dwarf noticing your situation and 
tossing you a spare weapon.
B: Moments later, your new axe breaks. 
You have no Morale Points left but still 
need a weapon.
	
¾ The GM tells you there’s a sword 
near one of the fallen corpses, but 
you’ll have to rush over and grab it, 
risking exposure to the gargoyles.
	
¾ The GM calls for a Cunning + 
Combat check to see if you can grab 
it unnoticed.
The gamemaster (GM) can also create 
resources using Threat Points—for example, 
providing the cyclops with a perfectly 
balanced boulder to hurl at a Hero.
There are only resources that cannot be created with 
Morale Points: magical artefacts.
Qualities
A Quality is a special property of a resource 
that makes it more effective. Using a Quality 
may make a Skill check easier by reducing its 
Difficulty Level (DL), provided that a given 
Quality can be narratively connected with the 
task it is meant to provide a bonus for. 
For example, a sword as a resource might have 
different Qualities, such as:
	
¾ Ancestral - a symbol of a noble lineage 
	
¾ Razor-sharp - enhanced cutting ability
	
¾ Unbreakable - incredibly durable 
Each Quality is useful in a different situation, 
but it is important to remember that only one 
Quality of the resource being used can affect 
the difficulty level of a Skill check.

Units as Resources
A special type of resource is the units 
commanded by Heroes—though they should 
never be treated as just another resource!
Just as a player character is more than just a 
collection of stats on a character sheet, units 
should also develop over the course of the game, 
becoming a living (yes, revenants and skeletons, 
you too) and integral part of the imagined 
world. Assigning Qualities is one way to make a 
Hero’s units more unique and personal.
The longer a Hero commands a unit, the better 
they understand it, and their soldiers cease to 
be just an anonymous crowd. At any point, a 
player can spend a Morale Point to assign a 
new Quality to a unit. However, this should be 
narratively justified by events in the game.
Qualities assigned to units don’t have to be just 
about individual soldiers. Players can add:
	
¾ Notable achievements that earned a 
moniker to a unit
	
¾ Cultural aspects such as unit traditions, 
mascots, or banners.
	
¾ Story-driven elements tied to the unit’s 
identity and history.
Example: The GM role-plays a particularly 
charismatic and experienced crossbowman 
sergeant during a scene where the 
army marches out. A player enjoys this 
interaction and spends a Morale Point to 
add him to the unit as a Quality.
	
¾ This makes the unit more effective 
in certain situations.
	
¾ The GM now knows that this 
sergeant should reappear in future 
adventures.

Combat
As with any scene where characters act against 
each other, determining the order of actions 
(Initiative) is essential. The foundation 
of combat is opposed rolls. The character 
initiating an action is called the attacker, while 
the character targeted by the action is the 
defender.
The nature of actions taken in combat is 
determined by combining the Combat Skill 
with the appropriate Trait.
If you do not have a weapon or resource that 
allows for ranged attacks:
	
¾ You can only engage in combat with 
characters or units within the same zone 
(close range).
	
¾ You may also attack an enemy in an adjacent 
zone (medium range), but the attack roll’s 
Difficulty Level (DL) increases by +1.
If you have a weapon or resource that allows for 
medium-range attacks:
	
¾ You can attack a target in the same zone 
(close range) or in an adjacent zone 
(medium range).
	
¾ You may also attempt to attack a target in 
a distant zone (long range), but the attack 
roll’s Difficulty Level (DL) increases by +1.
For reference:
Close-range weapons:
sword, throwing axe, etc.
Medium-range weapons:
bow, crossbow, etc.
Long-range weapons:

Vitality and Injuries
Vitality determines how much physical and 
mental damage a Hero can endure before 
suffering a serious injury. A Hero’s Vitality value 
depends on their Lineage and Character Class.
	
¾ Vitality fully replenishes at the end of a scene.
	
¾ If a Hero’s Vitality drops to 0 during a 
scene, they suffer an Injury.
	
¾ Vitality does not recover mid-scene (unless 
it is restored through spells or Hero Merits).
	
¾ Any additional damage after reaching 0 
Vitality immediately causes another Injury, 
even if the attack only deals 1 damage.
	
¾ A Hero can suffer up to four Injuries. If they 
are forced to take a fifth Injury, they are killed.
Healing Injuries
Injuries are serious physical or mental wounds that 
do not heal on their own. They must be actively 
treated. Some basic methods of healing include:
	
¾ Tavern Rest – At the end of this phase, each 
Hero heals 2 Injuries.
	
¾ Magic – A successful casting of a Spell: 
Restoration restores 1 Injury.
	
¾ Merit – Using the Medicine Merit heals 1 Injury.
	
¾ Skill – A successful Mysticism + Focus Skill 
check heals 1 Injury. During the Tavern 
Phase, each Hero can be healed this way 
only once.
Magic
The primary sources of magical energy in 
Enroth are the elements: fire, water, earth, 
and air. In this extraordinary world magic is 
within arm’s reach and frequently wielded by 
both Heroes of Might and Heroes of Magic. It 
is so pervasive and accessible that anyone with 
a spellbook and enough determination can 
harness elemental forces. However, true mastery 
of magic requires talent and practice.
To learn spells, a Hero must possess a spell 
book, where magical formulas and incantations 
are recorded. Each mage customizes their spell 
casting to suit their personal style, meaning 
access to another’s spell book does not grant 
automatic knowledge of their spells.
Casting Spells 
Each spell has the following attributes:
	
¾ Power Level – The overall strength of the spell.
	
¾ Range – The distance at which the spell can be cast.
	
¾ Mana Cost – The amount of Spell Points 
(SP) required to successfully cast the spell.

successful.
	
¾ Spell Power - what the spell does.
	
¾ Additional Effects – Any extra effects 
triggered by the spell.
To cast a spell, a Hero must perform a Skill check

The basic DL of a spell is equal to its level.
Spell casting follows the same Skill check 
mechanics as other actions, meaning:
	
¾ Complications may occur on high rolls.
	
¾ Excess successes generate Morale or Threat

Spell Points

for casting spells. The number of SP a Hero has 
depends on Character Class, Merits, and Traits

Spell Points, of course, replenish when you drink 
water from a well. And since wells aren’t found

automatically at the beginning of each day of in 
game time. The entirety of a pool is refreshed at the 
beginning of every new Campaign.
Magic Dice

number of six-sided Magic Dice (d6) to the roll

Chapter 5
Gameplay
In tabletop RPGs, various terms are used to describe a single session. In our system, we will simply 
refer to it as a game. A game is the smallest narrative unit, where the players’ Heroes embark on 
adventures, engage in epic battles, and deepen their connection to the world. Multiple games naturally 
flow into one another, forming a campaign that typically builds toward a spectacular grand finale.
In HoMM 2d20, each game follows a defined 
structure, divided into phases, with each phase 
consisting of scenes. A game can range from as 
little as two hours to several hours in length, 
while a campaign might last up to several games.
You may go through one or multiple phases 
during a single game session. The number of 
phases depends on how long each takes. The 
phases do not have to follow a strict sequence. 
They are not a rigid game structure but rather 
a way to organize the different situations that 
Heroes will encounter.
How Does a Scene Play Out in Practice?
	
¾ The gamemaster (GM) describes when and 
where the scene takes place.
	
¾ Players take actions, describing and 
declaring what their Heroes do.
	
¾ Actions are resolved—if needed, players roll 
dice for Skill checks—until the scene plays out.
	
¾ The scene is concluded, summarizing what 
happened.


3131
	
¾ Preparation Phase – Heroes set their 
goals and prepare for their journey. 
	
¾ Exploration Phase – Heroes venture 
into the world with their armies. 
	
¾ Adventure Phase – Heroes embark 
on a mission without their armies. 
	
¾ Tavern Phase – Heroes rest, recover, 
and gather rumours.
	
¾ Battle Phase – Armies clash on the 
battlefield. 
A Campaign always begins with the Preparation 
Phase and ends when you decide to start preparing 
for a new Campaign. Sometimes, a Campaign can 
be completed in a single game session, but more 
often, it will take multiple sessions.
Ending a Campaign does not necessarily mean that 
the entire story is concluded. Longer narratives, 
known as Campaign Arcs, consist of several 
interconnected Campaigns, forming a larger story.
A Campaign can be based on a pre-written scenario 
(such as the one at the end of the Starter) or 
improvised by the gamemaster using tools provided 
in the core rulebook.
The Party
A key element of role-playing games is the 
party, understood as a group of interconnected 
characters working toward a shared goal. 
Throughout the Campaign, Heroes will 
primarily act together.
This is why establishing relationships between 
them and defining a reason for cooperation 
is essential to character creation—just as 
important as determining Trait values or 
choosing a Lineage.
Of course, just because the Heroes work together 
doesn’t mean they have to like each other. The 
relationships within the party should reflect each 
character’s personality, beliefs, and history.
However—at least for now—they are a party, 
bound together by a common goal.
How to Play Heroes of 
Might and Magic 2d20
	
¾ Scale and Spectacle—go big! 
This rule applies to battle scenes, 
political intrigue, and character 
conflicts. Subtle moments are great 
but shine best when contrasted 
with bold, epic events.
	
¾ Goals and Conflict – Characters—
both Heroes and NPCs—must have 
clear goals that drive them forward. 
When opposing goals collide, 
conflict arises.
	
¾ High Stakes—involve every action 
should carry a risk or opportunity. 
The higher the stakes, the more 
weight and excitement the game’s 
events will carry.
	
¾ Complications— achievements feel 
more rewarding when obstacles are 
overcome. An epic scene should 
escalate as decisions are made. A 
Hero should face multiple trials, 
each carrying increasing risk.
	
¾ Driving the Story Forward— failure 
should not stall the game unless it 
results in death. The worst possible 
outcome is a failure that halts all 
progress, leaving the story at a 
standstill.
The Town
One of the elements that binds the party of 
Heroes together is the town where they are 
stationed. It serves as their base, whether 
permanent or temporary, and is the place where 
every Campaign begins and ends. For each Hero, 
the town is important for a reason—they may 
want to protect it, change it, or gain full control 
over it.
The town will change and develop along with the 
story during the campaign. The core rulebook 
provides more details.

Preparation Phase
At the beginning of the game, the gamemaster 
(GM) presents the players with at least one 
problem to solve. The Heroes gather in the 
town for a council, during which they decide 
how to approach the issue and how to prepare. 
Some actions are undertaken personally within 
the town, while others are delegated to their 
units. The success of each action depends on the 
result of a Skill check.
Heroes can, for example:
	
¾ Send scouts (Scouting + Cunning if they 
wish to remain undetected, or Scouting + 
Zest if they aim to provoke an enemy into 
action).
	
¾ Analyse maps to find the best route to their 
goal (Tactics + Focus).
	
¾ Plan the logistics of a military campaign 
(Management + Mind).
	
¾ Raise troop morale with a rousing speech 
(Command + Zest).
The Preparation Phase ends when the Heroes 
decide to:
	
¾ Lead their army out of the town, starting 
the Exploration Phase.
	
¾ Embark on a mission as a team, starting the 
Adventure Phase.
Beyond gathering information and roleplaying 
their characters’ personalities, these actions 
aim to accumulate Morale Points for use in 
later phases of the game. Players can store up to 
six Morale Points in their shared pool, but the 
GM has no limit on Threat Points. This means 
players should not spend excessive time in this 
phase.
During a Campaign, the Preparation Phase will 
also involve developing the town and recruiting 
units.
Exploration Phase
The Heroes leave the town with their units 
to execute the plan established during the 
Preparation Phase.
The goal of the Exploration Phase may include:
	
¾ Finding an enemy the party intends to 
defeat (e.g., a horde of goblins raiding the 
area).
	
¾ Reaching a specific location (e.g., a bridge 
destroyed by magic).
	
¾ Aiding an ally (e.g., the defenders of a 
besieged castle).
	
¾ Exploring new territories.
During exploration, Heroes will stumble upon 
unexpected occurrences that present both 
challenges and opportunities. These will be 
referred to as Encounters and may either be 
outlined in the scenario (in the form of a Table 
of Random Encounters) or prepared in advance 
by the gamemaster (more on how to prepare an 
Encounter can be found in the core rulebook). 
These Encounters may involve interactions with 
NPCs—both friendly and hostile—as well as 
the discovery of intriguing, useful, or dangerous 
locations.
For managing exploration, especially 
discovering new lands, the core rulebook 
provides tools for the GM—some of 
which are included in the ready-to-play 
adventure at the end of the Starter.
The Exploration Phase is interrupted when:
	
¾ A battle begins (transitioning into the 
Battle Phase).
	
¾ Heroes leave their forces to personally 
complete a mission (transitioning into the 
Adventure Phase).
	
¾ Heroes stop at a tavern (transitioning into 
the Tavern Phase).

Adventure Phase
Heroes do not always act with the support of 
their armies. Sometimes, by choice or necessity, 
they will set out alone, like a classic band of 
adventurers, to undertake an important task 
and experience an adventure. This could involve 
retrieving a powerful artifact, uncovering a 
mystery in a nearby forest, visiting an ally, or 
confronting a threat that cannot be dealt with 
through conventional means.
Why would Heroes venture 
out without their army?
Because they are this story’s central 
figures, they’re not about to let others 
have all the fun! A Hero’s motivation 
may come from—among other reasons- a 
need to keep the mission’s details secret, 
minimize losses among their troops, or 
establish their authority through personal 
achievement. Think of this phase as an 
opportunity to showcase how incredible 
your Hero is. The army will be ready and 
waiting for when it is needed again.
The Adventure Phase can take place:
	
¾ Immediately after the Preparation Phase, if 
the Heroes decide to handle the presented 
problem themselves.
	
¾ As an interruption during the Exploration 
Phase, when the Heroes encounter a 
situation best addressed without their army.
	
¾ This is an interruption during the battle 
phase when a task arises that only the heroes 
can accomplish.
The Adventure Phase ends when the Heroes 
achieve their goal—or fail. The game then 
returns to the previous phase unless the 
Adventure Phase followed the Preparation 
Phase, in which case completing it marks the 
end of the Campaign.
Tavern Phase
Where better to gather your thoughts, 
strategize, recover vitality, or uncover rumors 
about your enemies and potential quests than in 
a tavern?
Beyond providing an opportunity for 
roleplaying and developing relationships 
between the Heroes, the Tavern Phase allows 
them to:
	
¾ Regain strength – Restore full Vitality and 
heal two Injuries.
	
¾ Introduce a new Hero to the party after the 
death of a character.
	
¾ Gather rumors about current events and 
opportunities in the region.
Additionally, the Tavern Phase functions as a 
miniature Preparation Phase without returning 
to the town—allowing the Heroes to take 
actions that generate extra Morale Points for 
future use.
Battle Phase
This is the moment in the game when the 
Heroes’ armies clash with the enemy on the 
battlefield. The beginning may vary. Sometimes, 
the units will have time to set up camp near the 
future battlefield, and commanders will gather 
in a tent to determine the best strategy. Other 
times, the battle will begin suddenly, when 
the Heroes fall into a trap or launch a surprise 
attack on the enemy.
The Heroes have specific Strategic Objectives 
presented by the gamemaster (GM) during the 
battle. The exact mechanics of army combat are 
described in the next chapter.
The Battle Phase ends when:
	
¾ The enemy is defeated.
	
¾ The Heroes decide to retreat.
	
¾ The number of rounds determined at the 
start of the battle runs out.
Depending on the situation, this may mean 
returning to the Exploration Phase or ending 
the Campaign.

Waging Battles
Army
The units led by a Hero make up your army. During battle, you will use them for the purpose 
of completing Strategic Objectives (see page 36). A Hero usually starts the game with two units 
associated with their nation. Later, they can expand their army freely, depending on which units they 
have the opportunity to recruit (and whether they succeed).
Unit
A unit refers to a group of soldiers using the 
same type of weaponry (e.g., crossbowmen) or 
creatures of the same species (e.g., griffins).
Each unit is described by its:
	
¾ Size
	
¾ Endurance
	
¾ Combat Value (CV)
	
¾ Range
	
¾ Abilities
	
¾ Qualities
Unit Size
This determines how many creatures make up 
the unit. Unsurprisingly, the size categories 
are as follows: Few, Several, Pack, Lots, Horde, 
Throng, Swarm, Zounds, and Legion. 
The weakest units do not exist in the smallest 
size categories. For example, if a group of 
skeletons suffers a loss, the unit is considered 
broken since a couple of skeletons would not be 
a meaningful force on the battlefield.
Size does not determine a unit’s ultimate 
combat prowess. A single behemoth 
devours many goblins for breakfast, and 
a couple of gorgons will have no trouble 
facing off against a horde of wolf riders. 
Only by combining a unit’s size and 
Combat Value does its true potential on 
the battlefield reveal itself.

Unit Endurance
Determines how many losses a unit can 
sustain. A loss is the equivalent of damage 
in the context of a unit. However, suffering 
losses does not necessarily mean that a specific 
number of soldiers have been killed—it may also 
represent a temporary disarray, a high number 
of wounded, or a partial retreat. The unit’s final 
numbers will be determined only during the 
battle’s aftermath.
Unit Combat Value
A unit’s strength, which determines the number 
of Army Points gained at the start of a battle 
(see below) and the unit’s effectiveness in 
completing Strategic Objectives.
Unit Range
Defines the distance at which a unit can operate 
freely, meaning it can attempt to complete 
Strategic Objectives without requiring risky 
maneuvers (see page 37).
Unit Abilities
Additional actions are available to all units of 
this type (e.g., ranged attacks, berserker rage), 
along with information on how many times 
they can be activated during battle. Some unit 
abilities introduce exceptions to the general 
rules of the game.

like other resources, can reduce the Difficulty

Battle Phase Structure
A battle begins with the gamemaster describing 
the situation and presenting the Strategic 
Objectives for the Heroes’ army—essentially 
defining what must be accomplished to achieve 
victory. Strategic Objectives may be close, at 
a medium distance, or far from the Heroes’ 
forces. As the battle progresses, the gamemaster 
may introduce additional Strategic Objectives 
using Threat Points. Completing at least half of 
the objectives before the battle ends results in 
victory for the Heroes.
The duration of the battle phase is determined 
by the gamemaster at the start of the 
engagement, giving players a clear timeframe to 
accomplish their objectives. Like action scenes, 
battles are divided into rounds and turns. A 
small skirmish may last two rounds, a major 
battle three to four rounds, while a prolonged 
siege could span five or more rounds. In the 
Starter scenario, the length of the battle phase 
is predefined by the example adventure.
During battle, time and space are shaped by 
narrative needs. A single round can represent 
minutes of intense skirmish or hours of 
gruelling struggle. The story focuses on pivotal 
moments of each round—attempts to complete 
Strategic Objectives. These moments are played 
out in scenes and resolved with dice rolls. 
Numerous smaller clashes are assumed to occur 
in the background, but they are omitted—just 
as less crucial moments are skipped in novels or 
action films.
Heroes in Battle
During their turn, each Hero can perform a 
Deed of Might, which involves commanding 
their units or personally engaging in combat, as 
well as a Deed of Magic, which involves casting 
a spell. These actions can be taken in any order.
	
¾ A Deed of Might is always directed at 
completing a Strategic Objective.
	
¾ A Deed of Magic may also be used to 
enhance a unit, heal another Hero, or 
perform other beneficial effects.
Strategic Objectives
Strategic Objectives are tasks set by the 
gamemaster for Heroes and their armies during 
battle. Players must fill a Progress Track with 
a specified number of steps to complete an 
objective. This can be achieved using:
	
¾ Army Points
	
¾ Excess successes from a Power Action
	
¾ Spell effects, provided they fit the narrative 
context
If the Progress Track is not completed, the 
attempt can be resumed in the following round. 
Other Heroes may also contribute toward 
completing the objective during their turns.
Filling the Progress Track means the objective 
is completed, bringing a positive change to the 
battlefield (either narratively, mechanically, 
or both). Conversely, failing to complete the 
objective by the battle’s end has consequences 
(either narratively, mechanically, or both).
Completing at least half of the Strategic 
Objectives before the battle ends results in 
victory for the Heroes.
Imminent Objectives
Some Strategic Objectives cannot wait for a 
convenient moment. At the start of the battle, 
the gamemaster will specify if any objectives 
are Imminent, meaning they must be completed 
within a single round.
Additionally, the gamemaster may spend two 
Threat Points at any time to designate an 
additional objective as Urgent. In doing so, they 
must clearly define the consequences of failing 
to complete the objective on time.
Examples of Strategic Objectives can be 
found in the Krewlod Frontier campaign 
on page 52.

A Deed of Might refers to any action a Hero takes 
on the battlefield to achieve a Strategic Objective 
without expending Spell Points. All Skill checks 
for Deeds of Might are at least Difficult (DL 3). 
Players may use Qualities to reduce the Success 
Threshold (ST), while the gamemaster may spend 
Threat Points to increase it.
A Hero may perform a Deed of Might:
	
¾ Alone
	
¾ With the support of one or more units
	
¾ By sending units into action

¾ Describe what you want to do and make a 
Skill check using the chosen Trait.
	
¾ Regardless of the result, immediately mark 
the loss of 1 Vitality Point.
	
¾ Success? Any excess successes can be 
allocated toward completing the Strategic 
Objective.
	
¾ Failure? The Hero loses an additional 
Vitality Point.

alongside your Hero. Then, decide how

completing the Strategic Objective.

Not necessarily. Many units have Abilities

A Maneuver is a special type of Deed of Might. 
It represents a bold or cunning action on 
the battlefield that allows a Hero to reach a 
Strategic Objective outside their Range or 
find a faster (but riskier) way to complete an

Performing a Maneuver always increases the 
Range by one.
	
¾ A short-range unit can, through a Maneuver, 
attempt to complete an Objective in 
medium range.
	
¾ A medium-range unit can, through 
a Maneuver, attempt to complete an 
Objective in the long range.

Deed of Magic
A Deed of Magic refers to actions that require a 
Hero to spend Spell Points. The Difficulty Level 
(DL) of spells during battle follows the spell’s 
description unless the GM spends a Threat

Spells in battle are usually used to enhance units’

may also be used to achieve Strategic Objectives.
For example, defeating a pack of goblins 
is an Objective that can easily be achieved 
with Magic Arrow. However, using Blessing

Instead, Blessing could be cast to enhance the

GM’s role in Battle

¾ Clearly describing the situation,
	
¾ Presenting Strategic Objectives,
	
¾ Reacting to players’ actions.
During battle, the GM does not directly control

Threat Points in Battle
At the start of the Battle Phase, the GM 
receives additional Threat Points beyond those 
accumulated in previous phases:
	
¾ 2 Threat Points for every 5 Combat Value

During battle, the GM can use Threat Points in

Retreat
It may happen that the first rounds of the battle 
go so unfavorably for the Heroes that continuing 
it would only prolong an inevitable defeat. 
During their turn, a player may declare that 
their Hero orders a retreat. This does not require 
any additional Skill check; narratively, it means 
that their units withdraw from the battlefield. 
Regardless of the battle’s final outcome, a Hero 
who has retreated does not receive an additional 
reward from the battle resolution.

Battle resolution
The battle ends when the final round has been 
played, the last strategic objective has been 
completed, or all Heroes have declared a retreat. 
The final outcome is determined by the number 
of strategic objectives completed.
	
¾ Glorious Victory - This is considered 
a glorious victory if the Heroes have 
completed all strategic objectives. In 
addition to the rewards (narrative and/or 
mechanical) for completing the strategic 
objectives, each Hero may restore two 
casualties to their chosen units. 
	
¾ Victory - If the Heroes complete more than 
half of the strategic objectives but not all, 
this is considered a victory. In addition to 
the rewards and consequences (narrative 
and/or mechanical) for completed or failed 
strategic objectives, each Hero may restore 
one casualty to a chosen unit. 
	
¾ Defeat - If the Heroes fail to complete at 
least half of the strategic objectives, this 
is considered a defeat. In such a case, they 
only receive rewards for the objectives they 
managed to complete, and the gamemaster 
applies all consequences.

The Krewlod Frontier 
Starter Adventure
The Krewlod Frontier is a short introductory adventure based on the rules provided in the Starter, 
designed to introduce players to the HoMM2d20 system. If you are a player, you’d better stop reading 
now to avoid spoiling the fun! But if you are taking on the role of the gamemaster (GM), below you 
will find a scenario that will help you run your first session in this system.
Keep in mind that this is not a linear script of the story. Whether and when certain elements appear 
in the session depends on the decisions made by the players during the Preparation Phase and their 
subsequent actions. The text provides guidance on how to react to these choices, but every RPG session 
is different, so there will be moments when you must improvise.
Introduction
The town of Firscotch, where the campaign begins, 
lies in southern Avauntnell, on the border between 
Erathia and Krewlod. This inhospitable region, 
where dense forests and fertile fields gradually give 
way to rocky steppes, is officially the claim of King 
Nicolas Gryphonheart’s domain, stretching all the 
way to the border with Bracada. In reality, it is a 
disputed territory. On one hand, Erathian settlers 
are attempting to establish villages and towns 
on the frontier; on the other, tribal raiders from 
Krewlod frequently cross into the area to attack 
caravans travelling from Bracada to Erathia’s capital.
Lord Blackridge, the ruler of these lands, recently 
passed away, dividing his domain between his two 
children. The lion’s share of his holdings went to his 
eldest son. His daughter, Dame Andrea, inherited 
Firscotch along with all its troubles. To help her 
restore order, she is supported by her late father’s 
trusted advisor, the cleric Rufus Birdcherry.
Before his death, Lord Blackridge struck a deal with 
an ambitious ogre warlord named Jamarco, who 
pledged to help drive other Krewlodian warlords 
beyond Erathia’s borders. Jamarco is backed by 
his own allies: the seasoned goblin battle mage 
Gribnash and—surprisingly—an elven scout from 
AvLee, Earlene, who is bound to him by an honour 
debt, along with her unit of centaurs.
Situation Overview for 
the GM
The campaign revolves around two main 
objectives for the Heroes:
	
¾ Resuming the construction of Firscotch’s 
castle requires reclaiming the goblin-occupied 
quarry and restoring trade with Bracada.
	
¾ Locating the Shield of the Yawning Dead, a 
powerful magical artifact, before it falls into 
enemy hands.
The first objective is clear to them from the 
very beginning, but only goblin Gribnash is 
aware of the second one. The rest may only 
learn about the artifact’s existence as the 
campaign unfolds.
The Town
The construction of Firscotch’s castle has come to a 
standstill. The first problem is the lack of building 
materials. Deliveries ceased weeks ago when a horde 
of goblins took over the nearby quarry. They have 
been flocking there from the surrounding areas, 
seeking safety from Tyraxor. To resume quarrying 
operations, they must be dealt with.
The second problem is a lack of funds. Since 
trade with Bracada came to a halt and merchants 
no longer stop in Firscotch, the town has fallen

into poverty. Unpaid craftsmen and laborers have 
left in search of work elsewhere. However, if the

Shield of the Yawning Dead
A goblin warlord, Tyraxor, is currently combing 
firscotch environs. Acting on behalf of a powerful 
necromancer, Loros, the goblin chieftain is trying 
to locate a splinter of the infamous Armour of 
the Damned: The Shield of the Yawning 
Dead. It was after many years of meticulous 
studies in necromancy and the nature 
of magic itself that Loros had grown to 
a conviction that magical artifacts can 
harbour a fraction of its user’s essence 
even after their demise. Thus, he attempts 
to acquire the Shield not only for its raw

none other than Sandro himself. The Shield 
of the Yawning Dead is hidden within 
tunnels that were excavated long ago by 
Nighon troglodytes. No one in the region 
knows of their existence—let alone the 
artifact.
Initially, Tyraxor is solely focused on his 
mission and ignores Firscotch. But if he 
secures the artifact, his attention will turn 
to conquest, and he will seek to attack and 
plunder the town. It is not just the goblins 
who fear him—local settlers are also terrified

A battle against Tyraxor’s forces may occur

the rumours revolve around topics the 
Heroes are already familiar with.
In a longer campaign, rumour tables 
will introduce additional plot threads, 
potentially piquing the Heroes’ interest and 
steering the story in entirely new directions.
At the end of the Tavern Phase, one point of 
morale is removed from the morale pool.
Adventure Phase
When Heroes decide to set out without their 
armies, transition to the Adventure Phase. In 
Borderlands of Krewlod, this most likely occurs 
when they descend into the underground, 
though they might also choose to:
	
¾ Resolve the quarry situation on their own
	
¾ Search for the magical fountain
	
¾ Investigate the mysterious cottage
Navigating the 
Underground
Finding one’s way through the underground 
requires a successful Skill check in Scouting 
or Scholarship. A success allows the Heroes to 
choose their path toward the quarry or in pursuit 
of the artifact. The Nighonian Map can help 
reduce the difficulty level of the Skill check, 
should Heroes have it with them. Alternatively, 
had they the opportunity to study it, they may 
take a Mind related Skill check to gain the same 
benefit. A successful Skill Check ensures the 
Heroes reach their destination, but unexpected 
challenges still lie ahead. The GM rolls on the 
Underground Encounters Table, and if the check 
results in complications, the GM may either roll 
again or increase the difficulty of the upcoming 
challenge.

Table

The Shield of the Yawning 
Dead
The crystal encrusted in the pedestal maintains 
the spell that has suspended the cave dwellers in 
time. The shield is none other than the Shield of 
the Yawning Dead, likely left here by Crag Hack 
and his allies after their battle against Sandro.
To claim the artifact, the Heroes must first 
break the spell (Mysticism Skill check). 
However, doing so will release the minotaur and 
troglodytes, who will immediately charge into 
battle against the Heroes. (Find enemy stats at 
the end of the adventure on page 56.)
The Heroes can avoid combat if they propose a 
clever alternative solution and succeed in a Skill 
check.
Gaining the Shield
A Hero who claims the Shield of the Yawning 
Dead may add it to their character sheet as 
a resource with the Frightening quality. The 
shield is an artifact granting +3 to Tenacity of 
a Hero that is using it, and allows its wielder 
to add a d20 to opposed Skill checks when 
defending, but not when attacking.
When the Heroes reach the cavern where 
the artifact rests, read or paraphrase the 
following description:
You find yourselves in a vast cavern, a 
place where, perhaps as far back as the 
Bracaduun Empire, a deadly battle was 
fought. Beneath your feet, yellowed 
skulls and rusted armor litter the ground. 
The air is thick with the scent of dust, 
rust, and magic. In the center of the 
hall, several groups of troglodytes and 
an enormous minotaur stand in perfect 
stillness as if frozen in time. They all face 
away from the entrance.
Beyond them, a much more recent 
pedestal holds a massive shield. The area 
around is fizzling with magic as if the 
shield altered the very air around it, the 
stone of the pedestal is partially molten, 
and pieces of stone hover around as if 
the presence of the dreadful artifact bent 
reality. In front of the shield, encrusted 
into the pedestal, is a gemstone, glowing 
with a deep purple light.

Battle Phase
A battle with Tyraxor is almost inevitable 
during the campaign. How exactly it unfolds 
depends on the course of events in the session. 
Below is the structure of one possible battle 
scenario.
If the battle occurs under different 
circumstances, adjust the presented elements 
accordingly while keeping the strategic 
objectives’ numerical values and distances 
consistent.
Defending the Settlement
Battle Duration: 3 rounds
The Heroes encounter Tyraxor as the Wolf 
Riders, supported by several hordes of goblins, 
launch an attack on a small fortified settlement.
At the start of the battle, read or 
paraphrase the following description:
The villagers, hidden behind palisades, 
armed only with pitchforks and flails, are 
ready to fight to the death. However, they 
stand little chance of repelling the enemy 
in the long run. Tyraxor and the rest of his 
army watch from a small hill, expecting to 
soon bask in the sight of burning homes. 
When he spots the approaching Heroes 
and their armies, he gives the order to 
repel them.
Initial Strategic 
Objectives
Breaking Through Tyraxor’s Guard
Most of Tyraxor’s forces consist of small 
goblin groups that pose little threat to the 
Heroes and their armies alone. However, 
the elite hobgoblins form a tight defensive 
line protecting their warlord. They must be 
overcome!
Progress Track for the Strategic Objective: 10
Range: Close
Reward: Shattering the hobgoblins’ defense 
opens a path to Tyraxor and the enemy’s rear 
lines. All Maneuvers and actions to achieve 
strategic objectives at medium and long distances 
have their Target Number reduced by 1.
Consequence: The hobgoblins, retreating after 
the battle, will still manage to inflict a loss on 
one of the Heroes’ units.
Preventing the Fire
The Wolf Riders, wielding torches, aim to break 
through the palisades and set fire to the barn, 
where a defenseless portion of the villagers is 
hiding. They must be stopped!
Progress Track for the Strategic Objective: 4
(imminent objective)
Range: Close
Reward: The villagers are saved from a horrific fate.
Consequence: The remaining villagers stop 
fighting the goblins and either attempt to rescue 
their loved ones or flee. The progress track of the 
“Repel the Main Enemy Forces” objective increases 
by two spaces.
Repelling the Main Enemy Forces
In many areas, the villagers’ resistance is 
weakening. Several sections of the palisades 
have already fallen, and goblins are slowly 
pulling aside a wagon blocking the main road. 
The enemy must be defeated before they break 
through!

At Tyraxor’s command, a pack of Wolf Riders

A charging group of Wolf Riders suddenly

Threat Points Cost:: 5

Progress Track for the Strategic Objective: 4
Range: Close
Reward: The enemy has been repelled, and the 
army’s flank is safe for now.
Consequence: One of the Heroes marks a loss 
on a unit chosen by the GM.

Scaling the Battle to the 
Number of Heroes
The strategic objectives outlined above were designed 
for a session with five Heroes. If fewer Heroes are 
participating, modify the battle as follows:
	
¾ For 2 Heroes:
	
¾ Remove the Preventing the Fire and 
Just One Catapult objectives (Just One 
Catapult may be introduced later as an 
additional objective for 5 Threat Points).
	
¾ The Breaking Through Tyraxor’s Guard 
objective has a progress track of 8 
instead of 10.
	
¾ The Slaying Tyraxor objective changes 
its range from Medium to Long.
	
¾ The battle is extended to 4 Rounds 
instead of 3.
	
¾ For 3 Heroes:
	
¾ Remove the Just One Catapult objective 
(it may be introduced later as an 
additional objective for 5 Threat Points).
	
¾ The Breaking Through Tyraxor’s Guard 
objective has a progress track of 8 
instead of 10.
	
¾ The Slaying Tyraxor objective changes 
its range from Medium to Long.
	
¾ For 4 Heroes:
	
¾ The Breaking Through Tyraxor’s Guard 
objective has a progress track of 8 
instead of 10.
