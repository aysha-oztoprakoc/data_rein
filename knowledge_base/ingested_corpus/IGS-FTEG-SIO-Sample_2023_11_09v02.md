Quick Start
Overview
Overview
Use this book however you darn well want to.  
Here are some suggestions to get started.
For a more in-depth discussion of how to use this 
book, concepts introduced and referenced herein, how 
to use certain rules concepts across a wide variety of 
rules systems, and much more, please refer to the back 
of the book and its Appendices, starting on page 25.
Picking an Image
Picking an Image
Get two six-sided dice, ideally of different colors.  
Designate one of the dice “Row” and the other 
“Column”.
Roll those 
  2d6.
Refer to the grid’s row number that shows up on your 
Row die.
Refer to the grid’s column number that is shown on 
your Column die.
That’s the image that the fates, and this Oracle, have 
selected for you in that moment.
Using the Results
You can use the image itself as inspiration for your 
answer, and let your creativity fly.
Or, you can refer to more detail.  Each image has its 
own dedicated full page, containing over 25 pieces of 
information related to that image.  These range from 
the name of the image, to a unique item or benefit, to a 
specific quest that you can immediately use.
Introduction to the Free Sample Edition
Overview
Overview
This Free Sample Edition of the Solo Image Oracle is 
perfectly usable on its own.  
This book has 16 images in a 4x4 grid.  
The full book has 36 images in a 6x6 grid.
The content that is provided with each image in this 
book is exactly the same as what’s provided in the full 
edition of the Image Oracle.
The full version also contains rules descriptions and 
mappings across major fantasy TTRPG systems that 
help make the most of every element and result that’s 
provided.
Enjoy!
What’s Here
What’s Here
Sixteen images ready to spark inspiration in the 
brains of the solo adventurer, or the time-pressed or 
creatively stymied traditional G/DM.
For each of those 16 images, over 25 creative elements: 
pieces of information that either provide an answer, 
generate adventure content, or guide your adventures 
in different ways.
All in all, even this free version contains over 400 
ways to make your adventures more interesting.
What’s Not Here
What’s Not Here
This Free edition of the Image Oracle is a 4x4 grid of 
16 images.  
The full edition of the book contains a 6x6 grid, and 36 
images.  
So, you’re short 20 images from the full version... and 
the corresponding 500+ creative elements associated 
with them.
In addition, the full edition contains an extensive 
Appendix of “Rules System Mapping”.  That 9-page 
section that’s not included in this free edition 
describes rules conventions across major fantasy 
TTRPG systems—5E, Pathfinder, Pathfinder 2E, OSR, 
and DCC.
For example, this section describes mechanically what 
a Dexterity saving throw means in OSR games.
Conventions
Conventions
Certain aspects of this book may still reference the full 
edition of the material.  
Any reference to the total number of images, for 
example, or rolling d6s instead of d4s to obtain a 
specific image result, have to be taken with the context 
of your free version in mind.

Ways to Use
Ways to Use
There’s lots of ways to use this book.  Here are some 
common ones.
A Specific Question
Form a specific question in your head.  It could have a 
Yes or No answer, or it could be something a bit more 
free-form, but still specific in terms of context.
For example:
•	 “Does this room contain an exit?”
•	 “Can I find any interesting plants in this section of 
the forest?”
•	 “Are there any fresh water sources in the area?”
•	 “Did the archduke leave any information about where 
they may have been headed?”
An Open-Ended Question
You can also ask more open-ended queries of this book. 
These can still have some context, but you would be 
not satisfied with a simple yes or no in response.
For best effect, consider at least asking a question with 
one of the element categories in mind: 
  General, 
  Social, 
  Combat, or 
  Quest / Encounter.
Some examples of an open-ended question:
•	 “Who is drinking at the tavern bar?”
•	 “What are these enemies like?”
•	 “What do I need to do to make progress in the next 
step of my quest?”
Specific Element
Once you’re familiar with the 25+ pieces of 
information that each image provides Results for (see 
Elements, p 12), you might simply pick one of those 
elements and randomly pick an image, then flip to 
that image’s dedicated page to read the result for that 
specific piece of information.
Generate Adventure Content
Arguably, any one of the hundreds of individual pieces 
of information in this book could be used to generate 
adventure content, given sufficient creativity and/or 
context.
But there are several elements that flat-out provide 
new and interesting adventure content, ready to run 
with zero preparation.  
These are:
•	 Social: Describes unique features of an NPC you’re 
talking with.
•	 New Quest: An entire quest snippet, ready to 
run as-is, or to expand or make your own with 
embellishment.
•	 Reward: A unique item (magical or mundane, but 
still interesting!), or beneficial effect for the party.
•	 Penalty: A negative consequence for one or more 
PCs.
•	 Combat: Make each group of wandering enemies 
interesting with a wide range of descriptions and 
tactical effects.
•	 Random Encounter: From wandering seers to 
complex intrigue, from the start of new quests to 
revenge from those affected by old quests, each 
image contains an entire one-off encounter ready to 
deploy.
Flip to a Page
Just flip to a random page, and find inspiration there.  
No dice needed!
Every page of this book was designed to provide 
inspiration and opportunity for guiding your solo 
game.  Well, except for the back cover.  And the front 
cover, I guess.  And, well, maybe the Table of Contents 
isn’t that exciting.  You get my point.
The Start of Something 
The Start of Something 
Special
Special
The best part about this book is that it is hardly limited 
to what’s provided.  Feel free to use the approach and 
concepts presented here to fuel your own Oracle.  
Just draw a bunch of pictures (or find them 
somewhere), and start creating your own Solo Image 
Oracle!

How To Use This Book
Overview
Overview
Use this book to create adventure content, derive 
inspiration, and guide your adventures.  
Although this book was created primarily for use 
in solo adventures, there’s no reason a G/DM in a 
traditional gaming context couldn’t use it as well, 
either because they’re time-crunched to prepare for 
tonight’s session, if they have writer’s block, or if they 
simply want to introduce more randomness into the 
proceedings.
How you use this book is as unique as you as a player, 
a G/DM, or a solo quester, and as the campaign you are 
running or find yourself in. 
There is no end to the number of ways you can use the 
hundreds of options and results in this book to fuel 
infinite adventures.  
Other FlexTale Books
Other FlexTale Books
This book has been designed to use several of the same 
terms and concepts that have been introduced in other 
FlexTale books.  
You can use this book without having any of those 
other books.
However, you can get a great deal more out of this 
book if you use it in combination with other books, 
since it’s been designed to work directly with them for 
a very rich solo adventuring experience.
Notable connections:
•	 FlexAI Guidebook: The definitive guide to 
dynamically determining contextually-appropriate 
behavior for monsters and NPCs in both combat and 
social encounters.  You’ll see references to FlexAI 
concepts throughout the Combat and Social sections 
of each image’s results.
•	 Solo Adventuring Toolkit: The most comprehensive 
and in-depth guide to solo TTRPG adventuring, this 
book has enormously rich sections describing how 
to generate dynamic quests, an easy-to-implement 
Clues concept, and much, much more.  Results in the 
Quests & Encounters sections often refer to these 
concepts.
•	 Hexcrawl Toolkit: Although there’s no explicit 
references to particular concepts that this book 
introduces, there are many results that mention 
distances in terms such as “a fortnight’s travel”.  Any 
questing done at the overland, macro scale of detail 
will benefit from the Hexcrawl Toolkit, the most 
definitive and complete guide to overland travel for 
TTRPGs ever written.
Terminology
Terminology
Some of these terms may be obvious, but others may 
have a more particular meaning in the context of this 
book.  It’s easiest to make sure we’re all on the same 
page by defining some terms up front.
Question
A statement that you make about what happens next in 
your game.  
This can be simple (“Does this cave lead to the 
chapel?”), or complex (“What do I need to do to defeat 
the necromancer?”).  The Question can be vague 
(“what’s going on in town right now?”) or specific (“Is 
the king allergic to raspberries?”).  
Generally speaking, the more simple and/or vague 
a Question is, the more work you have to do to 
Interpret the Result and Apply it.  
Similarly, the more specific a Question is, the easier it 
will be to interpret.
You
“You” is assumed to be you, the person reading this 
right now, and using this book in the game session.  
Almost always, this will be the G/DM.  If you are 
playing 
  solo, you’re pretty much treating the 
book itself as the G/DM, at least for the purposes of 
answering the Question you pose.
Context
The complete set of circumstances that are in play 
when you ask a Question is the Context of that 
Question.
Your PC’s statistics, their position in the game world, 
whether they are in combat, their opponents, and so 
on all count as part of the Context.  This is important, 
because a given Result may apply very differently 
depending on the Context.
Oracle
This book, in its entirety, is an Oracle.  
You ask a Question of the Oracle by using the book to 
generate a dynamic Result that applies in Context to 
that Question.
Determination
When you ask a Question of this Oracle, you roll some

dice, refer to pages in this book, and come up with one 
or more Results.  The process of doing all of that is 
called Determination.
When you have a Question, and use this book to 
generate answers, you are Determining the relevant 
adventure content.
Result
A Result is an answer you seek and obtain from this 
book.
The form and nature of a Result depends a great deal 
on the Question that was posed to produce it.  “Are 
there Orcs in the room when I enter it” is a much 
different Question than “What does the King want 
from us?”.
More formally, a Result is one of the words, phrases, 
classifications, or full descriptions that is provided in 
this book, as it relates to a given dice roll.  “Yes” is a 
Result, but then so is a paragraph-long description of 
a special magical item.
Interpretation
Although some of the Results presented in this book 
are several-paragraphs-long outlines of entire quests 
or summaries of entire magic items, most of what 
you’ll find here is intentionally vague: there’s enough 
detail to get you started, but there’s not so much detail  
that it ties the Result to a very specific Context that 
wouldn’t work elsewhere.
The creativity that you employ to take a Result, adapt 
it to a Context, and have it make sense and have it be 
the most fun and useful is called Interpretation.
Element
An Element is one of the possible types of Result that 
you can receive from rolling an image.  It’s a kind 
of information, inspiration, or description that is 
related to that picture, either in a literal sense, or in a 
figurative one.  Some are even puns.  
Which Element you pay attention to when you ask 
a Question depends on that Question, but also the 
Context.  For example, if your Context involves an 
active, raging battle, you’ll likely pay attention to 
different Elements in the Results than if the Context 
is simply speaking to an apothecary.
The Elements section below lists all of the Elements 
that each image receives in this book.
Apply
You Apply a Result to a Context in response to a 
Question, based on your Interpretation. 
Applying a Result just means that it takes effect in 
your game.  Stuff happens in the game as a result of 
the Result that you generated from this book.
Usually, you Apply a Result only after you’ve 
considered how to Interpret it in the Context of the 
Question you asked.

Overview
There’s 36 images, each of which has 25 elements: 
that’s nearly 1,000 different pieces of dynamic content 
and inspiration.
Within that vast landscape of potential adventure 
options and creative sparks, there are some concepts 
that are referred to with shorthand or terms that may 
not apply to your rules system, house rules, or other 
understanding.  Not all of these are intuitive.
I’ve tried to anticipate the potential issues and 
questions that might arise, and to identify tools and 
techniques that, while common with many G/DMs, 
might not have found their way to your own gaming 
table or usage. 
This section describes as much of that sort of thing as I 
could predict.
Advantage and Disadvantage
This is a mechanic that is borrowed from Fifth 
Edition.  This book uses this technique, and you 
can use it, regardless of whether your rules system 
natively invokes the concept.
For those new to the concept: if you have 
  
Advantage, you may roll twice, and select the result 
that are more favorable to you.  If you’re making an 
attack roll, for example, and have advantage, you roll 
two d20s, and apply your attack bonus to each exactly 
the same.  Use the higher of those two rolls to resolve 
the attack.  If you rolled a 4 and a 17, you’d only use 
the 17, and ignore the 4.
Conversely, if you suffer 
  Disadvantage, you must 
roll twice, and must select the result that is less 
advantageous to you.  In the above example with the 
same rolls, having disadvantage means you would have 
to use the 4, and ignore the 17.
Canceling
If a circumstance would impose both advantage and 
disadvantage, neither applies and you roll normally.
This cancellation is not “one for one” or mathematical; 
any number of advantages and any number of 
disadvantages all cancel each other out completely.  
That is: If a context would apply one of these concepts 
more than once, and the other of these concepts fewer 
times but at least once, they all cancel each other out, 
and you roll normally.  
For example, if a circumstance would apply advantage 
twice, and disadvantage once, none of these effects 
applies and you roll normally.
Stacking
Advantage and disadvantage do not “stack”.  There is 
no such thing as “super-advantage” where you roll 
three dice and pick the best of the three, for example.  
You either have advantage, or you don’t; same thing 
for disadvantage.
Who Gets the Benefit
Advantage and disadvantage always refer to the 
perspective of the person making the roll, unless 
otherwise described.  
So, for example, if a result imposes disadvantage on 
rolls to determine random encounters, you roll two 
dice, and if either one of them indicates a random 
encounter takes place, then one does.  This is because 
you’re rolling on behalf of the party to determine if 
they run into an event.  
Some Assembly Required
It’s in the nature of an Oracle tool such as this that 
some “assembly is required”.  Either what is described 
in the results is intentionally too vague to be of use, or 
its specificity means that it will not completely apply 
smoothly in every situation.  As the G/DM or solo 
player, part of your job is to assess whether the results 
make sense in the context they’re being generated 
for.  If a small piece of the result doesn’t seem to 
apply in context, just ignore it.  For example, if a 
Combat Reaction result indicates that reinforcements 
surround the party, but it is physically impossible for 
reinforcements to arrive from the direction which 
would be required for this to happen—or it’s simply 
not possible for the party to be surrounded at all—then 
just ignore that piece of the results altogether.
Sanity Check
Any time you determine a result, if it doesn’t feel 
right for the circumstances or your need, ignore it and 
reroll.  
Although it’s possible to take something that you might 
not initially have thought of and get creative about 
adapting it into the situation, trust your gut and first 
impression unless such inventiveness is what excites 
you about solo adventuring in the first place.

Active Quests
At any given moment, an adventuring party may have 
zero, one, or more quests or stories that they have 
been exposed to, but have not yet fully resolved.  These 
are active quests.  
If a result refers to “the active quest”, it means the one 
that the party is most actively engaged with in context. 
If it refers to “an active quest”, or “the quest of your 
choice”, or something a bit more ambiguously along 
those lines, you may pick which active quest(s) the 
result applies to.  
If a result refers to one or more active quests, and you 
currently have zero active quests, start a new quest 
and apply the result to that new quest.
Selecting Targets
Unless otherwise indicated, any result that describes 
“an X” will randomly select one.  
For example, if a result says “one PC”, pick one of the 
PCs in the adventuring party at random.  
If the set of possible targets for a result is not equal 
to the size of a die, make a list of the candidates, and 
number them; then, roll a die of a size at least as large 
as the list’s length.  Reroll until you get a result that is 
in the list.  
For example, if a result indicated “membership in a 
random faction”, and there were fourteen factions 
defined in the game world thus far, you’d make a 
numbered list of those 14, then roll a d20, rerolling 
and results of 15-20.
Advanced List-Making Decisions
You might be called upon to select something from a 
large or confusing set of possible targets.  For example, 
“a random city within a week’s journey”.  
First, locate all of the possible targets; then, add each 
one to a list.  In the example given, you could estimate, 
and say that, despite terrain differences, you want to 
pick every city within 20 hexes.  
Spend additional time to whittle the list down to a 
finer level of precision only if that’s what you want 
to do; a generally accurate list assembled quickly is 
better than a fine-tuned and perfect list that takes an 
hour to figure out.
Effect Duration
Unless a result describes otherwise, all effects listed 
last until the end of the current social or combat 
encounter.  
If the effect described does not apply to the context of 
an encounter, it lasts until the next long rest the party 
completes successfully.  
Many results will mention a duration of “a fortnight”; 
this is two weeks or 14 days.
Liquid Wealth
Coins and gemstones with a resale value count as 
“liquid wealth”.  Typically, this is expressed as a 
function of “the party”; the party’s liquid wealth is 
equal to the sum of all coins they carry, plus the value 
of all gems they carry, regardless of how these items 
are divvied up amongst individual PCs.  
Note that in most cases, the specific amount this 
calculation equals doesn’t necessarily represent the 
context “knowing” how much wealth the party has; it’s 
simply the basis for whatever amount needs to apply.  
Damage in Results
Some results indicate merely that the PCs are 
“subjected to damage”.  This may of a particular 
damage type, e.g., Piercing or Acid.  
This should be on the order of 1d6 per 3 full Hit Dice 
of the average party or fraction thereof.  For example, 
a group of 8th-level adventurers would suffer 3d6 
damage.  
Poison Effects
Unless more specifically described, a “poison effect” 
reduces one ability score, selected at random.  Roll a

ability score damage natively, this effect lasts until

Your adventuring party’s APL is equal to the average 
class level or hit dice of each PC, rounded up.  For 
example, a group of adventurers who are levels 10, 11, 
9, and 10 would have an APL of 10.

  solo, and have only a single PC,

Social Encounters and Checks
A social encounter is a complete conversation, or a 
complete series of interactions that involve the same 
participants (e.g., the PCs and another group; one PC 
and one NPC; the party and one NPC; and so on).  
When the composition of the people involved on either

encounter has begun and the old one has ended, for the 
purposes of effects whose duration is defined by social 
encounters.  At the G/DM’s discretion, such effects may 
continue on into an adjoining social encounter as well.  
A social check is a single interaction within a social 
encounter, and typically involves a question, a 
prompt for information, or a desire to drive your 
conversational companion into action or inaction.  
Each social check involves a single skill check or saving 
throw, or an opposed roll between the participants, as 
befits your rules system and how your gaming table 
conducts such scenarios.

it might be a Diplomacy check, or an Intimidate

Let’s use the common example of an enemy fleeing 
once they are reduced to “half their starting quantity 
or hit points”.  If you are facing a single enemy, this 
means they will run away once their current HP is less 
than or equal to their maximum hit points.  If you’re 
facing more than one foe, this applies to body count, 
not hit points: once half their starting number of 
creatures, rounded up, has been felled, the remaining 
group will flee.  
“Starting strength” is the quantity of creatures you 
face in an encounter, and their maximum hit points.  
Take care with percentages: “When reduced to 75% 
of their starting strength” means if they take 25% 
damage or casualties, for example.
Morale Checks
Some rules systems involve “morale checks”, wherein 
an enemy must make a saving throw or run away, for 
example.  
If your rules system lacks such a notion, any effect that 
refers to “on morale checks” instead applies to any 
mind-affecting effects which would take the target out 
of combat entirely (confusion, insanity, and so on).
Conversational Volleys
A conversational “volley” is one exchange of words 
between two parties.  The PCs ask a single question, 
and the NPC responds to it, for example.  A volley may 
take a handful of seconds, or a minute or longer if 
preamble and context are provided to the question or 
prompt, or if the response is a very long one.  
The opening of a chat, where one party acknowledges 
and greets the other, counts as a conversational volley.  
If you’re unclear about whether a volley has happened, 
if both parties to a conversation have spoken, and 
the topic has not changed, that generally counts as a 
volley.
