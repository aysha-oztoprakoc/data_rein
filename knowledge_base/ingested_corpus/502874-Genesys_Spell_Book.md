Magic in Genesys isn’t a separate game bolted onto the rules. It’s the same dice in your hand pointed at older
powers — study, devotion, instinct, death, spirit, and the dangerous places where those powers overlap.
This book hands you a complete fantasy magic system, table-ready and built on the engine you already know.
Every spell lists its difficulty, strain, range, duration, overload options, empower spends, dispel rules, and what
happens when it goes wrong — all in Exodus terms. No conversion. No guesswork.
Use it as a player’s spellbook. Use it as a GM’s reference. Use it as the foundation for a fantasy setting of your
own.

Reading the Spell Block
The colored bar before each spell name marks the tra‐
dition — single-color for Arcane, Divine, or Primal.
The pill row beneath the title reads left to right as:
◆Difficulty — the dice pool for the casting check. Some spells in‐
clude an upgraded difficulty die (red 
).
◆Strain — the strain the caster suffers when casting. A spell that
costs 1 strain is simple; 2 is normal; 3–5 indicates a powerful or
pushed effect.
◆Range — Self, Engaged, Touch, Short, Medium, Long, or
Extreme.
◆Duration — Instant, Round, Encounter, Scene, or Until
Dispelled.
◆Dispel — only present on spells with a non-instantaneous duration.
This is the dice pool an opposing caster must roll to end the spell
early.
If the spell deals damage or carries weapon-like quali‐
ties, a second pill row appears: Damage · Crit ·
Qualities.
Casting a Spell — The Workflow
1. Declare the spell and target. State the spell, choose a legal tar‐
get within range, and confirm you meet the prerequisites.
2. Build the dice pool. Take the listed Difficulty as your difficulty
pool. Add ability dice from the relevant characteristic, and profi‐
ciency upgrades from your skill ranks (Arcana, Divine, Primal —
or the averaged/lowest rank for a combined-tradition spell). Add
boost dice and setback dice as the situation warrants.
3. Choose Overload options (optional). Each Overload option
modifies the dice pool before you roll — adding a difficulty die,
upgrading a difficulty die to a challenge die, adding a setback die,
or adding a boost die in exchange for weakening the spell.
4. Pay strain. Suffer the listed strain (modified by talents).
5. Roll the pool. Add up successes vs failures, advantages vs
threats, triumphs and despairs.
6. Apply the effect on success. A spell with at least one net success
goes off. The Effect line tells you what happens.
7. Spend Empower symbols. Each a, aa, t, etc. listed in
Empower may be activated by spending matching symbols from
the result. Empower riders stack onto the base effect.
8. Resolve threats and despair. Threats and despair create compli‐
cations per the GM, including the spell’s Mishap if one is listed
and a despair was rolled.
Strain & Difficulty (player + GM guidance)
Strain represents the caster’s mental and spiritual ex‐
haustion from working magic. The published strain on
each spell is the baseline cost — talents and conditions
modify it at the table.
◆Strain 1. Cantrip-level effects. The kind of magic an apprentice
does without a second thought.
◆Strain 2. A normal spell at any difficulty. The default for journey‐
man and most adept-tier spells.
◆Strain 3–5. Powerful or pushed effects. The spell does more than
its difficulty alone suggests — multiple effects, sustained dura‐
tions, large areas, or scene-changing reach.
A spell that costs 4 strain at difficulty 3 has typically
had its base difficulty reduced by one die in exchange
for the extra strain (the strain-down trade). 5 strain is
the cap.
Difficulty represents what the rules don’t al‐
ready cover and shouldn’t be free. A spell adds a dif‐
ficulty die when it pushes damage above its tier, adds
a secondary effect, spans multiple turns, targets multi‐
ple creatures, or creates an effect the rules don’t al‐
ready describe. Difficulty 1 is a cantrip; difficulty 5 is
reality-bending High Magic.
Overload options are added by the caster at cast
time and are independent of the published strain
trade. An Overload option adds difficulty (or upgrades
a difficulty die) in exchange for an enhanced effect —
more damage, more range, more targets, sustain.
Dispel — How to End an Active Spell
Every spell with a duration other than Instant carries
a Dispel dice pool, shown as the rightmost pill on the
spell block.
Who can dispel. The original caster may always
end their own active spell as an out-of-turn incidental
at no cost. An opposing caster may attempt to end the
spell early using their own appropriate magical skill
(Arcana, Divine, Primal — or the averaged/lowest rank
for a combined-tradition spell, matching the spell be‐
ing dispelled).
The check. The dispelling caster rolls their casting
check at the listed Dispel difficulty. The dispelling
caster suffers their own strain cost as if casting any
other spell (default 2 strain, or as their tradition’s
Counterspell-equivalent specifies).
Caster’s net successes ride along. When the origi‐
nal spell was cast, the caster’s net successes from that
casting check are added to the dispel difficulty. A spell
cast with 3 net successes at base Dispel 
 is dis‐
pelled at difficulty 5 — until enough time passes that
the original effect winds down.

On success. The spell ends immediately. All ongoing

On failure. The spell remains active. The dispelling

On Despair from a dispel attempt. GM discretion.
Common results include the dispeller suffering an ad‐

Resist — How a Target Breaks Free of a Sustained Spell
Several spells affect a single creature for more than an
instant — a binding, a curse, a dominant compulsion.
When the spell’s Effect line says Resist (Opposed), or
when a sustained spell targets a creature directly, use
this rule.
The target is held without recourse on the round
of the cast. They get one full round of the effect no
matter what.
Beginning at the end of each of the target’s turns
thereafter, the target may roll the noted skill (usually
Discipline, sometimes Coercion or Resilience) as a ma‐
neuver. The check’s difficulty equals the caster’s net
successes from the original casting check, mini‐
mum 1, maximum 5. On success, the spell ends. On
failure, the target remains affected through their next
turn.
A barely-cast spell collapses on the first attempt. A
spectacularly-cast one is a Hard or Daunting check to
break. The roll and the consequence scale together —
the player who threw their best dice into the casting
earns a stickier hold.
Triumph spend. If the casting check produced a
t, the caster may spend it to upgrade one of the
difficulty dice on every future resist roll to a challenge
die. The binding now risks despair on a failed break —
at the GM’s discretion, a despair on the resist may in‐
flict an additional small consequence (target falls
prone when freed, drops a held item, suffers 1 strain
from the cramp, etc.).
Resist vs. Dispel — what’s the difference?
◆Dispel is external: another caster ends the spell from outside, by
rolling against the listed Dispel pool. Anyone with the right mag‐
ical skill may attempt it.
◆Resist is internal: the spell’s own target attempts to throw it off,
by rolling Discipline (or as listed) against the original casting’s
net successes. Only the affected target may resist; allies cannot
resist on their behalf.
A sustained spell may be ended either way — dis‐
pelled by a friendly caster, or resisted by the target.
Whichever happens first stops the spell.

A Note on Casting Combined Traditions
When casting a combined-tradition spell, your
skill rank for the casting check is the floor of the
average of the two prerequisite skills (e.g., Arcana
+ Primal for Necromancy, divided by two, rounded
down). For High Magic spells, your skill rank is the
lowest of Arcana, Divine, and Primal. The talent
rank gates access to the spell; the averaged or
floored skill rank determines the dice you roll once
you qualify.
