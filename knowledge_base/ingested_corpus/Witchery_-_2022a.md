WHAT THIS IS
This is a unified magic system, designed to be adaptable to most 
traditional tabletop roleplaying systems. 
  
This magic system is focused, like most RPG magic systems, on a list of 
magical effects that are in practice organized into spells, with the 
option to create more. It has been structured to feel more akin to 
witchcraft or folk magic than to the Vancian wizardry most traditional 
in RPGs; not a great rarity, but worth noting.  Well-read gamers who 
have encountered Ars Magica or the Verb/Noun casting system of 
GURPS Magic will find this system very familiar; it is one of their 
descendants (and proudly so).
  
Each spell in this system is built by combining an element (like fire), an 
art (like conjuring), and a conduit (like brew). However, these 
components don't dictate the spell exactly; a fire-conjuring brew might 
be a blackened lump that explodes on any hard impact, or a glass 
bottle that sheds light for a few hours when shaken up.
  
To cast a spell, your character must be an adept with all of the three 
components involved (the element, art and conduit), and make a roll or 
otherwise engage in mechanics to do so properly. The Guide invents 
dangers and added benefits possible for this spellcasting, possibly in 
advance, possibly after the fact.

WHO WITCHES ARE
A witch is a practitioner of magic as it is described herein; that's all 
there is to it.  They may be of any ethnic or religious origin, any gender 
or sexuality.  In many settings, some groupings may have easier access 
to witchcraft, such as if the body of witchcraft primarily recruits from 
women, or if those of less-common genders (according to the cultural 
or local norms of the setting) are assumed to be magically touched and 
thus taught witchcraft, or if some other setting feature which skews the 
demographics applies.

SETTING ASSUMPTIONS
The flavour text of the spell lists, names of and organization of the 
magical powers, and the notes which follow assume a setting that 
roughly resembles a folkloric Europe.
 
A folkloric Europe might mean using actual maps of historic Europe 
where "all the legends are true", having actual past events included with 
some fictional reinterpretation.  This might even be extended to 
include conflict between the Catholic church and pagan faiths (which 
would almost certainly act as the standard body witches work from, in 
such an extension).  
 
Moving away from history and into fantasy might mean a somewhat 
more poetic reality that still acts as a strong analogue, as found in the 
works of Guy Gavriel Kay (Tigana, A Song For Arbonne, and so on). 
Alternatively, the folkloric aspect could be prominent, setting the game 
in a world similar to a fairy tale, as in The Eyes Of The Dragon 
(Stephen King) or Uprooted (Naomi Novik).
 
For the most part, traditional fantasy settings are heavily linked to 
folkloric Europe, so dropping these witches into such a setting can 
likely be done with only minor tweaks.
 
Should the Guide wish to step away from that implied setting to other 
cultural or fictional baselines, some reconstruction of the material 
presented may be needed.  This might include renaming a significant 
number of items, rephrasing or discarding spells, or dividing up 
elements, arts, or conduits among various traditions of magic (so that 
some are the province of oracles while others are handled by  priests or 
wizards or what-have-you).
5


 
 
COVENS
Witches can organize themselves in any number of manners in differing 
settings.  However, the most common forms of organization across 
settings are the solitary witch and the tightly-knit working group, or 
coven.  If the tone of magic is more academic and “wizardly,” covens 
expand to become colleges; if militant, they may become units.
 
Since gameplay of RPGs lends itself well to tightly-knit working groups, 
it's assumed here that player-run witches will either be solitary (or 
nearly so) witches operating with a group of non-witches, or that all the 
player characters will be part of a coven together.  
6
STEADINGS
Whether solitary or based in a coven, most witches can generally be 
expected to have (collectively, if a coven) a territory they consider their 
home – wandering magicians are heavily outnumbered by settled ones 
in most such genres.  These homes are referred to here as steadings.  A 
steading may easily be large enough to include a village or other small 
settlement.  Large towns and cities can be also assumed to have their 
witches, but the material of the rules suits country life best, so the focus 
is there.
 
Given that magical sites (hearths) exist in these rules, witches can be 
expected to take ownership of at least some of them and build 
steadings around them.  When hearths are discovered early, before 
they become the province of a ruling spirit, they can be built up so a 
soliary witch or coven can move in - the basic work of improving a 
hearth to steading levels being the witch equivalent of a barn-raising.
 
A game played with these rules and centred on witches could very 
easily be started with a group of witches about to form a new coven 
around a hearth, sorting steading boundaries and hosting the gathering 
to build their hearth up.  Should the Guide have a coven of characters 
and no other starting point in mind, this is a pretty good go-to.
 
Groups of witches having steadings and living there means, plainly, 
having lives. Friends, lovers, children.  Small politics with one another 
and with settlements.  As figures of real and potent powers, Witches 
are very likely to be respected (or feared!) in and around their 
steadings. They may thus be sought out for aid and counsel, whether 
openly or in desperation, and looked to by those both within the 
steading and without as natural arbiters of, sources of, and champions 
for any magical concerns.


 
 
SPIRITS
The element of shade affects the spirits of the dead; whether these 
spirits are in fact immortal souls or merely memories left behind to 
take on some life of their own is left to the Guide to answer (or not 
answer, as desired).  
 
Likewise, the hearth element can affect spirits of place, known as 
geniuses.  These can be portrayed as weak intelligences born of magic, 
or as gods of stream and tree, as needed (more on that shortly).
 
Finally, the element of dream makes mention of beings that can reach 
the world through dreams.  Beyond those notes, no great mention is 
made of them, leaving them open to interpretation, though the basic 
folkloric explanation is that these are faeries.
 
All three of these types of spirit (or whatever the faeries might be) exist 
as places the Guide can further develop ideas.  The setting can exist 
under threat of invasion, it can be filled with wakeful mountains and 
sinister forests, it can have places and times where the dead walk and 
take on solid shapes to harry the living.  Alternatively, some of these 
forces can be benign to humanity at large (though not necessarily so to 
witches in particular).  Ancestors may watch over the living, natural 
spirits seek only to preserve their territory, and faeries in truth acts as 
stewards to dreams – but bent magicians, necromancers, defilers, and 
the like – might upset this order and raise the ire of these forces or 
even twist them into evil servitude.
 
The Guide should consider the basic cosmology they want to portray 
so far as these forces are concerned, and also consider whether that 
cosmology is understood by the setting at large and by witches in 
general.
7
BEASTS
Given that the focus is likely to be rural or wild, animals will be 
abundant in the areas witches frequent, and many witches will have 
some magic to relate to them.  In stories of the sort involving witches, 
beasts are often significantly wiser and more human-like than their 
portrayals in modern culture.  The Guide should decide if this is true in 
the setting, and (if so) whether it is is entirely or only partly so; are 
there lineages of wise beasts, or just the rare one touched by magic?


 
 
WONDERS AND GODS
The existence of mystical sites on the scale at which witches operate 
and the possible emergence of spirits which govern them suggests 
strongly that such sites also exist on much greater scales, overseen by 
godly spirits.
 
The genius of the volcano, the god of the forest, the doom-spectre of 
some cursed ruin, all make sense within this framework.  So too does 
the enchanted wood surrounding the tree of golden apples, and other 
such configurations – these are hearths run wild and grown up to 
immense scope.
 
Depending on the nature of such places and the temperament of such 
spirits, such areas might drive away humans (and humanlike beings), or 
might collect them as allies, servants, or worshippers.  
8
ON ESSENCE
There are minor mentions throughout this text which refer to energy 
being channelled, but that energy isn't otherwise quantified or 
explained.  This is a deliberate omission, intended to leave the 
mechanization of such energies entirely optional; these rules already 
add quite a bit of handling of dangers to an engine, and also adding a 
layer of abstract resource management seemed a bit much.
 
If it is desirable to bring it in, such energy would likely be best termed 
essence if keeping to the folkloric assumptions throughout.  This might 
become quintessence or aether if moving to a more 'wizardly' framing.  
Mana, as a term, is a modern alteration of an Polynesian term, and in 
terms of folklore it's meanings are quite specific and not 'witchy' as it's 
framed here. 
 
In rules terms, essence would likely be a pool of points carried by a 
witch, spent to improve magical resolution or cancel out dangers 
directly.  Hearth would exist as natural pooling points of essence, from 
which it might be gathered (though not without risk if the hearth has a 
protective genius).
 


 
 
THE WITCHY GAME
Given all that, the more the action of a game touches on witchcraft, 
the more the action will involve the following four things:
 
First, the characters can be involved in the mundane traffic of the 
steading.  Travellers passing through, disputes among smallfolk that end 
up with witches, trade of goods (including herbs, magical tools, and 
books of spells) with covens, issues within witchy families, the study of 
magic and the unearthing of new spells.
 
Second, characters may be called on to troubleshoot potential 
problems of a magical nature. Old battlefield graveyards where shades 
gather may need to be quieted lest dead things wake.  Children who 
dream of world beyond and may be in danger of becoming beacons for 
beings there.  Monstrosities can appear from the wild to set up nests 
and dens. Places can accumulate magical energy, becoming hearth-like 
(though they may not stabilize into actual hearths), and such places can 
in turn spawn troubling wonders and malicious creatures; these also 
need handling.
  
Third, characters may be called on to help with witchy problems that 
are wildly out of control.  Trouble spots that went hidden or ignored 
for too long can unleash swarms of magical creatures, incursions and 
refugees from beyond the steading can arrive, belief systems that 
villainize the witches themselves can enter steading communities, 
warlords and other authorities may decide to build outposts or greatly 
expand settlement within a steading – all require plenty of hands, witch 
and otherwise.
 
Fourth, witches may spend time visiting one another, or attending 
larger gatherings and actions of witchcraft; some such journeys are best 
with hardy companions.  A coven may need to decide how they can or 
can't relate to the steading next to theirs, where the coven cruelly lords 
itself over the occupants.  They may go to the foundings of new 
hearths, or join in attempts to break the power of a dark forest valley 
ruled by some malevolent spirit.

BASIC REQUIREMENTS
In order to fit this magic system to a game, the rules engine and game 
as a whole will need to already have, or be hacked to add: 
BOONS
Characters will have and acquire boons which, when combined, give 
them the capability to learn spells.  Boons have no scores; you either 
have a boon or do not. Depending on the rules engine you're using, 
they might be positioned as options purchased with points, features of 
a class or other package, options that can be selected upon 'gaining a 
level', or added on as a new thing entirely.
 
♦For character creation: A witch will need to have at least three 
boons (specifically, to be adept in one element, one art, and one 
conduit) in order to be an independent spellcaster.
 
DEGREES OF SUCCESS
Mechanically, when a character casts a spell, there's a basic effect that 
will happen, dangers of the spellcasting that might happen, and some 
enhancements that could be gained (all of which might be assigned by 
the Guide ad-hoc for each casting, or encoded directly into the spells 
when those are created).  The degree of success is then “spent” to 
avoid dangers and potentially gain enhancements.
 
This means that the game's core mechanism needs to be given a means 
to generate such degrees of success, and ideally somewhere in the 
range of 0-4 degrees of success.  
 
♦For example: If resolution is done by rolling a twenty-sided die, 
adding a stat and some bonuses, and comparing that to a difficulty 
number, then, degrees of success could be brought in by saying "Use 
this stat and this bonus; if you beat a difficulty of 10, that's one 
degree of success; each five full points you beat it by adds another 
degree of success".
DOWNTIME 
A game that's always driving hard into new scenarios and missions, 
without time for characters to visit home, engage in crafts, and 
otherwise engage in the daily lives of the characters, will be a relatively 
poor match for this system.  It's possible to imagine a witch that could 
maintain such a lifestyle within what's given here, but making that the 
only option would be cutting off a good deal of what makes these rules 
worth using at all.
11


THE DANGERS
The various parts of the spell-making setup (the elements, arts, and 
conduits) each list a number of dangers that are associated with their 
respective use.  Examples are given, but mechanics are not, since these 
will vary by system.
 
Some of these dangers will be handled entirely by Guide narration, but 
others may need to be assigned specific mechanical meanings in your 
rules engine.  Here's a list of the dangers mentioned; consider for each 
if you'll usually narrate its effect or add mechanics to it (and possibly 
list off what kinds of mechanics you might use, if so):
♦Affliction: Things like being tangled, sickened, cursed, blinded or 
even physically transformed.
♦Delay: Actions taking up more time than one might expect, or even 
'hanging' indefinitely until something else is done to fix the delay. 
♦Displacement: Things ending up where they shouldn't, including 
effects going astray.
♦Expense: Resources (usually items) being used up.
♦Helplessness: The spellcaster being rendered heavily entangled, 
paralyzed, unconscious, or comatose.
♦Injury: General damage, sometimes of a broad type (burning, etc) 
being done to the spellcaster or their allies.
♦Peril: Entirely new threats being generated or attracted (like lighting 
the building you're in on fire).
♦Strangeness: Odd effects, often unsettling or confusing to onlookers, 
which can complicate the situation at hand.
♦Weariness: General fatigue and exhaustion.
12


 
 
AUGMENTATION
If all dangers are cancelled, or the witch considers those present to be 
entirely acceptable, they may instead choose to spend degrees of 
success augmenting the spell they've cast, after checking in with the 
Guide for “can I make this more [whatever]?”.  Some ways this might 
be done:
 
♦Power: The spell deals more damage or is otherwise stronger.
♦Duration: The spell lasts longer.
♦Area: The spell affects a larger area.
♦Precision: The targeting of the spell is more precise than usual.
♦Drama: The spell creates strangeness or a sort that gives it style or 
flair; this doesn't linger long.

CASTING EXAMPLE
Telwick is casting the bonfire reborn, which changes a pinch of ashes 
from a bonfire back into the flames of that bonfire for a few moments; 
the ashes are flung at a target as they explode into flame.  It's a fire-
conjuring taglock, for those who've read ahead.  The Guide assigns the 
following dangers:
♦Injury: Telwick could easily burn himself.
♦Displacement: The explosion of fire might not actually land where 
Telwick's trying to throw it.
♦Peril: There's loads of stuff that might catch fire on the scene, 
creating problems for Telwick and friends.
♦Strangeness: The ashes Telwick is using are from a bonfire there was 
lots of singing and dancing around; as the fire explodes, everyone 
will hear that singing and see dancers in the fire.
Telwick's player does resolution and gets four degrees of success, which 
is top-notch.  Telwick's player then uses two of those degrees of success 
to avoid the injury and the displacement, deciding to leave the peril 
and strangeness – and asks to make the spell larger and more dramatic, 
so fiery dancers will erupt from the ashes, bounding around the 
flammable areas of the scene and setting them ablaze while also striking 
out and burning enemies in the area.
 
The Guide approves these augmentations, and the spell is cast.

BOONS AND PROGRESS
On their way toward becoming a witch, a character will gain the 
following two boons. A useful general rule is that a character is either 
born with natural magic and develops a witch talent early on, or must 
become an apprentice witch, with heavy exposure to magic and 
instruction, in order to gain one.
♦Witch Talent: You are able to cast a single spell, created with the 
Guide (as detailed later on in this section).  This spell follows all the 
usual rules, and is associated with a specific art, requires resolutions, 
and has dangers, all as usual.  If you are not an apprentice witch, it is 
likely that you do not fully understand this spell or it's workings, and 
may be disturbed by it, have a whole collection of other 
(unworkable) spells it is buried among, or some other such confusion. 
♦Apprentice Witch: You are under instruction as a witch, and 
beginning to gain an understanding of witchcraft.  As such, you have 
access of some kind to an experienced spellcaster and their 
resources, and will likely develop a witch talent fairly soon if you do 
not already have one.
At some point, a character receiving instruction as a witch will make a 
breakthrough (that is, they will be entitled to pick an added boon), and 
will trade in both of the above boons and their new choice to gain each 
of the following boons.  When this happens, they will specify the art, 
element, and conduit tied to their witch talent – so a character whose 
witch talent is a fire-conjuring taglock will learn the art of conjuring, 
the element of fire, and the taglock conduit.  Their witch talent spell 
will then become their first known spell, and they will need to redefine 
their relationship with their mentor.
♦Art Adept [Specify]: You can learn spells of the specified art if you 
are also adept with the element and conduit associated with those 
spells.
♦Element Adept [Specify]: You can learn spells of the specified 
element if you are also adept with the art and conduit associated 
with those spells.
♦Conduit Adept [Specify]: You can learn spells of the specified 
conduit if you are also adept with the art and element associated 
with those spells.
Thereafter, the witch may gain new versions of those same three boons, 
specifying new arts, elements, and conduits; whenever they gain a new 
boon, they should also gain a single spell immediately (the magic they 
practised with to gain the new boon).  They may also learn new spells 
based on their collection of boons; more on that later.

TWELVE ELEMENTS
Elements are often described as primal (earth, air, fire, water) or 
complex (everything else), or referred to as domains, dominions, or by 
other names.  By whatever name, there are twelve such.
 
A spell's element is the thing it has a magical effect on, even if it passes 
through a person to do so.  A potion that makes you resistant to fire 
when drunk is a fire-warding brew, because the fire is the target of the 
warding; your involvement is secondary.

SPELL CREATION
To create a spell, Guide and player together lay out an art, element, 
and conduit, reading their components as:
Element-Art Conduit
Thus, you might have a Beast-Dominating Taglock, or an Air-Infusing 
Circle, or a Dream-Divining Tool.  Read over the full description of 
these pieces, as found in the next few sections, and consider what sorts 
of things this might result in.  Potentially, brainstorm a number of such 
effects.
 
Next, consider and discuss which effects might be useful to the 
character, and which they would spend time and energy to research.  
Once you have a particular spell in mind, it's time to lay down some 
specifics.
 
A well-written spell is generally clear, whether explicitly or implicitly 
about how it is cast, about what it targets and how, about what it does 
to those targets, and about how long it lasts.  It may also specify the 
ways it is dangerous to cast, especially if it is always dangerous to do so.
 
A spell can be written as working at only a very specific scale or as 
something that can be cast larger or smaller as desired, if the Guide 
approves.  Overall, combative spells should be restrained to a narrow 
focus; this is because setting dangers and figuring effects with variable 
spells takes longer and keeping the pace moving in situations like this is 
critical.
 
Should you be stumped, reference the list of existing spells for 
inspiration on what's possible and to get a very rough idea of how 
strong effects should generally be made.
 
For example, a dream-warding circle might be:
 
♦A ring of iron-heavy stones all around a steading, made to protect 
against faerie influence within.
 
♦A round sigil drawn on the forehead to block mental influence.
 
♦A line drawn around a bed to keep nightmares at bay so long as the 
caster watches over the sleeper and maintains the spell.
 
♦A circular device engraved on a round shield, so that the shield can 
defend against faerie and illusory weapons.

EXAMPLE: BREWING FIRE
Dayna the witch is adept with brews, with fire, and with the arts of 
conjuring, infusing, and warding; she needs three starting spells.  Right 
off, we know they're all going to be about fire, and they're all going to 
be brews, because those are the only element and conduit she has.  The 
player sits down with the Guide to talk it over.
 
Dayna's player and the Guide decide to make the first spell a Flame-
Conjuring Brew, which seems straightforward.  
 
So, this is going to be some kind of compound made in a cauldron that 
reacts to use by making flames, light, or heat. The obvious "adventure" 
choice here is something that can be thrown and ignites on impact, 
useful as a weapon or a firestarter. But Dayna's player gets a little more 
creative, and goes with light instead of fire - a paint that glows when 
exposed to air. 
 
After a little discussion with the Guide, this spell takes shape, and is 
noted down as Firefly Salve; a bottle is as big as a fist.  The paint within 
such a bottle glows for a whole night after opening - Dayna can use the 
bottle itself as a light source until it's mostly empty. It's not exactly safe 
to brew, though; flashes of radiance thrown off during the process can 
blind the maker for days or burn them badly.
 
Next, it seems like a a Flame-Warding Brew would be handy; Dayna's 
player would like this to be a potion that when drunk makes the 
drinker resistant to heat. The Guide and player talk about this for a 
while, with the Guide puzzling out a degree of balance between how 
long it could last, how strong it could be, and how dangerous to make. 
Dayn's player, considering the options, goes for one extreme end - it 
lasts only a few minutes, but is very strong indeed, and moderately 
dangerous to make.
 
The result is Dragonscale Beads; a user crushes one between thumb and 
finger and sharply inhales the vapour. This renders them almost 
completely immune to burns for just a few minutes. Brewing it involves 
intense heat, and can curse the creator with an inverted effect, a strong 
and weeks-long vulnerability to fire.
 
Finally, Dayna's player decides something combative would be good, 
and decides that a Flame-Infusing Brew might be interesting for this 
purpose - something in the "flaming sword" category is what she has in 
mind. She talks this over with the Guide, who lists out some of the 
properties of fire that she might imbue into a weapon; they settle on 
the spell taking the form of a piece of oil-caked cloth. When a metal 
object that was made from molten metal is rubbed with the cloth 
quickly, the cloth dissolves and the item slowly "wakes up" to forging 
heat before cooling again - a process that often warps and ruins it, if a 
blade or fine-tuned item of any sort.
 
Dayna's player details these spells in her spellbook, and that's that.

GAINING SPELLS
A witch can teach spells that they know to another witch directly with 
one-on-one instruction, so long as the other is adept in the element, 
art, and conduit. This requires a full day of personal instruction for 
each spell taught, and ideally (though not absolutely necessarily) at least 
one actual casting of the spell.
 
Spells can also be codified, written out as instructions with notes on 
things to be careful of, ways to control for them, and so on.  The 
actual instructions could be put on a single page, but the annotations 
often make a single spell into a small book or a chapter in one. 
Learning a spell from a book generally requires three days of careful 
rereading and performing small exercises towards the actual act.
 
The first time a witch casts a spell, the Guide should add in at least two 
dangers -  or, if the witch has been shown the spell being cast in-person, 
one additional danger.
A witch can receive instruction in a spell that they only have two of the 
element, art, and conduit for, and use that instruction as practise; when 
they gain the final required boon, that becomes their new spell gained.
ENTIRELY NEW SPELLS
Completely new spells enter the canon of witchcraft by one of three 
means:
 
♦By Talent: Whether naturally or as an outgrowth of early training in 
withcraft, new witches develop a talent which becomes a spell when 
they become a full witch – these are often entirely new spells, which 
can then be taught and passed around.
 
♦By Extraction: Ceremonies invented by and performed by non-
witches often 'drift' into containing some minor magic.  Participation 
in and refinement of those activities can lead to the unearthing of 
new spells – often rites, but not always.
 
♦By Hearth: Deep study of a wondrous feature at a hearth, or of the 
powers of a hearth's genius, often over the course of years, can 
result in the formulation of new spells.  In some cases, where a 
strong genius is willing to work with witches as equals (or where 
witches are willing to humble themselves to a spirit), this can result in 
massive expansions of witch lore.

THE ELEMENTS
PRIMAL AND COMPLEX
20


 
 
As noted earlier, elements are often described as primal (earth, air, fire, 
water) or complex (everything else). Plant is sometimes included among 
primal elements as wood, and other configurations of the elements 
might also have metal as a primal element.  In some traditions of magic, 
these elements are called domains, dominions, spheres, schools, or 
other such.  The term 'element' was chosen here as the main term of 
use because it's less likely to lead to any confusion regarding which 
thing is the element, the art, or the conduit.
BASIC MEASURES
Each element gives a 'basic measure' intended to give some sense of the 
default scale for the element.  A spell that affects this amount of the 
element is, all else being equal, likely a fairly safe spell – this is the scale 
for “daily use” spells where the caster can't afford a lot of weird side 
effects.  Fearsome combat spells, great rites, and epic workings will 
often go far beyond this, but will begin accumulating dangers much 
stronger than mere inconvenience as they do so. 
THE DELIBERATE ABSENCE
Regular users of various magic systems may note that there's no general 
“mind” element – instead, there's dream, which approaches matters of 
the mind but does so indirectly.  This is very much a deliberate choice, 
and it's set up that way so that things like mind control would need to 
be added in for games where they're fitting, rather than taken out for 
games where they're not – there's plenty of horror to be found in 
dream-dominating magic without hitting at character agency directly.
RECONFIGURING THE ELEMENTS
The twelve-element scheme as exemplified throughout this section is by 
no means the only way that domains of stuff could be categorized by a 
magical tradition, and there's potentially room for further elements to 
be added to what's given here.
 
Codifying an element requires:
 
♦A fairly clear description of what is and isn't included in the element. 
 
♦A basic measure, as above.
 
♦A list of possible dangers that can occur when use of the element 
goes sideways, and what those might look like in the fiction.
 
♦Associations, for use in adding further fiction to 'how a spell is cast', 
for thinking up tools, brew ingredients, and so on.

ELEMENTAL NOTES


 
 
Spells of air affect weather, instill speed and lightness, and can 
manipulate the air into acting as an invisible force.  The element of air 
includes vapours and gases, including mine and marsh gas, smoke and 
fog.
 
The typical scale of an air spell is an amount of vapour roughly large 
enough to chokingly fill a single room or hut; a cube about ten feet to 
a side. The risk increases each time this volume is octupled (such as by 
making it twice as tall, twice as wide, and twice as long).  Trying to 
conjure a full-sized thunderstorm from a cloudless sky could kill a 
solitary caster (major weather-workings are generally done as rites).
 
Air spells often cause unintended localized changes in air currents and 
do not always operate at speed; trouble with air magic can mean:
 
♦Weather that's out of season for the next few days (strangeness).
 
♦Notable delays before the effect occurs (delay)
 
♦'Wandering' effects that go where they shouldn't (displacement).

Spells of dreaming include spells of sleep, illusions, glamours, and 
mundane dreams. While some dream-witches think of themselves 
primarily as illusionists, others maintain a network of contact across 
nights and great distances for times of need.  Dreaming realms are also 
how forces from beyond reality often enter the world; it is dream-
casters who fight them first.
 
The typical dream spell targets or handles a single dream or illusion, 
lasting only a few minutes (about five). Each time this duration or 
number of targets is doubled, the risks of the spell increase.
 
Dreams are not minds, but can provoke or spur them.  Conjuring up 
dreams into an animal can slowly expand their sapience, and conjuring 
dreams into animated items (and corpses) can spur their acquiring 
something at least like a mind.
 
Dream spells gone awry can:
 
♦Place the caster in a deep, extended sleep (helplessness).
 
♦Attract the attentions of the beings of the dream realms (peril)
 
♦Plague the caster with some form of illusory problem only they can 
sense (affliction).

♦Objects: Blanket, blindfold, pillows and stand-ins, objects placed over

♦Environs: Dim, safe, comfortable spaces.

advertised by covens, though the ability to help find good mining sites

The typical scale of an earth spell varies, by mass, by how heavily 
worked the subject is, and roughly according to how rare the material 
is. A simple spell would be one affecting two or three buckets worth of

♦Cause rumblings or disturbances in the area of effect and beyond

Spells of fire can affect heat and light as well as actual flames and 
embers. 
 
The basic measure of a fire spell is about as much heat, light, or flame 
as a torch puts off.  This need not be worked with in that volume, 
however; it could mean a shower of tiny embers, or a few degrees of 
temperature change over a wide area.  Doubling the measure increases 
the danger of the spell and yields what a pot-bellied iron stove might 
generate.  Further doublings can work whole bonfires, or beyond to the 
amount of flame put off by a burning hut.
 
Fire is not a forgiving element; poor results with it are often dangerous. 
Problems with fire magic can:
 
♦Burn or freeze the caster, or spawn unintended additional cold or 
heat effects around the casting that harm (injury) 
 
♦Destroy goods and shelter, blasting them to ash (expense)
 
♦Start conflagrations that burn out of control (peril)

♦Actions: Igniting or extinguishing something.  Hissing, rapidly and

♦Objects: Actual fires, ashes, firewood, coal, firestarting tools,

♦Environs: Anywhere a fire or campfire size or larger is prepared,

The element of hearths governs sacred and magical sites. A hearth 
might...  
  
...Have a heart, a centre where one element of magic is slightly easier 
or safer to use.
 
...Have a hedge, a boundary within which all magic done by anyone 
that's 'not welcome' or 'not attuned' is more dangerous.
 
...Have a wonder, a magical feature, such as a fire that never goes out, 
or a pool which aids in healing, or similar, often within or adjacent to 
the heart.
 
...Have a genius, an intelligence able to cast minor magic in the area 
without apparent risk.
  
When a spell is cast on a hearth, it typically targets only one of these 
features, and alters it only very slightly; significant changes to these are 
wildly dangerous.  
 
Hearth spells often siphon energies away to the hearth (weariness and 
injury), and strong spells of this sort do so at extreme levels.  Instant 
death is the usual result of attempting overly-ambitious heath magic.

A name, in the sense embraced by witches, is a reputed persona. Most 
people have extremely weak names in this sense; it's primarily nobility 
and royalty that have strong names. 
 
For many heroic figures, the creation of a name that will live on 
beyond them is of great importance. Bards and heralds are, from this 
view, professionals in the field.
 
The basic measure of a name is a mild positive or negative belief about 
one person; affecting stronger beliefs or more people increases the risks 
of a mis-cast.
 
Name spells with troubles sometimes:
 
♦Drive rumour about the caster or perceptions of them, often as 
“They desperately want [whatever the magic does]” (affliction).
 
♦Confuse the identities of the caster and the target in the minds of 
strangers (affliction).
 
♦Make the identity of the caster, and many of their deeds (especially 
secret ones) immediately apparent to any onlooker (affliction).

ASSOCIATIONS
 
♦Actions: Speech and writing, including botting it out or mumbling it 
deliberately; stamping or playing in rhythm with a casting or story.
 
♦Objects: Pens, paper, engraving and recording objects, musical

♦Environs: In crowds and any edge-of-crowd spaces (entryways and

Sometimes referred to as the element of wood, the plant element 
includes plants from lichen to great oaks, as well as material that is 
directly derived from plants such as paper and sap.
 
The basic measure of plant material handled by such a spell is about 
enough to fill a wheelbarrow; a sapling can thus be manipulated with 
little danger of the spell going badly awry.  Each doubling of this mass 
increases the risks of trouble with the magic.
 
Plant spells gone wrong can:
 
♦Start working very slowly or after a pause (delay). 
 
♦Rip power from and blight nearby untargeted plants (strangeness or 
expense).
 
♦Affect the caster, giving them wooden fingernails and greening their 
skin (affliction), or even causing thorns to grow out from their body 
(injury).

ASSOCIATIONS

♦Objects: Farming, gardening, and arbory tools; sickles, hoes, rakes,

♦Environs: Heavily overgrown areas and natural barrens; wild places

Shades are the lingering memories or spirits of the dead; to what extent 
they are 'souls' may or may not be known, as the Guide rules. Shades 
are normally visible only as a slight bend in dim light, audible only as a 
whisper. They are no more substantial than fog. Shades can be 
conjured from and banished to limbo; while there, they can't act or be 
affected except by conjuring them up.
 
Some shades possess powers, such as possession of the living and access 
to dreams. These powers express the passions of a shade; a shade can 
only use them for reasons they truly care about.  This helps many, but 
poses a strong problem in trying to compel use of such powers. 
 
A shade spell can easily affect one shade for few hours; doubling the 
number of subject or duration generally increases the risks.  
 
Shade spells that go awry can:
 
♦Anger local shades, drawing them to harass the caster (peril). 
 
♦Shroud the caster with the 'feel' of death, which others may react to 
with disgust, fear, or even violence (affliction).
 
♦Warp the area so shades are stronger in it, making it 'haunted' 
(strangeness).

ASSOCIATIONS
 
♦Actions: Whispering, light breathing, putting out lights, destruction of 
vessels containing spell material.
 
♦Objects: Bones, skulls, other remains.  Coloured clothing, with colour

mori or trappings of death (hourglasses, scythes, etc).   
 
♦Environs: Midnight. Graveyards, etc.  Shrines to the dead, including

A wyrd is a troubling fate, curse, or hex that waits for a person, a 
group, or an institution. A given subject can be affected by several 
wyrds - There can be dooms that wait for you, your family, your coven, 
and so on, all of which affect you.  A wyrd is always phrased as a 
simple negative.
 
A wyrd affects the stakes the Guide sets for actions. If your wyrd was 
to kill those you love, spells or actions you took that could be given the 
danger of harming such targets would be (this might as a consequence 
of failure or addendum in non-magical resolution, as fits the rules 
engine).  A basic wyrd spell affects one target, at the scale of adding or 
removing one such stakes-setting rule, and lasting in a month.
 
Wyrd spells most often go wrong by "splashing" the wyrd onto the 
caster, their allies or hearth, or by creating some kind of wyrd 
elsewhere (strangeness).
WYRD
ASSOCIATIONS
 
♦Actions: Spitting, actively cursing out loud, small violent gestures or 
suppression or blocking of them.  Flicking one hand with the other 
could be conjuring or warding, depending which hand 'matters'. 
 
♦Objects: Symbolic weapons and ill-fate representations. Needles, 
lumps of dry dung, defaced coins, and so on.
 
♦Environs: Spaces that have been defiled (salted-over farmland, for 
example) or redeemed from such defilement.


 
 
THE ARTS
EXPRESSED INTENT
34


 
 
Arts are how you tell magic what to do with an element – make it, 
destroy it, keep it back, etc.  Magical traditions that give magic an 
academic feel often centre on arts as the most abstracted and 'highest' 
component of magic, identifying practitioners as conjurers, banishers, 
dominators, diviners, infusers, and warders.
DURATIONS
Each art notes whether spells of that art tend to have a duration or not. 
Banishings, for example, rarely have durations, while conjurations 
almost always do.  Duration can be measured by breaths, days, weeks, 
or more esoteric qualifies like:
 
♦Until the next sunrise / moonrise. 
 
♦Until the next new/crescent/half/gibbous/full moon.
 
♦Until kissed by a member of the ruling bloodline.
 
♦So long as the user never leaves the woods.
ON TRANSFORMATIONS
As it occasionally can prompt questions: Turning one thing into 
another is handled in this system under the art of infusing.  You turn 
into a wolf, in whole or in part, by infusing wolf-ness into yourself; you 
turn lead into gold by infusing gold-ness into lead.
ON “DIRECT DAMAGE”
Again, as it may not be immediately obvious: Direct attack magic is 
often banishment.  A blood-banishing taglock spell might be “Rip out a 
targets' hair and curse their heart to wither”. 
RECONFIGURING THE ARTS
The setup given here could be split up into more verbs or replaced 
with different ones with a bit of work.  There's not as much room to 
add more arts without radically expanding what magic can do, but it's 
possible there are a few more out there that could be put in.
 
Codifying an art requires:
 
♦A fairly clear definition of the kinds of things the art does. 
 
♦A note on duration, as above.
 
♦A list of possible dangers that can occur when use of the element 
goes sideways, and what those might look like in the fiction.
 
♦Some expressions of the art useful for creating or varying spells.
35
ART NOTES


 
 
Banishing spells harm, destroy, dispel, or remove their targets directly.  
This is not an all-or-nothing proposition; a spell meant to break a curse 
may instead weaken it.
 
Banishing spells do not generally have duration; the magic expends 
itself against the target and then is gone.  In some cases, where 
something banished might be restored or recreated quickly, a duration 
may be appropriate; this is usually about an hour (for greater keep-
away, following a banishing up with a warding may be in order).
 
Banishing magic, when it goes astray, can:
 
♦Disrupt the users own magical abilities for a time (affliction).
 
♦Tire the user deeply (weariness)
 
♦Dispel other magic the user is benefiting from (expense).
36
BANISHING
EXPRESSIONS
 
♦Gestural: Motions of attack and of casting off or throwing down; 
banishment gestures are abrupt and often aggressive.
 
♦Verbal: Exhortations to be gone, to wither, die, be snuffed out, 
crumble, and fall.
 
♦Symbolic: Cut cords, broken chains, instruments of cutting and 
destruction.


 
 
37
Conjuring spells create, summon, or call up beings, objects, or forces 
into the world.
 
A conjuring almost always has duration, but this duration is often 
naturally set - a conjured fire given fuel will burn so long as the fuel 
lasts, a conjured storm will spend itself, and so on.  Where no such 
natural limit occurs, a conjuring may occur in a flash (such as if 
conjuring a burst of flame without fuel) or may last until the next 
quarter of the day begins (at dawn, noon, sunset, or midnight). 
Increasing duration beyond this comes with added dangers.
 
Trouble with conjuring magic often manifests as:
 
♦Effects straying outside or beyond the intended limits (displacement).
 
♦Tiring the user deeply (weariness)
 
♦Added effects or entities being called up (peril or strangeness).
CONJURING
EXPRESSIONS
 
♦Gestural: Actions as if catching something out of the air, or inviting 
it to come to the caster.
 
♦Verbal: Calls and invitations; conjuring invocations often range from 
polite request to near-seductive appeals.
 
♦Symbolic: Gateways, objects of welcoming, hospitality, and respect 
or reminders of prior 'visits'. 


 
 
A dominating spell influences or controls a target without changing its 
basic nature.  This can mean directing a fire over time to twist in 
various directions as if blown by the wind; it might mean creating a 
new law of behaviour in an animal such as "people wearing hats are 
friendly until shown otherwise".
 
Dominating spells that allow ongoing control last as long as they are 
focused on exclusively; dominating spells that create rules of behaviour 
have duration.  At base, this is a duration of a hundred breaths (a 
couple of minutes).  An ongoing domination that lasts until the next 
quarter-day or more will have added dangers to cast.
 
Dominating magic that's not quite as intended may:
 
♦Have 'escape clauses' which allow it to be broken by some means that 
will be discovered on divining it (strangeness).
 
♦Be extremely apparent to the target, who will resent it (peril)
 
♦Tire the caster rapidly (weariness).
38
DOMINATING
EXPRESSIONS
 
♦Gestural: Motions of grasping and seizing, often of imagined in visible 
strings or controls.
 
♦Verbal: Imprecations and orders; regardless of language, spoken 
portions of dominating spells sound like giving orders. 
 
♦Symbolic: Clasps, ropes, boxes, cages, and chains; lists of rules and 
demands.


 
 
39
Divining spells are means of learning information. This includes spinners 
that point to a distant subject, bones thrown and cards laid to give an 
overall reading, astrological readings, visions in fires and reflections, and 
many other forms.
 
Ongoing divinations such as visions can last so long as the caster 
focuses on them exclusively.  Divinations which directly answer 
questions have no duration; the power comes and goes in a moment.
 
Troubled or miscast divining magic may:
 
♦Stun the caster or put them in a trance they must recover from, 
potentially even needing help (helplessness).
 
♦Warp one sense of the user so that the desired information is the 
only kind that sense receives, often for several days (affliction).
 
♦Give information that's true now mingled with information that was 
true in the past, will be true in the future, or is poetically but not 
technically true (displacement).
DIVINING
EXPRESSIONS
 
♦Gestural: Most divining gestures interrupt, focus around, or indicate 
sensory input - cover your ears and then uncover to hear the 
changed sounds, and similar. 
 
♦Verbal: Divinatory incantations tend to be searching and 
meandering, while asking magic show or reveal what is desired.
 
♦Symbolic: Signs of eyes and ears, and objects made to resemble such, 
as well as maps, books of names, and similar references.


 
 
Spells that infuse add some of the essential nature of the element 
involved to their target.  This transformation can be mild and hard for 
others to notice, such as making a target smell like a friend to mice, or 
severe and obvious, such as turning a target into a mouse. Infusions can 
be very strange; a wyrd-infusion, for example, can make the subject the 
embodiment of a doom meant for someone else.
 
Infusing spells have durations.  At base, this is a duration of a hundred 
breaths (a couple of minutes).  A infusion that lasts until the next 
sunrise or more will have added dangers to cast.
 
Infusing magic can all too easily:
 
♦Infuse qualities other than those intended into the target or into the 
caster (affliction).
 
♦Affect the caster rather than a distinct target (displacement).
 
♦Deal damage to a subject while warping their nature (injury).
40
INFUSING
EXPRESSIONS
 
♦Gestural: Motions of putting something on, or of pressing or rubbing 
something into a surface.
 
♦Verbal: When infusing, incantations tend to sound like attempts to 
soothe the magic, calming it and convincing it to take up its place.
 
♦Symbolic: Things-within-things, such as an elementally-appropriate 
item being pushed into a cloth and tied off inside.


 
 
41
Warding spells guard against a sphere of activity or source of harm.  
This mitigation is often partial, rather than absolute; if you held an 
effigy made to ward away steel (an earth-warding poppet), a sword 
might bruise rather than pierce.
 
Warding spells have durations. At base, they last until the next quarter 
of the day begins (at dawn, noon, sunset, or midnight). Increasing 
duration beyond this comes with added dangers.
 
Miscasts when warding can:
 
♦Deflect damages and effects to other persons or goods tied to the 
casting in unintended ways (expense).
 
♦Interfere with the ability of the subject to express effects of the same 
sort they're being guarded against (affliction)
 
♦”Guard” the subject from non-harmful phenomena that are 
somewhat similar to the intended effects (affliction or strangeness).
WARDING
EXPRESSIONS
 
♦Gestural: Motions to stop, hold back, or movements as drawing of 
lines, boundaries, or barriers in the air or on a target.
 
♦Verbal: Warding invocations have the caster telling the magic to 
stand firm, hold fast, be strong; rallying and inspiring of magic.
 
♦Symbolic: Any physical defence or sign of one, whether militant (as 
in a knight's shield), mundane (as in an apron), or otherwise.


 
 
THE CONDUITS
MYSTIC CHANNELS
42


 
 
Conduits are the central focuses that magic goes through in order to 
enact your art upon an element.  In some settings, each conduit very 
nearly has it's own tradition of magic, with alchemical brewers entirely 
distinct from tool-making artificers and rite-leading priests.
TARGETING
Conduits are used as the rules structure for targeting of effects – circles 
target what's inside them, brews what they contact in set ways, poppets 
what they resemble, taglocks what they sample, and tools what they are 
used on.  Rites are the catch-all for “any target”.
MULTIPLE CONDUITS
It's entirely possible for a spell, or the casting of one, to technically 
include multiple conduits simultaneously.  If a poppet-doll is woven 
from the hair of the target it resembles, that spell could be a poppet or 
taglock spell.  Where such spells exist, the Guide can determine if they 
can be learned by those adept in either conduit, only by those adept in 
one, or if both are required – and if both are not required, does 
possession of the other give some benefit?  If the rules engine operates 
with larger numbers (like rolling a twenty-sided die), a bonus for having 
both (or all, or whatever) might be easy and natural to add in, where if 
it operates on small numbers (like rolling a four-sided die), it might not.
 
RECONFIGURING THE CONDUITS
Conduits are the components most likely to vary heavily, get added to 
or removed from, if moving away from the Euro-witchcraft feel being 
forwarded here.  Magic that's primarily focused by songs, by sigils, by 
architecture – there's loads of easy additions and alterations to make; 
the only real limiter is trying not to have ones that overlap too much.
 
Codifying a conduit requires:
 
♦A fairly clear definition of what the conduit is and what kinds of 
specific forms it can take. 
 
♦A note on targeting, as above.
 
♦A list of possible dangers that can occur when use of the element 
goes sideways, and what those might look like in the fiction.
 
♦Some discussion of compositions, and how they include associations 
and expression – cauldrons and ingredients for a brew, colours and 
materials for a circle, and so on.
43
CONDUIT NOTES


 
 
A brew is a compound that carries a magical effect. Brews are created 
in liquid form, but may 'finalized' by being dried, powdered, or baked. 
Each brew takes a full day of work to create, and can be stored unused 
for a week.  The roll to create a brew is made at the end of the 
workday.
 
Brews are always consumed by use, though not always ingested - a 
poison smeared on a dagger is consumed when a foe is stabbed; an ink 
that's written with is consumed, a powder thrown into a fire is 
consumed.
 
Dangers may apply to the creator at the time of brewing (being 
poisoned by your ingredients), or to the user (a potion that exhausts 
the drinker); a mix is common. Any brewer will know generally what 
flaws exist in the brew just created.  
 
The most common dangers of brewing itself are:
 
♦Damage to the brewer from exposure to vapours (injury).
 
♦Extended time needed for the brewing process (delay).
 
44
BREW
COMPOSITION
Brews can include associations and expressions as part of the makeup 
of the brewing vessel or later containers (boil it in a stone pot, store it 
in a glass flask), as part of the heat source (over a fire of rowan logs), as 
part of the ingredients (ash from a great bonfire), or as actions taken 
over the brew in preparation (chant this phrase while bubbling).

A circle is a round border with an obvious inside and outside. Circles 
may be created (pouring salt around yourself while turning), engraved, 
or acknowledged where they exist (by moving along their whole 
border). Creation or acknowledgement of a circle requires no roll; only 
the casting does. 
 
If a circle spell is cast that has duration, and the boundary of the circle 
is broken during that duration, the spell ends. Equally, if the caster quits 
the circle (exiting if within, stepping more than a few paces from the 
edge if without), the spell ends. A spell cast using a circle may affect all 
valid targets inside of it, or some subset as appropriate.
 
Circle magic has relatively light risks comparatively, but can involve:
 
♦'Edge effects' where the border of the circle itself becomes 
troublesome in minor ways (strangeness).
 
♦Added risks if the character of "the circle" is being stretched (to a 
cross-section of a pole, the edge of a coin, or similar), often affecting 
the wrong target somehow (displacement, strangeness).
CIRCLE
COMPOSITION
Associations and expressions are usually put into a circle either as part 
of its physical makeup (made from blue chalk, made by pouring out of 
a metal bowl) or during the process of laying out the circle or some 
symbol contained within it (draw the horns of an elk inside the circle, 
make a gesture of banishment as each stone is laid).


 
 
A poppet is a physical representation of a person, creature, or locale - 
such as a doll, effigy, model, statue, or set of clothes. Small, disposable 
doll-like poppets are the most common type. Creating a poppet does 
not usually require resolution, only using one.
 
Once a spell is cast on a specific individual or a class of target (such as 
"all nearby rats") using a poppet, that poppet can only ever be used 
again for that same target. 
 
If a poppet spell harms the subject, the poppet used takes identical or 
analogous harm.  If a poppet spell has a duration, then you must keep 
the poppet with you for that duration.
 
Poppet magic has a few unique dangers:
 
♦The poppet is a weak representation, and the magic targets 
something else (displacement).
 
♦The subject becomes linked to the poppet, able to see what the 
poppet sees or to control it (strangeness).
 
♦A risk of destroying the poppet in spells where such destruction isn't 
already the case (expense).

POPPET
COMPOSITION
Since poppets are often directly representative, symbolic associations 
are often part of the surface of the poppet and material associations 
often used in the makeup, while the expressions of art are more often 
part of the use rather than the creation of the poppet.

A rite is a magical ritual involving one caster and a number of other 
active participants. A rite requires at least ten minutes per participant 
to perform, and takes an hour to instruct each participant on how to 
do the rite the first time.  
 
Rites have no restrictions on targeting; they can potentially affect 
anything.
 
Rites are potentially more dangerous than other spells, but made safer 
by more participants; as a rough rule, the Guide should double the 
number of dangers the spell would incur cast another way, and then 
reduce that by one for each assisting participant, down to half the usual 
number of dangers at best.
 
Ritual magic can go wrong, leading to:
 
♦The rite extending far longer than planned (delay)
 
♦Tiring out all the participants (weariness)
 
♦Backlashes in the vein of the magic used (injury, peril, affliction, etc) 
affecting all participants.
RITE
COMPOSITION
Rites tend to use a limited number of associations and expressions, but 
use them in a coordinated, often repeated, manner.  Group chants and 
near or actual dances are common ways to work invocations and 
gestures into a rite.


 
 
Taglock spells focus their effects by using a sample of their target as a 
channel to that target. While “taglock” properly means a mat of hair, 
this can actually include any direct sample of a given subject, from the 
blood of a targeted person to the air in your lungs when calling the 
wind. This extends a little more; a child is a taglock for their parent, a 
corpse for a shade, a creator for an illusion.  A wolf pelt is a taglock for 
wolfishness (should you wish to become a wolf, for example), but not 
for any specific wolf (you could not dominate a wolf through it).
A taglock typically requires no crafting; if you rip some hair from a 
target, you can generally use it to cast a taglock spell on them. 
 
The common problems to avoid when casting with a taglock:
 
♦Where the magic is violent, it can also unleash magic around the 
taglock itself violent enough to harm the caster (injury).
 
♦A risk of destroying the taglock in spells where such destruction isn't 
already the case (expense).
48
TAGLOCK
COMPOSITION
A taglock itself often is extremely strongly associated with the element; 
where it is not (as in an infusion, where the element differs from the 
target), strongly associated tools are often used to manipulate that 
taglock.  In either case, gestures and other expressions are often 
directed at or using the taglock itself.

A tool spell is cast and rolled for once, on using an appropriate item 
for the spell; the casting takes only moments, but the item is usually 
custom-made to fit the spell.  After this is done, the item itself carries 
the effect, which can be activated without a roll possessing both the 
element and art of the spell. Using a spelled tool always requires that 
the item be brandished, often by touching things symbolically.
 
Dangers played in the casting apply both to the creator at the time of 
creation and to the user at each use, and cancelling them at creation 
also cancels them at use.  Since there is no roll to use, those dangers 
cannot be cancelled at use.  
 
Tool creation rolls are heavily-laden with dangers based on element and 
art, and can also tire the creator and user notably (weariness).  In all 
but the most trivial cases, the number of dangers involved in creating a 
tool will be enough that they cannot all be cancelled out, and the 
creator will simply need to pick and choose among them to decide 
which will become the standard cost for using the item each time.
 
Where associations, expressions, and composition can potentially grant 
any benefit, tool-creators would be advised to take full advantage of 
that; the casting might well end up being far less fraught than the 
preparation for casting.
TOOL
COMPOSITION
Most tools express (or can be used in motion to express) the art that 
they harness.  The element involved might also be fairly clear, but if 
not, then it is often reflected in the materials used for the tool 
(including things like stones in the handle or pommel and the like), 
symbols used for decoration, and so on.

SECOND BREATH
(Air-Conjuring Taglock)

(Air-Infusing Brew)

(Air-Divining Tool)

over that breath, scents would come to the Grey Witch - each scent 
coming in order of how far away it was, and all along the direct line 
she was facing.
 
SHEPHERD OF CLEAR SKIES
(Air-Banishing Rite)
This spell clears the skies. The caster of this spell, Marianne, has long 
used it when bad weather threatens her flock and the pastures have

around her until all have done this, and all then run back away, tearing

(Beast-Banishing Circle)

STEWING STONE
(Beast-Conjuring Brew)
Fist-sized and glassy brown lumps, these 'stones' can be dropped into a

duration (often a few months). Should the people break their promises 
to the beast, the spell end immediately. 
SHAPING THE EGG
(Beast-Infusing Taglock)
This disreputable spell requires an egg of whatever sort, and a taglock 
from some creature. The egg is placed in contact with the taglock, and 
addressed, telling it what features it might learn from the beast the

described features.  The changes are permanent, but are only functional 
and comfortable if well-suited to the anatomy of the creature – a pain-
maddened monstrosity is a common result.

SILVER HANDS

This rite is used on prosthetic limbs, hands, and so on, which are

HALT-BRAID

RESURRECTION CAULDRON

remain a diagram of their body showing the issue. This diagram will 
often be abstracted or fanciful, but clear about the issue regardless.

GLAMOUR COMB

ADDER-STONE

DREAMCATCH SCATTER
(Dream-Warding Taglock)

around where they will lay their head, using these as a taglock to guard 
their dreams.  Should any attempt to enter their dreams, these droplets 
burn away and reduce the power of those efforts as they do.  Even if 
this fails, the drops leave tiny burns indicating that were challenged.

(Earth-Banishing Brew)
This fist-sized bag of tarry liquid sinks into, stains black, and weakens 
any stone it is poured onto or splattered across. A stone subjected to 
this treatment becomes much easier to work, but much less able to

FORGE-MOLD
(Earth-Conjuring Circle)
The caster piles stone dust onto a small, circular workbench carved

KNOCK HAMMER
(Earth-Divining Tool)

SOWING STONES
(Earth-Dominating Rite)
This rite is cast by throwing a series of stones across a field. Many

down to a depth of about ten feet.  This rite can be used to curse a 
field briefly, forcing a farmer to pick it clear, but improves the field

SCAR-WELDING
(Earth-Infusing Poppet)
This spell employs a small metal poppet of a person, which is melted

original metal vanishes in this process. This often leaves a terrible scar 
and the pain typically results in the subject screaming their way to 
unconsciousness, but it cleans and removes the wound entirely.
BLOODING THE MONOLITH
(Earth-Warding Taglock)
The caster must bloody their own hand and place it against a block of 
metal or stone; anvils are sometimes used. The caster then asks that 
block to take a blow for them, and the spell is done. For as long as the 
spell lasts, one injury inflicted on the subject by a stone or metal 
weapon can be passed to the block instead.

(Fire-Banishing Circle)

FIRECALLER CRYSTAL
(Fire-Conjuring Tool)
A firecaller crystal focuses light readily as if it were a magnifying glass

(Fire-Divining Taglock)

burning in the colour of the dye, regardless of distance between them.
BLAZE DUST
(Fire-Dominating Brew)

moving their arms and hands.
MIRRORED SUNSETS
(Fire-Infusing Rite)
This ritual is performed on a series of mirrors, all reflecting a sunset 
from one another.  The caster and participants, starting at the mirror 
furthest from the one actually facing the sun, veil the mirrors one by 
one, asking the light to linger with them. Until the next sunrise, the 
mirrors glow softly, providing light throughout the night.
CHAR MANNEQUIN
(Fire-Warding Poppet)
This poppet is most often a log carved in a rough resemblance of the 
subject. The caster addresses it as if it were the subject, which may 
mean talking to it as if it was themself. While speaking, the caster rubs it 
down with ashes, and tells it that fire cannot harm it and will only 
blacken it. For the duration, damage done to the subject by fire will be 
redirected to their equipment so long as they have any - they will not 
burn until they are naked and covered in ash.

GOING FACELESS
(Hearth-Warding Poppet)

BLESSING BOWL

(Water-Conjuring Taglock)
This spell is used on a sealed bottle, vial, or skin of liquid. A pin is 
driven into the seal and told to pour out that which lies within. The 
bottle is then poured entirely out into another vessel, but remains full 
despite this. If a brewed liquid is duplicated, the duplicate will not 
function as the original - the Guide will invent whatever effect they like.
TIDESTAY
(Water-Dominating Brew)
This tiny vial is poured into other liquid with the user naming some 
part or soluble thing in the liquid and demanding it be left behind. The 
liquid is then poured away. The part named will be left behind by the 
liquid. This can be used to keep salt while pouring seawater, to keep all 
that is not iron (the impurities) while pouring liquid metal, and so on - a 
cauldron of seawater can leave behind all sorts of things.
OCEAN WINDOW
(Water-Warding Circle)
This circle is generally drawn on the inside of a hull with a nail, 
encircling a leak, along with demands shouted that the ocean remain 
outside. The circle then stops the passage of water from one side to the 
other briefly - long enough for a patch to be hammered on.
DROPLETS RUN TOGETHER
(Water-Infusing Poppet)
This spell uses two soft clay poppets, which must be almost identical - 
and requires two subject objects or (willing) creatures, also similar and 
of the same material or species. The subjects are placed close 
proximity, and are exhorted to be as one, while the caster joins the two 
poppets together and sculpts a single, larger poppet from them. When 
the magic takes hold, the two subjects also fuse, running together to 
form a new subject of twice the size for as long as the spell lasts -for 
example, turning two hunting dogs into a single enormous hound.
WELL WALKING
(Water-Divining Rite)
Humming and walking in time, celebrants meander about an area 
blindfolded. Slowly, they will begin to circle a spot; this is the best 
place to dig a well in the area.  Note, though; best doesn't mean good, 
just as good as it's going to get.

CASTING OFF
(Wyrd-Banishing Taglock)
This spell exists to weaken or destroy a personal Wyrd. The taglock 
used is the hair of the subject - all their hair, shaved and piled in a 
bowl. The hair is thrown into a fire and incantations spoken over it, 
that the Wyrd may burn away with the hair.
WOLF AT THE DOOR
(Wyrd-Conjuring Circle)
The caster circles a community with a bag of coloured stones, dropping 
one every few steps, and names some doom on it.  This spell alone is 
rarely enough to bring the doom about, but will aid trouble of the sort 
named should it be present or come calling.
SHAKING THE BAG
(Wyrd-Divining Rite)
This rite uses a skin bag filled with stones, about half of which have 
symbols on them and half of which are blank. The bag is thrown hand 
to hand among the participants during a dance. The bag must have at 
least as enough stones every could draw a blank. At the end of the 
dance, each participant draws a stone from the bag; any participant 
with a Wyrd will draw the symbol that best describes that Wyrd.
UNWRITTEN INK
(Wyrd-Warding Brew)
This vial of ink appears empty.  When a pen is dipped inside, that pen 
is inked and the vial actually emptied.  Should the pen-holder then 
write a name with the ink, the name will fade from the page and the 
person named will cease to be subject to Wyrds of all sorts for a time.
WHEEL OF THORNS
(Wyrd-Dominating Tool)
This tattoo is blazoned on the palm; it shows eight different pains that 
might be suffered (poverty, illness, burning, drowning, and so on). By 
activating the magic in it, touching a 'thorn', and pressing it to the 
forehead of a target, it changes their existing wyrd to the one chosen 
for a time.  This can be used to turn positive wyrds into cursed ones, or 
to control a cursed wyrd temporarily while preparing to remove it.
HARBINGERS PANOPLY
(Wyrd-Infusing Poppet)
This poppet is a full and very obvious costume fashioned to indicate 
some terrible trouble; the wearer is dressed as death, war, or similar; 
the spell is cast when it is put on.  Should the wearer encounter any 
person or group whose wyrd is the one the costume indicates, the 
wearer will find the magic aiding them in all attempts to carry out that 
wyrd, assuming they have the means.
