It's Only Magic
I
’ve spent my entire life thinking about 
how amazing it would be if magic 
were real, a known part of the world I 
live in, and something that I was able 
to use. I think every fan of the fantasy 
genre has, at some point, thought the 
very same thing. Magic is awesome. 
Magic being real would be awesome. 
Being able to use magic to deal with 
the annoyances, inconveniences, and 
problems in life would be awesome. 
And yes, using magic just for fun would 
be awesome.
Fantasy fans have been lucky enough 
to get so many great modern fantasy 
stories, television shows, and films that 
give us magical worlds to escape to, 
spark our imaginations, and provide 
fodder for fan fiction and roleplaying 
campaigns. Maybe your favorite story 
is where a misfit group of high school 
students fight vampires, demons, 
and other threats. Or vampires, 
shapeshifters, and faeries reveal their 
existence to the world and claim equal 
rights as people. Or two brothers 
travel the country hunting demons and 
supernatural evil creatures. Or the main 
characters study magic at a prestigious 
university while uncovering a secret 
threat. Or the shopkeeper angel and 
the serpent from the garden team up to 
stop the Antichrist. Or entities like death 
and dream are personified and interact 
with mortals. Or a trio of witch sisters 
tries to balance their personal lives 
and supernatural threats. Or teenagers 
discover their magical heritage and learn 
to navigate a secret world of magic. Or 
immortals chase each other with swords, 
hoping to claim a mysterious prize. Or 
dozens of other worlds that I haven’t 
read or even heard of.
Regardless of the specifics, they’re 
all about people using magic in a 
world that’s otherwise the same as 
our twentieth- or twenty-first-century 
life. Cell phones. Automobiles. Africa, 
Europe, and the Americas. As real and 
familiar as the world we grew up in, but 
with the benefits, joys, and hazards of 
actual magic.
That’s what It’s Only Magic is all 
about. In these pages are the tools, 
sets, plots, and character concepts you 
need to run a Cypher System game 
in your favorite modern fantasy story, 
or use The Axial—the cozy witchcore 
coffee shop setting we created for this 
book—or even create your own modern 
magical world. Use your imagination, 
be inspired, and dive deep into that 
familiar old feeling of the real world 
made more awesome . . . with magic.

INTRODUCTION
Cozy fantasy is a fantasy 
genre that focuses on 
smaller communities 
and close relationships, 
as opposed to traditional 
high fantasy, which 
tends to be about 
epic adventures and 
saving the world.
Witchcore is a fashion 
and home décor aesthetic 
centered around witchy 
themes, often involving 
magic, herbs, and gems.

CHAPTER 1: MODERN FANTASY
TECHNO-MAGIC
Magic is as well known as technology 
and may be as common as technology, 
and mixing the two is possible. While one 
person commutes to work on a motorcycle, 
their co-worker gets there on a flying broom,
and their boss shows up in a limousine-like
vehicle powered by an air elemental. 
Regular people may have access to magic 
created by others, but they may not be able 
to directly use it themselves—much like 
how most people have a mobile phone or a 
computer, but wouldn’t know how to build 
one or understand how they work. The PCs 
in this kind of setting might know how 
to use magic, technology, or a little bit of 
both. Examples: Bright, Cast a Deadly Spell, 
Howl’s Moving Castle, The Legend of Korra, 
the Marvel Cinematic Universe, 
On a Pale Horse, Onward, 
Shadowrun RPG.
MONSTERS ARE REAL
Inhuman things of various kinds are real—
and are usually a threat to the normal 
people of the world. In these stories, the 
PCs typically are humans trying to keep 
these monsters at bay. Often, the premise 
of the setting is that one specific kind of 
monster is real (demons, vampires, ghosts, 
and so on), but other kinds (werewolves, 
zombies, mummies, and so on) are 
fictional . . . although that assumption may 
prove to be wrong as well. Examples: Anita 
Blake: Vampire Hunter series, Being Human, 
Buffy the Vampire Slayer, Dark Shadows, 
Fablehaven, Forever Knight, Good Omens, 
Grimm, Hellblazer/Constantine, Hellboy, 
Night Watch, True Blood, Twin Peaks.
SECRETLY MAGICAL WORLD
Magic and the supernatural are real, 
but most regular people don’t know it 
and go through life thinking they’re in a 
nonmagical world. The PCs often start 
out by discovering the existence of magic, 
then explore the nuances of how the world 
actually works, all the while keeping the 
secret. Examples: A Dirty Job, American 
Gods, Anansi Boys, Bewitched, Charmed, 
Chilling Adventures of Sabrina, The Dresden 
Files series, Fablehaven, Good Omens, 
Highlander, I Dream of Jeannie, Imajica, 
The Magicians, Sabrina the Teenage Witch, 
Sandman, The Spiderwick Chronicles.
USING THIS BOOK
It’s Only Magic is a companion 
to the Cypher System Rulebook. 
This means you won’t find 
an introduction to the Cypher 
System, how to create 
characters, full rules of the 
game, or other related concepts 
here. It’s Only Magic assumes 
you’ve got all that in the 
Cypher System Rulebook, and 
that you’re ready to dive right 
into fantasy‑specific content.
MODERN FANTASY GENRES
There are many kinds of stories you can tell (and games you can run) 
under the broad umbrella of “modern fantasy.” Television shows such as 
Buffy the Vampire Slayer, Supernatural, True Blood, Charmed, The Magicians, 
and Sabrina the Teenage Witch are all part of the modern fantasy genre, 
but each has its own structure and expectations for different aspects of 
a modern fantasy world—like whether magic is real, whether monsters 
walk among us, whether regular humans can learn magic, and whether 
other worlds or dimensions exist. It can be helpful to think of these as 
subgenres of modern fantasy, especially when considering the sort of 
game you want to run as a GM or play in as a player.
The following is an overview of many typical modern fantasy genres, 
whether from television, film, novels, graphic novels, or other sources. 
Each entry has a brief explanation of the genre’s key ideas and a few 
examples of popular media that fit that genre. Note that these categories 
are broad, and there’s often overlap between them. Use the categories 
to help define the concept of the setting you’re creating, but don’t let them confine 
your creativity.


It's Only Magic
It’s rewarding to come up with your own 
modern fantasy setting. It can also be a 
challenge to start from scratch. This section 
helps you think about important topics 
and questions relating to the setting, and 
can give you a jumping-off point for when 
you’re stuck in the creative process. Even if 
you’re experienced at worldbuilding, these 
guidelines can help fill in some details, 
create new twists, and approach the topic 
from different angles to get your creative 
juices flowing.
Near the end of this chapter is a section 
about advising players about the details of 
your setting; that section includes a lot of 
example answers to the questions here. If 
you’re stuck on a question that intrigues 
you but can’t think of a good answer for 
your campaign, look to that section for 
inspiration and ideas.
There are quite a few topics here—enough 
that it might get overwhelming! It’s okay to 
start with two or three of them (probably 
World, Magic, and Player Characters) 
and make those the core of your setting 
concept. Tackle the other topics as needed, 
in whatever order you need—and it’s fine if 
some topics never get a firm answer.

CHAPTER 2: BUILDING YOUR MODERN FANTASY SETTING
World: Is the world basically Earth with 
magic, or is it a different world entirely? If 
it’s on Earth, does the story take place in the 
present day? Or is it set in the recent past? 
If a world other than Earth, is it similar to 
modern Earth, with the equivalent of cars 
and mobile phones? Or more like a recent 
historical period, such as the Cold War or 
the Roaring Twenties?
Starting Location: Where does the campaign 
start? Is it a small town, a big city, or a rural 
area? For example, setting the game in a 
big city means the entire campaign could 
take place in various parts of the city, but 
a small town means the player characters 
and NPCs have more opportunities to 
learn about each other. Do you want the 
campaign to focus on this area, or is it just 
the starting point and the story will take the 
characters to nearby and distant locations? 
What is it about this location that makes 
it the best place to start the story? Was 
there an important event here relevant to 
the campaign, like the player characters 
were born here or they all awakened here 
after a mysterious disappearance? Is there 
something here (like a hellmouth or magical 
university) that makes it a point of interest 
for the magical world? Did something bring 
the PCs here? 
Present: What interesting things are 
happening right now that the PCs can get 
involved in? Who (other than the NPCs) is 
important to the setting? What issues and 
conflicts have just become a problem or just 
been solved? Are there any threats that are 
immediate or on the horizon? How aware 
are people (PCs and NPCs) of the ongoing 
problems in the setting? What other things 
are happening in the background and side 
scenes that flavor the story but may not 
affect the main plotline?
Future: Are there any present conflicts that, 
if resolved, would result in a major power 
or paradigm shift in the setting? Is magic 
fading away and will be gone by the time 
the campaign ends? Is there a specific 
end point you want to work toward, such 
as a magical apocalypse, the return (and 
possible defeat) of a major historical villain, 
or a society-changing event like revealing 
the existence of magic or vampires? Do 
the PCs have any hints (such as from 
prophecies or divination magic) about what 
is to come and their role in it?
Past: What significant past events shaped 
the present day? Was there a historical 
battle? Did a notorious or respected sorcerer 
live here? A political assassination? The 
sudden reappearance of magic, or the reveal 
of its existence? Was there a war between 
magicians and normal folk, or between two 
supernatural creatures or factions? Is there 
a sleeping thing that is starting to awaken? 
How long ago did these events occur, and 
how well are they known to the people of 
the present day? How can the PCs learn 
more about the past, and how is the past 
relevant to the present‑day events?
Magic: Is the existence of magic new? Old? 
A cycle of plenty and scarcity? What things 
can magic do? What things can’t magic 
do, or is bad at? Is there a price or cost 
for learning or using magic? How does it 
interact with technology?
Knowledge of Magic: Do the PCs know 
about magic at the start of the campaign? 
Is its existence a well-guarded secret known 
only to a select few? Are there conspiracy 
theories among the “mundanes” that magic 
actually exists? Is it known but essentially 
unavailable to most people, the way nuclear 
weapons are known to exist but most 
people will never see one? Is it known and 
common, like technology? Can anyone learn 
magic, or is it limited to certain bloodlines 
or institutions? How do those “in the 
know” feel about magic? Is it considered 
a gift, a curse, a vanishing resource, an 
amoral tool, a dangerous field of study, or 
a great equalizer against oppression and 
exploitation?
“Recent historical period” 
in this context means 
anything during the 
Industrial Revolution or 
later, as opposed to the 
pseudo-medieval era 
of traditional fantasy 
settings, the Age of Sail, 
or the Elizabethan era.

Because Spirit Callers are summoned whenever someone dies, it’s common

would have died of natural causes, which could be days or decades. Similarly to 
the provisional period, the ghost is visible and tangible, does not need food or 
sleep, and is stuck in the clothing they died in.

MAGIC AND THE LAW
MAGIC AND THE LAW
Legislation around magic follows common 
sense: if something would be illegal by 
mundane means, it is also illegal by magical 
ones. Theft is still theft, murder is still 
murder, and fraud is still fraud—no matter 
how you do it.
Spells and rituals with legal outcomes 
may require illegal ingredients. If you get 
pulled over with a human liver, a sample of 
uranium ore, and a sedated marmoset in 
your car, you’re probably going to jail. But 
if you use those items in a ritual to restore 
a depleted artifact, then dispose of them 
safely and discreetly, you’re unlikely to face 
consequences, except perhaps for a guilty 
conscience.
Some uses of magic are uniquely difficult 
to prohibit or punish, even if they clearly seem 
wrong. Mind control is one such case—where 
is the line between influence and coercion 
(especially for magic that makes a person 
more persuasive, as opposed to directly 
influencing someone’s mind with magic)? 
These ethical questions, more suited for a 
community than a courtroom, can be explored 
in the course of your Colvin Park adventures.
