INTRODUCTION
G
oodwife Elizabeth White was born in 1640 in a humble 
village where many people struggled to wrest a living from 
the reluctant soil. Over the years, she collected many helpful 
bits of knowledge that make village life safer, simpler, and more 
prosperous. Some of these things she discovered on her own, and 
others she learned from healers, wise women, and other “cunning 
folk.” (You or I might describe these people as “witches,” but most 
of them would reject that title for fear of persecution—the 17th 
century was unkind for those who practiced anything not approved 
by religious authorities.) Wanting to pass on her knowledge to others 
but lacking an heir, Goody White wrote down her lore in a book so 
she could pass it on to a worthy successor.
This book is an adaptation of her legacy, presented in game 
terms for PCs and NPCs in a fantasy game world to learn. It holds 
descriptions of 45 folk magic “spells” as penned by Goody White 
in her book, all of which have been attributed to various folk beliefs 
or historical witches, such as causing or curing impotence, brewing 
love potions, easing difficult births, or afflicting others with warts 
and poxes.
Each of the following pages present one of these folk remedies, 
leading with Goody White’s description of a “charm” or “spell.” 
Following her words are three different ways you can incorporate that 
folk remedy into your Pathfinder campaign: as an actual spell for a 
spellcasting class such as cleric or wizard, as a hex for the witch class, 
or a folk ritual that anyone can learn, even those who aren’t spellcasters. 
These aren’t the magic of legendary wizards and sorcerers like breaking 
mountains, hurling fire, and teleportation. This is stuff that a hedge 
mage might learn, or a local wise woman, midwife, or superstitious 
farmer might use to make life a little easier. They add flavor to a 
campaign, and some are things that even a famous adventurer would 
want to learn, such as stanching a bleeding wound or treating a burn.
How these spells are introduced to the campaign is up to the GM. 
If found as an in-game item, Goody White’s Book of Folk Magic might 
effectively be a wizard’s spellbook. The descriptions of the folk 
remedies might be enough information to allow a witch character 
to select one as a hex. A PC could practice a ritual presented in the 
book until it works. Alternatively, this can just be a source of new 
game mechanics for the campaign and are introduced through spell 
research, scrolls, or wisdom an NPC shares.
For the record, many of Goody White’s spells deal with real-world 
issues like pregnancy, body image, seizures, and so on. I don’t think 
these topics are funny. I have friends who have suffered miscarriages. 
I have friends who’ve been fat-shamed. I have friends who have 
epilepsy. I also don’t consider these spell writeups to be disrespectful 
toward those issues. It would be great if there were an actual spell to 
prevent a miscarriage, help maintain a healthy weight, cure epilepsy, 
and so on. Don’t use this book as an excuse to make light of another 
person’s struggles, whether in real life or a campaign.
Thank you, and good gaming!
HEXES
Any witch can learn the witch hexes in this book. Most duplicate 
one or more of the minor spells presented on the same page, often 
with a difference or twist. For example, the tame animal spell (see 
page 4) improves an animal’s attitude toward you and gives you a 
bonus on skill checks to influence that animal; the equivalent witch 
hex has the same effect, but also allows you to telepathically call an 
animal to you that you’ve tamed with the hex, so long as it is within 
1 mile.
Unless otherwise stated, a hex that duplicates the effects of a 
spell allows the target to attempt a save against that effect, using 
the same saving throw type (Fortitude, Reflex, or Will) but using 
your hex’s DC.
peRpeTual Hexes
Many of the hexes in this book are defined as “perpetual” hexes. 
The effects of such hexes linger on the target, requiring neither 
attention nor concentration, until the witch dismisses the hex or 
the target is somehow able to remove it. Unless otherwise specified, 
a witch can indefinitely maintain a number of active hexes of that 
type equal to her Intelligence bonus. As a move action, a witch can 
to dismiss one of her perpetual hex effects at any range, even if the 
target is on another plane (unless otherwise stated).
If a witch knows more than one perpetual hex, each perpetual 
hex has its own maximum number. If a perpetual hex has multiple 
perpetual options, they count together toward that hex’s maximum.
Example: Jessica of the West is a witch with a Intelligence modifier 
of +4 and knows the potency hex (see page 11) and fertility hex (see 
page 13). She can maintain 4 potency effects and 4 fertility effects at 
the same time. She can spend a move action to remove one of her 
potency or fertility hex effects from a target (at any range) without 
affecting any of her other perpetual hex effects.
The scar hex (Ultimate Magic) counts as a perpetual hex.
CASTER ABILITY  
MODIFIER AND CHECKS
Your caster ability modifier (abbreviated “CAM”) is the ability 
modifier of the ability score (Intelligence, Wisdom, or Charisma) 
you use to determine your spell DCs. This term avoids the need for 
rituals and spells to list what ability score you use for each of the 
current and future character classes. If you aren’t a spellcaster, your 
caster ability modifier is the highest of your Intelligence, Wisdom, 
or Charisma modifier. If you have more than one spellcasting class 
with different key ability scores, use whichever is the highest.
Occasionally, a ritual or spell listed in this book will ask you to 
make a “caster ability check.” That is just making an Intelligence, 
Wisdom, or Charisma check using whichever one you would as 
your CAM.
REVERSE SPELLS
Many of the spells in this book say they are a spell’s “reverse”—
usually referring to a spell with opposite effects. This means that 
if you know one of the spells in the pair, you automatically know 
the other spell as well. For example, if you learn the restore potency

RITUALS
The rituals presented here are a simple form of magic. Anyone 
can perform a ritual; all someone needs is the knowledge of what 
words, gestures, and materials are necessary. In most cases, a 
character must be taught the ritual by someone else—only very 
exceptional people can create their own rituals, and only after much 
experimentation and failure. How the magic takes effect—faith, 
innate power, intense study, music, or some other source—is 
different from person to person. A lay priest might cure a sick 
animal through prayer and faith, and her student might accomplish 
the same feat by tapping into inborn magic.
Performing a ritual requires concentration, just like casting a spell. 
Unless otherwise stated, a ritual counts as a 1st-level spell for the 
purpose of determining save and concentration check DCs. The time 
needed to perform the ritual corresponds is like time spent casting a 
spell; it’s how long you must spend performing the triggering actions 
before the magic takes effect. Rituals are considered spells for the 
purpose of negating or destroying them (such as with dispel magic). 
Your caster level for the ritual is equal to your caster level from your 
highest spellcasting class; if you have no spellcasting classes, it’s 1/2 
your character level (minimum 1).
Rituals lack the power and reliability of true spells. Each has a 
chance for you to successfully perform the magic, typically a base 
value modified by your caster ability modifier (CAM) and sometimes 
other factors. If you have a familiar (whether a standard familiar such 
as from being a witch or wizard, or a lesser familiar described on page 
36) and your familiar is present while you are performing a ritual, 
increase the ritual’s chance of success by 10%. When you complete a 
ritual, roll d% to determine if you successfully activate the magic. If 
you succeed, the ritual takes effect (although the ritual’s description 
may allow the target to attempt a saving throw to resist the effect). 
If you fail, nothing happens beyond your time and materials spent.
Learning a ritual takes twice as long as performing it, whether you 
learn it from another person, read about it in a book, or acquire the 
knowledge in some other way. Until you have successfully performed 
the ritual, your chance of success for it is halved; this represents 
you experimenting to work out how you tap into the necessary 
power, which might be different than your instructor does it. This 
experimentation also explains why your spiritual mentor can use 
Wisdom as her CAM and you might use Intelligence, Wisdom, or 
Charisma, because the casting method that works for you is going to 
be a little different than your teacher’s.
You may perform a ritual as often as you want, so long as you have 
the proper materials for it; unlike a spell, a ritual does not use spell 
slots or otherwise deplete daily resources. Likewise, there’s no limit to 
the number of rituals you can learn.
A WARNING
Although many of these remedies are based on real-world 
legends, don’t attempt any of these rituals. They aren’t medical 
advice, and many of them may harm you. Just as you wouldn’t 
use an RPG book about surgery to perform surgery, don’t use an 
RPG book about folk magic to perform folk magic.
Furthermore, some of these chants and prayers are adapted 
from (but aren’t verbatim copies of) similar rituals used by actual 
pagans and practitioners of Wicca. This was done so as to not 
offend these people by using their actual religious rites in a game 
book (just as it would be inappropriate to publish RPG effects 
alongside their verbatim Catholic or Hebrew prayers).

O
ur lives are intertwined with animals, be they companions or 
livestock. Should you need to calm an unruly horse, mollify 
a barking hound, or befriend a skittish lamb, employ the 
charm I teach here.
This ritual was taught to me by Sillisarra, the druid witch of 
Underlake. It works best if you know the animal or its owner 
is present to inform you of its nature. Like a person, an animal’s 
temperament is a mix of the four natural elements, though one tends 
to dominate. If it is angry and frightening, its dominant element is 
fire. If it is free-willed and prone to quick changes, that element is air. 
If it is stubborn, earth. And if it goes wherever and does whatever 
it wants, water. Should you not know or be able to determine the 
animal’s essential nature, the rite is likely to fail.
Taming Charm: In a metal or clay bowl, place a handful of sand, 
bit of string, a small candle representing the tamer—either you or the 
person to befriend the animal—and a pinch of material representing 
the animal’s element. A match or wax shavings, a sprinkle of 
dandelion florets, a few drops of river water, or a tiny pebble are all 
effective materials for representing the animal’s element. Add a lock 
of the tamer’s hair and hair from the animal to be tamed. If you do 
not have hair from that animal, use hair from another animal of that 
kind, such as horsehair to tame a horse or wool to tame a sheep. Place 
the candle upright in the center of the sand and light it.
Speak this charm three times. If you are performing the ritual on 
behalf of another, they should repeat each line of the charm after you.
This evening we shall become one. 
With the good spirits as my witness 
I ask thee to bind this (man or woman) and beast 
Through body and mind. 
Let their bonding never part 
Obedience and caring, everlasting art.
While speaking the charm, tie the tamer’s hair to the animal’s hair 
and drip wax from the candle onto them both. Gather everything in 
the bowl into the center and light it on fire with the candle. When 
it all has burned to ash, bury half of it where the animal sleeps and 
scatter the other half under the tamer’s bed. If performed properly, 
the charm should align the beast’s will to the tamer. It tends to fade 
over a few days, but it can be repeated to give the animal more time 
to become used to the tamer.
I know there are binding spells that can make a bear or cougar 
docile enough to eat out of your hand, but they are unreliable tools 
even in the hands of the cunning folk. I would certainly not try using 
them. It is better to save your own life by fleeing than to risk a spell 
against a deadly beast.
TAME ANIMAL
School enchantment (charm) [mind-affecting]; Level bard 1, druid 1, 
ranger 1, shaman 1, sorcerer/wizard 1, witch 1
Casting Time 1 standard action
Components V, S
Range close (25 ft. + 5 ft./2 levels)
Target one animal
Duration 1 hour/level
Saving Throw Will negates; Spell Resistance yes
This charm improves an animal’s attitude toward you by one step; a 
typical domestic animal’s attitude starts at indifferent, and a typical 
wild animal’s attitude starts at unfriendly. If the animal is currently 
being threatened or attacked by you or your allies, it receives a +5 
bonus on its saving throw.
You gain a +4 bonus on Handle Animal checks to “push” the 
animal to perform a trick it doesn’t know or to “rear” the animal if it 
is wild (see Handle Animal, Pathfinder RPG Core Rulebook).
Any act by you or your apparent allies that threatens the tamed 
breaks the spell. When the spell ends, the animal remembers how 
you treated it and reacts accordingly.
Taming Hex
Taming Hex (Su): The witch can improve the attitude of an animal 
within 30 feet as the tame animal spell. Once per hour, the witch 
may attempt to telepathically call one of her tamed animals to her 
so long as the animal is within one mile. This calling requires a 
successful DC 15 Handle Animal check; the animal travels at its 
own speed and follows normal behavior (a wild deer would veer 
around a town to get to the witch on the other side, a rat would 
sneak through it, and an eagle would fly over it). This is a perpetual 
hex (see page 2).
Taming RiTual
The taming ritual takes approximately 1 hour to perform, and it’s 
chance of success is 50% + 5% × ritualist’s CAM. If you use hair 
from the intended animal (instead of another animal of that kind), 
the chance increases by 10%. If you perform the ritual for another 
person instead of yourself, the chance decreases by 10%. If you use 
the wrong element for the animal, the chance decreases by 10%. 
A successful ritual improves the animal’s attitude toward you (or 
the intended recipient) by one step and grants you (or the intended 
recipient) a +2 bonus on Handle Animal checks for that animal. On 
each day after the ritual, there’s a 40% chance that its effects fade 
(though the GM may decide that you or the intended recipient put 
in enough effort to keep the animal’s current attitude.)

A 
child’s doll often has a deeper purpose than merely 
something to play with. There is old wisdom there, that the 
love and care you put into a doll is a part of you, and you 
will watch over the child who carries it. A witch named Kody Volari 
taught me that there are ways to strengthen this sympathy between a 
doll and a person, creating a poppet that strengthens your ability to 
reach that person, for good or ill. I have heard other cunning folk call 
them moppets, mommets, pippies, or other strange things, but the 
will and the work of it is the same.
You may create one out of nearly anything, such as a root, corn 
husk, or branch, but I have found the most success with softer 
materials such as cloth, string, clay, or wax. The more work you put 
into crafting it, the stronger it is when you finish. I have never made 
a poppet that works if I did not include a taglock—some part of the 
person, such as a hair, nail clipping, a drop of blood, or even a bit of 
their clothing.
Binding a string into simple arms, legs, and head is the easiest 
method, but I prefer sewing a bit of cloth into a human shape, as that 
allows me to paint on it and clothe it. The closer I make it resemble 
the person, such as matching a scar or a favorite shirt, the more 
success I have at the poppet working as I wish.
You may take liberties with the color or decorations to match your 
intention for the poppet instead of matching its appearance. You 
may attach these to the poppet or place them inside. Brown or green 
has a strong connection to animals, such as protecting a shepherd’s 
boy from wolves. Black or images of swords or fire are good for 
banishing evil. Orange or yellow, or sun or fire symbols inspire 
creativity. Silver, white, or blue, and cloud shapes encourage healing. 
Love benefits from pink or red and images such as hearts and roses. 
Metal colors or actual coins promote wealth and prosperity. Red, 
white, shields, keys, and fences are for protection.
When finished, say a prayer or create a charm appropriate to the 
person, then breathe on the poppet or kiss its head.
CREATE POPPET
School necromancy; Level shaman 1, sorcerer/wizard 1, witch 1
Casting Time 1 hour
Components V, S, F (a handcrafted doll)
Range touch
Effect one poppet
Duration 1 day/level
Saving Throw Will negates (see text); Spell Resistance yes
You infuse the focus item with a magical link to a specific creature,

APPENDIX
classic names FoR House spiRiTs
Goody White uses these creature names in in the folklore sense, 
not referring to creatures in the game rules. For example, this 
magic doesn’t allow a character to summon or bargain with a 
gnome, salamander (Pathfinder RPG Bestiary), sylph (Pathfinder 
RPG Bestiary 2), or undine (Pathfinder RPG Bestiary 2) to perform 
common household chores. Her magic communicates with tiny 
fairies or elemental creatures as described in summon household 
elemental, but true fey associated with houses (such as brownies, 
Bestiary 2) might be pleased by similar gestures.
glamouR vs. glameR
“Glamer” is a game term for a type of illusion magic. 
“Glamour” is a variant spelling of that word, is not a game 
term, and in terms of folklore generally means the ability to 
shapeshift. This book uses the “glamour” spelling to mean 
actual transformation rather than illusion.
Healing spells and TeeTH
Spells such as cure light wounds can repair soft-tissue damage 
(such as damage to gums), but the rules are unclear on fixing 
damaged teeth or broken bones. Regenerate repairs broken bones 
and presumably would regrow lost or damaged teeth, but it is 
likewise unclear what effect heal, restoration, and other standard 
healing spells would have. The GM should determine if these 
spells can fix teeth, bones, horns, and similar body parts.

Goodwife Elizabeth White was a no-nonsense witch born in the 17th century. 
Over the years, she learned many spells, hexes, and rituals to deal with common 
concerns such as:
• Hair Loss
• Hunting
• Impotence
• Infertility
• Love
• Pregnancy
• Sickness
• Warts
Lacking an heir for her knowledge, she wrote it all down, creating Goody White’s Book 
of Folk Magic. Now her legacy has been “translated” into game terms—each of these 45 
folk remedies (based on actual folk beliefs or the powers of historical witches) 
is written in her own words, then presented as a spell and witch hex, 
as well as a ritual that any character can learn.
These spells are things that a hedge mage might learn, or a 
local wise woman, midwife, or superstitious farmer might use 
to make life a little easier. They add flavor to a campaign, and 
some are things that even a famous adventurer would want 
to learn. Whether you allow your player characters to find 
an actual copy of Goody White’s Book of Folk Magic, or if you 
just use it as a source of new low-powered magic for your 
campaign, these add flavor to a campaign and show that 
there is magic in the world beyond what happens in combat.
