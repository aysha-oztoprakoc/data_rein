Welcome to Fantasy age!
I
f you’re reading this, you’re about to delve into the 
fantastic world of fantasy roleplaying! This Quickstart 
document includes all of the information you need to 
play through an adventure for the Fantasy AGE roleplaying 
game using an abbreviated version of the full game’s rules.
Fantasy AGE is a game wherein you and your friends 
collaborate to play out the stories of fantasy heroes like those 
in novels and films. They pit their skill at arms, mysterious 
magic, and cunning schemes against terrible monsters, deadly 
hazards, and villainous foes in quests for glory, treasure, and 
immortalization through bardic song.
There’s a bit more to it, of course, but that’ll suffice for the 
purposes of getting you started on this text and the adven­
ture within. For this Quickstart, we’ve simplified matters, 
providing pre-generated characters (you’ll find them at the 
end of this book) and a ready-made adventure for them.
What’s Included
The Fantasy AGE Quickstart has three parts. The first is a 
condensed guide to the Adventure Game Engine, or AGE 
system, game rules that power the game, including a quick 
reference sheet players can keep handy during play. The 
second is an introductory adventure called Terror of the Ghost 
Ship, wherein the characters explore an abandoned ship at sea 
and confront the creature that killed its crew. The third is a 
set of pre-generated characters for the players referred to as 
player characters, or PCs for short.
What You’ll Need
To play through this Quickstart, you’ll need at least two 
people, though four to seven is best. One person takes the role 
of Game Master (GM) while the others are players. You’ll 
also need at least three regular, six-sided dice (abbreviated 
d6). Ideally, one of the dice should be a different color or size 
to differentiate it from the others. You’ll also need copies of 
various parts of this Quickstart, but more on that later. Pencils 
or pens and paper, or electronic means of recording informa­
tion at the game table, are also necessary.
If You’ve Never Played a 
Roleplaying game Before
If you’re interested in trying this out but have no idea what 
tabletop roleplaying is all about, that’s great! Roleplaying is 
some of the most fun you’ll ever have with your friends, but 
teaching all of the basics of roleplaying is a bit beyond the 
scope of this Quickstart. Start by going online and searching 
for some basic info. The Wikipedia page “role-playing game 
(pen and paper)” is a little dry but still makes for a decent 
starting point. Better yet, check out online videos of people 
playing tabletop RPGs (there are a ton of them, including 
Fantasy AGE games) or track down a friend who’s done this 
kind of thing before and ask them to show you how it’s done. 
The full Fantasy AGE Core Rulebook also introduces more of 
the concepts of roleplaying and gives advice to GMs. Trust 
us—even if this is your first time, you’re going to love it.
Before You Play
Before the group gets together to play, the Game Master 
should read this whole book, print out at least one copy of each 
pre-generated character, and print out enough copies of the 
Quick Reference sheet so each player can have one. If you’re 
working from a printed copy of the Fantasy AGE Quickstart, 
you can download a free, printable PDF copy from greenronin.
com. Other players can read the rules section of the Quickstart if 
they want to, but they shouldn’t read the adventure (spoilers!).
Character
Everything a player needs to know during the game can be 
found on their character sheet. It includes character details 
like Ability Scores, Ability Focuses, Ancestry, Background, 
Class, Talents, Equipment, and Arcana, all described here.
Ability Scores
Let’s start the tour of the Fantasy AGE character sheet by 
looking at its backbone: the nine abilities.
•	Accuracy measures aim, precision, and your ability to hit 
targets with ranged or thrown weapons.
•	Communication covers social skills and generally 
making friends and influencing people.
•	Constitution is your character’s overall health, fortitude, 
and resistance to harm, illness, and fatigue.
•	Dexterity covers deftness, agility, and reaction time, 
affecting how quickly and gracefully you move.
•	Fighting is your character’s capability in close combat, 
ranging from a brawl to wielding weapons.
•	Intelligence measures a character’s reasoning, memory, 
problem-solving, and overall knowledge.
About the Rules
The condensed rules presented in the Fantasy AGE Quickstart 
cover the basics, but you should be aware that some rules 
included in the full game have been left out. None of the char­
acter creation rules are included, for example, nor have the full 
systems for arcana or the details on the many magical trea­
sures characters might find. Many rules have been simplified or 
narrowed for use in this single context to help ease you into the 
system. If you run the sample adventure Terror of the Ghost Ship 
with the full Fantasy AGE rules, you can expand elements of the 
adventure to work with them.

Abilities have scores on a numeric scale from –2 (quite poor) 
to 4 or more (truly outstanding). Your character’s ability scores 
provide a “snapshot” of the areas where your character is 
outstanding, above average, average, or poor, and you use these 
scores to determine the outcome of most actions in the game. A 
score of 1 is considered average for player characters, who are, 
after all, the main characters of the story. A score of 0 is average 
for everyday individuals, the sort of folks with the good sense 
to avoid adventures and getting into all sorts of trouble.

Ancestry
You can choose from one of nine ancestries in Fantasy AGE: 
draak, dwarf, elf, gnome, goblin, halfling, human, orc, 
and wildfolk.
•	Draak are tall, scaly, dragon-like humanoids. They tend 
to be strong and willful and possess qualities like magic 
resistance or the ability to breathe fire as a special stunt.
•	Dwarves are stout folk who are associated with stone, 
metal, and the deep places of the world, known for their 
skill at arms and talent as miners and builders. 
•	Elves are graceful and perceptive people with intrinsic 
connections to the natural world and ancient magical 
traditions. They make consummate hunters and scouts, 
combining agility and subtle sorcery to great effect.
•	Gnomes are gregarious and clever and have a natural 
ability to easily visualize complex shapes and systems. 
This encourages many of them to become builders, 
tinkerers, and engineers. 
•	Goblins are small, quick, curious, and all too often seen 
as pests rather than people by outsiders. They have large 
heads and ears, big mouths, lean limbs, and greenish tan, 
brown, or blue skin.
•	Halflings tend to seek out new places, people, and expe­
riences as adventurers and are often seen as brave or 
even foolhardy by the standards of other folk. They’re 
small, about half the size of humans (thus the name).
•	Humans are among the most numerous and adaptable 
of all ancestries. Humans are often surprisingly adept 
at abilities where they have natural weaknesses, having 
strived to overcome their failings through study, natural 
knack, or sheer stubbornness.
•	Orcs are survivors and pragmatists. Various orc lineages 
feature pointed, bat-like ears, slits for nostrils, hairy

Background
Each Fantasy AGE character has a background that 
details their social class and their upbringing or life 
before becoming an adventurer. Each background provides 
an ability focus, and the pre-generated characters have their 
backgrounds listed on their character sheets. Many other back­
grounds are available in the full Fantasy AGE Core Rulebook.
Class
Adventurers in Fantasy AGE belong to one of four 
classes: envoy, mage, rogue, or warrior.
•	Envoys are skilled in negotiation, social skills, and 
working with or leading others.
•	Mages know the secrets of arcana and how to wield 
magic and cast various spells.
•	Rogues are trained in stealth, subterfuge, and combat 
focused on speed and agility.
•	Warriors are trained in the widest range of weapons and 
fighting styles.
Class determines a character’s starting Health, the weapon 
groups they are trained to use (see the Equipment section), 
and provides certain class powers and access to class stunts, 
detailed on their character sheets.
level
Over time, characters can increase in level, improving their 
class powers and gaining other benefits—a process often 
referred to as “leveling up” in RPGs. The pre-generated char­
acters in the Quickstart are level 1 characters, but Fantasy AGE 
characters can improve up to level 20.
Talents
Characters also have talents, giving them specific benefits 
based on natural aptitude or specialized training. Talents come 
in three degrees: Novice, Expert, and Master. The pre-generated 
characters’ talents, their degree with each, and their benefits are 
explained on their character sheets. All of the many other talents 
in the game are detailed in the Fantasy AGE Core Rulebook.

Weapons and Weapon Groups
Adventurers often rely on trusty weapons to defend them­
selves against monsters and other foes. Classes like rogues 
and warriors particularly specialize in wielding weapons.
Weapons in Fantasy AGE are broken down into weapon 
groups; characters are listed as trained in certain weapon 
groups on their character sheets. If you are trained in a 
weapon group, you can use its weapons normally. If you are 
untrained in a weapon group, you take a –2 penalty on attack 
rolls with those weapons, inflict half total damage (rounded 
down), and the weapon’s range is also halved (if it has one). 
The pre-generated characters are trained in all of the weapons 
they carry, as listed on their character sheets.
The character sheets list a character’s weapons, their bonus 
to attack with them, and the damage they inflict on a successful 
hit, as well as their effective distance for ranged weapons.
Armor and Shields
Adventurers wear armor to protect them from harm and 
some carry shields to help deflect attacks.
Armor in Fantasy AGE has an Armor Rating (or AR), 
which subtracts from the damage inflicted by an attack. 
Certain attacks, however, inflict penetrating damage, which 
ignores Armor Rating. Armor also has an Armor Penalty, 
representing its bulk and weight. If you are untrained in 
wearing a type of armor, its Armor Penalty applies to both 
your Speed and Dexterity tests. If you’re trained in that type 
of armor, its Penalty only affects your Speed.
Shields have a shield bonus. It adds to your Defense while 
using the shield. You must be trained in using shields to gain 
more than a +1 bonus from one. It’s important to note that 
some situations, like being surprised or attacked from behind, 
deprive you of your shield bonus to Defense.
The character sheets list a character’s armor, Armor Rating 
and Armor Penalty, if they carry a shield, and, if so, what type 
it is and what bonus it has.
Other Equipment
Adventurers often carry other equipment to survive in the 
wilderness or overcome various hazards. Each pre-generated 
character has a list of their equipment and descriptions of 
what it is for.
Arcana and Spells
Fantasy AGE mages know various arcana, areas of arcane or 
magical knowledge, which allow them to cast different spells, 
specific applications of those arcana. The pre-generated mage 
characters have their arcana and spells listed on their char­
acter sheets.
Magic Points
Each mage character has a magic points (or MP) score, 
measuring how much magical energy they have available to 
cast spells. When you successfully cast a spell, you pay its cost in 
magic points. If you don’t have enough MP to pay a spell’s cost, 
you can’t cast it, and when you have 0 MP left, you can’t cast 
spells at all! You regain magic points by resting: 1d6 + Willpower 
MP per hour of rest. After a Total Rest (see Recovery under the 
Health & Conditions section on p. 10), you regain all of your MP.
Spellpower
Each mage has a Spellpower score, which measures their 
magical might. Certain spells allow a test against Spellpower 
to resist or overcome their effects. Each character’s Spell­
power is listed on their character sheet.

Tests
The Adventure Game Engine system for Fantasy AGE uses three 
six-sided dice (abbreviated 3d6) for tests of characters’ abili­
ties. Note that one of the dice should be differentiated from 
the other two by color, size, or something else; this is known 
as the Stunt Die. Tests are made to find out whether your 
character succeeds or fails when a chancy situation comes up 
in the game. Tests come in four main forms: basic, opposed, 
advanced, and challenge.
Basic Tests 
To make a basic test, the most common kind, roll 3d6 and add 
the relevant ability score. If you have an appropriate focus for 
that ability, add +2 more. For example, when attempting to 
dodge around an obstacle, you roll 3d6, add your Dexterity 
score, and add +2 if you have the Acrobatics focus. A char­
acter may only add one ability score and one focus bonus per 
test. Appropriate focuses for a given test are listed in paren­
thesis after the ability the test calls for. For example, “an Intel­
ligence (Healing) test.” However, you don’t need to have the 
focus to attempt a test unless it says “required” after the focus.
The sum of your dice roll, ability, and focus bonus are 
compared to a target number (TN) that represents the difficulty 
of the test. The harder it is to succeed, the higher the TN. If the 
sum or your roll equals or exceeds the TN, then you pass the 
test and succeed! The target numbers for various tests are given 
in the rules and in the text of the adventure in this Quickstart.
Basic Test: 3D6 + Ability Score + Focus 
Bonus (+2) versus Target Number
Sometimes the circumstances of a test make it easier or more 
difficult than normal, giving a bonus or penalty to your total. 
Such bonuses are rarely greater than +3 and penalties rarely 
worse than –3. Typically, if circumstances make a test easier or 
harder, the Game Master can adjust the target number rather 
than the total of your roll.
Opposed Tests
Sometimes you pit your character’s ability against another 
character’s ability. This is called an opposed test. In an 
opposed test, all parties involved roll tests simultaneously, 
but rather than comparing their results to a target number, 
the character with the highest total wins the test. If there’s a 
tie, the character with the highest Stunt Die number wins. If 
it’s still a tie, the highest ability score wins (after which you 
can flip a coin or roll two more dice as a tiebreaker, if needed).
The parties in an opposed test sometimes use different 
abilities. For example, a character attempting to sneak past a 
sentry rolls Dexterity (Stealth) against the sentry’s Perception 
(Hearing). If one character has an advantage over their 
opponent that’s not already reflected in their ability or focus, 
those circumstances may provide a bonus or penalty to the 
roll. Such modifiers are usually no worse than –3 or better 
than +3. So, if it’s dark, the sneaking character might get a 
bonus to slip past the sentry unnoticed.
Advanced Tests 
A task might be so complicated or time-consuming that it is 
inappropriate to resolve it with a single roll. Examples include 
doing in-depth research, navigating through difficult condi­
tions, or competing with another speaker or performer to win 
a crowd’s approval. These situations involve an advanced 
test. Advanced tests are basic or opposed tests that require a 
series of rolls before success can be achieved. Each individual 
roll is carried out like a basic or opposed test.
On a sufficient roll—one that meets the TN or exceeds the 
opponent’s result—the result of your Stunt Die is added to a 
running total. On an insufficient roll, nothing is added. (Or 
your opponent’s running total may increase, for an opposed 
advanced test.) Success at the advanced test is achieved when 
the running total meets or exceeds the advanced test’s success 
threshold, which usually ranges from 5 to 20 or more. Each 
roll takes a certain amount of time—a round, a minute, an 
hour, or any other increment—depending on the test, so 
an advanced test can also be used to determine how long a 
task takes. Each roll might also consume some number of 
resources. So, usually, an advanced test can be failed only 
if time or resources run out, or if—in an opposed advanced 
test—your opponent wins the test first.
Challenge Tests
Some actions in Fantasy AGE impose consequences or setbacks 
when characters don’t do them well enough. These challenge 
tests can be thought of as advanced tests with added risks.
As with an advanced test, the Game Master determines 
a difficulty and success threshold and how much time each 
roll represents. The GM also decides on the ability and focus 
for the test and may vary them, or they may require different 
abilities or focuses in succession. Unlike an advanced test, a 
challenge test has consequences for each failed roll beyond 
just wasting time and resources. These consequences come 
in three levels—minor, moderate, and major—and the test 
determines how many times characters can fail at a challenge 
test before increasing the level of the consequences. Specific 
consequences are detailed along with the challenge test.
The Stunt Die
You roll three dice when making a test, with one die differ­
entiated from the other two. The differentiated die is known 
as the Stunt Die and has several uses in the AGE system. In 
particular, it is used to measure degrees of success or failure, 
to break ties, and to generate stunt points (SP). When you see 
a reference to the Stunt Die, just remember it’s the differenti­
ated die you roll with ability tests.

Degree of Success
Most of the time, you only need to know if an ability test 
succeeds or fails: Did you accomplish the goal or not? Some­
times, however, it’s important to know to what degree you 
succeeded. This is one of the uses of the Stunt Die. Check the 
dice on your ability test and note the number on the Stunt Die. 
The higher the value, the greater the degree of your success: 
A 1 on your Stunt Die means you barely pulled it off while a 
6 (or better, due to modifiers to the Stunt Die) means you did 
it flawlessly. Note that if you failed the test, the result of the 
Stunt Die is generally irrelevant.
The Game Master tells you when the degree of success is 
important and will ask for the Stunt Die’s value as well as 
your test total in those cases. This usually makes it a good idea 
to leave the dice on the table as you rolled them until your test 
is complete, just in case you need to refer to them again.
Example
Your character is doing a quick repair job. The GM asks for a 
test to see if you can do it but also asks for the degree from the 
Stunt Die to see how well you perform. With just a 1, the repair 
might not even last for the whole encounter while, with a 6, it’s 
almost as good as new!
Stunts
There’s a difference between accomplishing something and 
really accomplishing it with style. That difference comes into 
play in the AGE system with stunts.
When you roll dice for a test and any two or more of 
the dice show the same number, that test generates stunt 
points (abbreviated SP). If the test is successful, you can use 
these points to enhance the results, pulling off some special 
maneuver, trick, or flourish beyond just the benefits of 
success. In fact, you must spend any stunt points you generate 
on a test or else they disappear at the end of your turn. The 
kinds of stunts you can achieve depend on the type of action 
and encounter. See the Encounters section for examples of 
stunts you can perform during combat, exploration, and 
social encounters.
As a general rule, only active tests generate stunt points, 
such as when your character is trying to accomplish or achieve 
something. Tests you make in response to something else, 
such as a test to resist an effect, or a follow-up test required 
by another action, do not generate stunt points. This includes 
tests required when performing stunts themselves. Tests that 
do not generate SP are designated simple tests.
Example
In your test chasing after someone, you roll 3, 3, and 4 for a 
total of 10. You also have two matching numbers on the dice 
(two 3s), so your test generates stunt points. With a result of 
4 on the Stunt Die (the last die listed), you get 4 SP to spend 
immediately. Maybe you clear the obstacles so effectively that 
you’re also able to put something into the path of your quarry, 
helping you to catch up, for example.

Damage & Conditions
Adventurers risk danger from deadly monsters to cunning 
traps and various hazards. When those dangers strike, char­
acters might suffer damage  or various conditions.
Damage
Most dangerous things do damage, which is measured in the 
number of points subtracted from the character’s Health score. 
Damage is typically rolled on one or more dice, which are 
totaled up. Armor Rating reduces damage (unless it is pene­
trating). Any remaining damage is subtracted from the char­
acter’s Health. A character who reaches 0 Health is defeated. 
They take on a Defeat Condition of the attacker’s choice—or 
the Game Master’s choice, if the defeat was caused by a char­
acter, hazard, trap, or other circumstance controlled by the GM.
Conditions
During play in Fantasy AGE, a number of conditions can affect 
characters. Conditions are imposed by damage, hazards, and 
so forth. Some are merely bothersome while others far more 
serious, even potentially fatal. Recovering and removing one 
or more conditions usually takes a certain amount of time.
The following is a listing and description of the conditions 
characters may acquire, including how they recover from them. 
If the recovery line says, “Based on circumstance,” then the 
Game Master decides how and when the character recovers from 
the condition, based on what caused it and how long that affects 
the character. Effects that impose conditions usually describe 
the recovery conditions as well. The Fantasy AGE Core Rulebook 
describes additional conditions beyond those described here.
Defenseless
A Defenseless character loses their Dexterity bonus to Defense 
and suffers an additional –3 penalty to their Defense.
Recovery: Based on circumstance. If an ability makes a target 
Defenseless and does not  list a duration, they are Defenseless 
for 1 round. 
Dying	
DEFEAT CONDItiON
Your current Health drops to 0 if it is not at 0 already. If you 
are conscious, you can talk but can’t take any other actions. You 
risk death after 4 + Constitution rounds (at the end of the final 
round) unless you are healed to above 0 Health. At the end of the 
final round, make a TN 13 Constitution (Stamina) test. If you fail, 
you die. If you succeed, you are Unconscious (see Unconscious) 
for 1 hour (unless healed) but no longer Dying, at which point 
you must make another TN 13 Constitution (Stamina) test with 
the same results, except that if you generate stunt points on the 
second or subsequent tests, you stop being Unconscious. 
Recovery: You lose this condition as soon as an ally’s action 
or other beneficial event raises your current Health to 1 or 
greater, or you make a successful Constitution (Stamina) 
check after an hour.
Frightened
You are afraid of a specific person, place, or object. Until this 
fear subsides, you must use one of your actions to move away 
from the source of your fear each round or suffer a –3 penalty 
to all ability tests, with the exception of any tests required to 
remove obstacles to your retreat. If you are cornered by the 
source of your fear, you do not suffer any penalty to your 
actions as long as they’re intended to help defend you or find 
an escape route. 
Recovery: Based on circumstance.
Helpless	
Defeat COndition
You are conscious but can’t take minor or major actions, including 
those which require only mental effort, though you can take 
reactions (such as tests to avoid hazards) and free actions (such 
as speaking). You can stagger or crawl 2 yards per round. 
Recovery: If you gained this condition because your current 
Health dropped to 0, you lose it as soon as your current Health 
recovers to 1 or higher. Otherwise, based on circumstance.
Hindered
You lose one of your minor actions so that you may only take 
a single major or minor action on your turn. 
Recovery: Based on circumstance.
Prone
Prone characters can only crawl 1 + Dexterity (minimum 1) 
yards as their Move action unless they stand (see Recovery). 
Prone characters cannot use the Run major action on any turn 
where they are Prone, even if they get up. Characters get a 
+1 bonus to melee attacks rolls against Prone characters but 
suffer a –1 penalty to ranged attacks against them. 
Recovery: Unless you’re unable, a Move minor action allows

Combat Encounters
When a fight breaks out and it matters exactly who does what 
when and in what order, it’s time for a combat encounter. 
Combat encounters have other uses as well. Essentially, 
anything that would be considered an action scene in a movie 
with potential conflict can be handled as a combat encounter. 
During a combat encounter, an order of initiative is deter­
mined at the beginning of the encounter, and then all of the 
characters take turns in that order. Each cycle of all characters 
taking their turn is called a round. When each round ends, a 
new round begins—using the same initiative order.
Initiative 
At the beginning of a combat encounter, each character rolls 
an opposed test using Dexterity (Initiative). Rather than one 
character winning, however, all combatants’ test results are 
ranked in order from highest to lowest. Combatants then 
take their turns in that order. Ties are broken the same way as 
other opposed tests. The GM usually rolls separately for each 
important NPC but rolls for minor NPCs in groups, who all act 
at the same time (to keep things simple).
Actions
On their turn, a character can take one major action and one 
minor action. If the player wishes, however, their character 
may take two minor actions instead. The most common major 
and minor actions are listed here, but other specialized actions 
are detailed in the Fantasy AGE Core Rulebook. Using these as 
a guide, the GM can adjudicate other actions.

Recovery
When your character has lost Health, there are several ways 
to recover.
Heal Action 
Another character can aid you with a Heal action (see Heal 
under Major Actions, following). You can’t benefit from 
another Heal action until you take more damage, as there is 
only so much benefit you can gain from first aid.
Magic 
You can be healed with a spell or other supernatural power, 
following the rules listed for that spell, or power.
Rest 
Rest can help you recover as well. There are three kinds of rest 
in Fantasy AGE:
•	A breather is a 5-minute rest that lets you catch your 
breath and tend to minor injuries. After a breather, you 
regain 5 + your Constitution + Level in Health. You can 
only take one breather after an encounter. If you have a 
Defeat Condition at the end of an encounter, you can’t 
take a breather.
•	 A light rest is at least three hours where you don’t perform 
any actions more strenuous than speaking, reading, or 
walking no more than half your Speed. During a light rest, 
another character can devote time to treating your inju­
ries, provided they have access to the correct supplies. The 
healer makes a TN 13 Intelligence (Healing) test. If they 
succeed, they restore Health equal to their Intelligence + 5 
+ the healer’s level + the Stunt Die result.
•	A total rest is at least six hours of total inactivity. At the 
end of a total rest, you recover 10 + your Constitution + 
Level in Health.
Encounters
Fantasy AGE adventures are broken down into encounters, 
which come in three types: combat, exploration, and social.
•	Combat encounters are when things are happening fast 
and characters are called upon to fight and deal with 
life-threatening situations.
•	Exploration encounters involve the characters seeking 
out information, which could involve investigating a 
mystery, traveling through dangerous terrain, or delving 
into an ancient ruin.
•	Social encounters are interactions between the player 
characters and various non-player characters, usually 
aimed at achieving particular ends.

Chases
When one character chases another, an advanced TN 13 
Constitution (Running) test is required from each participant. 
Track accumulated points from the Stunt Die for each roll. 
The character with the highest Speed adds +1 on top of the 
Stunt Die result for each successful test.
The first character to hit 10 accumulated points in an indoor 
or urban area, or 15 points in an outdoor or suburban area, 
may choose to end the chase, either escaping or catching their 
quarry. If two people in a chase are within 2 points of each 
other, they are virtually neck and neck. Vehicles and mounts 
may also be involved in chases, though they use their own 
ability focuses, and get bonuses against slower participants.

exploration encounters often flow into combat encounters or 
are punctuated by social encounters. Stunt points can also be

Exploration encounters can feature a variety of events and 
challenges. These usually don’t happen round by round,

Hazards
Exploration encounters may involve encountering hazards—
potential risks from the environment. Characters in a Fantasy 
AGE campaign may need to contend with perilous falls, 
blazing fires, nefarious traps, and many other hazards. A 
hazard might have a test to avoid harm, such as a Dexterity 
(Acrobatics) test to avoid a fall, or an Intelligence (Lock 
Picking) test to disarm a mechanical trap. The hazard’s effect 
may be a certain amount of damage—ranging from 1d6 for a 
minor hazard to 6d6 for a truly deadly one—or impose certain 
conditions (see the Damage & Conditions section on p. 9 for 
more details). A hazard can be momentary, like a sudden fall 
or an explosion, or can last for extended periods, such as the 
dangers of suffocation or a raging flood.
Interacting with the Environment
Your Fantasy AGE character may be called upon to sneak past 
guards, walk across a narrow ledge, or pick a lock. If the chal­
lenge is another person, an opposed test is called for. Other­
wise, a basic test against a TN representing the difficulty of the 
task is typically what you’ll use to see if you succeed or fail. 
Use abilities and focuses that fit the task—Dexterity (Leger­
demain) to pick a pocket, for example. The GM has the final 
word on which tests, abilities, and focuses to use. The higher 
the number on the Stunt Die, the better you did, whether you 
rolled doubles or not.
Investigations
An investigation is a catch-all term for any action where 
you’re trying to get information. Generally, if you need to 
find a lead or clue to go forward in the adventure, like foot­
prints leading in the right direction, the GM should provide 
it. You’ll usually use Perception and Intelligence tests, and 
focuses related to the lead, to get more information, but you 
can always just try to guess. When you succeed, the GM gives 
you a clue: a fact about what’s going on. You can also use 
investigation tests to find out information that isn’t directly 
related to the adventure but that may help you in other ways. 
In those situations, you don’t automatically find the lead. You 
must make an appropriate test to discover it in the first place.

except the focus is on interacting with other people rather 
than with the environment. Social encounters normally use

error of the Ghost Ship is an introductory adventure 
for the second edition of the Fantasy AGE roleplaying 
game. It is intended for a group of four to six 1st to 
2nd level characters. The set-up of the adventure assumes the 
characters are traveling aboard a ship, although not neces­
sarily their own. They discover a derelict ship out in the Deep 
with potential treasure—and danger—aboard.
A pirate ship, the Sea Scourge, had captured a few treasures 
from a recent raid. Unfortunately for the pirates, their recent 
haul included an arcane amulet linked to a monster known 
as an Unseen Terror. Accidentally summoned by the amulet, 
the invisible monster stalked and killed all of the members 
of the crew, leaving the Sea Scourge a ghost ship, drifting 
derelict through the Deep, afflicted by the malevolent forces 
of the curse. Now, chance—or fate—has led the characters 
to the ghost ship, its treasures, and its remaining unseen 
inhabitant. Will they survive to reap the lost pirates’ spoils? 
Or will they succumb to the curse and join the ghastly crew 
for all eternity?
Theme
Terror of the Ghost Ship is akin to a “haunted tall tale” 
sailors might use to scare their audience in some seaside 
tavern: A derelict ship, adrift at sea, its crew all murdered 
by some unseen threat. It’s meant to be a tense exploration 
of the unknown with the potential for treasure and survival 
drawing the characters onwards as they seek to understand 
the nature of the monster threatening them and how they 
can deal with it. The adventure is primarily a mix of explo­
ration and combat encounters without much interaction 
since the characters cannot communicate or reason with the 
Unseen Terror.
Running Terror 
of the Ghost Ship
The scenes of Terror of the Ghost Ship are linked to locations 
on board the Sea Scourge. When the characters explore each 
area of the ship, if it is their first time in that area, they have 
the listed encounter. Adjust the encounter areas accordingly 
if characters return to an area they’ve already explored. The 
Unseen Terror is a kind of mobile encounter that can occur 
at any time during the adventure, with suggested places and 
times in the text.
Terror of the Ghost Ship should be a fairly easy adventure 
to incorporate into an existing Fantasy AGE campaign. It 
takes place at sea, out on the Deep, so it can easily fit in as 
something that happens while the characters are sailing to 
some other Shore. For example, you can use this adventure 
to extend the events of Set Sail for Breakwater Bay! from the 
Fantasy AGE Core Rulebook, with the characters aboard the 
Tradewind encountering the derelict Sea Scourge either before 
or after the events in Scenes 3 and 4 of the other adventure. 
It can likewise be a mid-journey encounter for another sea 
voyage, or even an encounter that a fishing boat or other

If the Party Refuses to Investigate
If the player characters are aboard someone else’s ship, then 
the captain decides it is either their duty to investigate the dere­
lict or a prime opportunity for a potential windfall—or some 
measure of both. The adventurers are given the opportunity 
to explore because they’re likely equipped for it—and they’re 
the most easily spared since they likely don’t have regular jobs 
aboard. If they bargained for their passage initially, the captain 
might even forgive some or all of their debt or promise them a 
potential cut of any “salvage” recovered from the Sea Scourge.
It may be the characters just aren’t the type to want to go 
aboard a seemingly abandoned ship out on the Deep. If it 
seems like they want to play it safe and simply ignore the 
Sea Scourge (thus bypassing the entire adventure), you can 
have a voice call out from somewhere on the ship, yelling 
“Aaaaaahhhhh, help! Help us!” once the characters are 
within earshot. This is actually Toby the griffkin (see Scene 
2) imitating one of the now-dead sailors, but the characters 
might believe someone on board the ship requires their help. 
You don’t need use this tactic if they seem willing (even eager) 
to board; they’ll encounter the griffkin soon enough, and the 
cries for help might actually dissuade potential boarders!
Scene 2
Above Decks
Exploration/Combat Encounter
Once the characters are aboard the Sea Scourge, read the 
players the following:
The deck of the ship is a scene of carnage: seven bodies 
are scattered across the deck, lying in dried pools of blood, 
fallen weapons close at hand. Most are human, but there 
is an orc, a goblin, and a wildfolk who looks like a feral

the scene, with nothing but the creaking of the deck as it 
slowly sways on the waves slapping against the hull.
The bodies all bear wounds that look like they were inflicted 
by some type of clawed beast, although the characters can’t 
tell what. They have all been dead for some time: the blood 
is dried and the bodies are starting to decay; scavenging 
sea-birds have been at them.
The Griffkin
At some point in the characters investigation of the bodies 
and other evidence on-deck, read the following to the players:
“LOOK OUT!” screams a voice out of nowhere, “CAN’T 
SEE! CAN’T SEE IT!” and a shape flutters and leaps among 
the rigging overhead.
Toby the griffkin (see Adversaries & Allies at the end of 
the adventure) has just announced his presence. The griffkin’s 
parrot-like upper body includes the ability to mimic voices, 
and Toby has picked up some choice phrases from the shouted 
last words of the Sea Scourge crew.

The Forecastle
The raised forecastle at the prow of the ship served as the Sea 
Scourge’s observation deck. Beneath the forecastle is the officer’s 
quarters, a single room with four bunks, each with an iron-
bound trunk at its foot. Three more bodies are found here, two 
humans and a dwarf, all with terrible claw wounds. Two short 
swords and a dagger lie scattered on the floor where they fell.
The trunks are not locked and contain mostly extra clothing 
and personal gear, although the characters can find a total 
of 31 silver pieces and a small leather-bound book written 
in Dwarvish; it is a collection of recipes (the dwarf was the 
Scourge’s cook).
The Quarterdeck
The raised quarterdeck to the aft of the ship is officer territory 
and holds the ship’s wheel for navigation. There is no one 
and nothing above decks there – the officer on-duty rushed 
down to confront whatever was attacking the crew and now 
lies dead among them on the main deck.
Underneath the quarterdeck is the captain’s quarters. When 
the characters open the door into the room, read the following 
to the players:
The room beneath the quarterdeck is clearly the captain’s 
quarters. Windows along the back look out from the aft 
of the ship over the waters of the sea. There’s a real bed 
bolted to the floor in the room, along with a writing desk 
that folds down from the wall. Slumped in a padded chair 
alongside that desk is a body, dark blood down the front 
of his once-white shirt, arms slack over the sides of the 
chair. Something gleams on the floor to one side.
	 Your attention is then drawn to the low growl coming 
from the left side of the room, where a table and chairs are 
arrayed in the midst of some low cabinets. A great brindle 
hound stalks around the table, where broken dishes and 
other debris are scattered on the floor. A great pair of 
knife-like fangs descend from its upper jaws as its lips curl 
in a snarl.
This is Swindle, the captain’s pet knifehound. See the 
Adversaries & Allies section for details. The knifehound is 
angry and frightened by both the death of his master and its 
confinement, as well as the presence of the Unseen Terror 
and the arrival of strangers. Swindle doesn’t attack the 
characters unless they take aggressive action towards him, 
but he fights fiercely if they do. If he loses half or more of 
his Health, Swindle flees from the combat and tries to hide 
below decks.
If any of the players ask, or a character has a total Perception 
(Seeing) bonus of +4 or more, they notice the knifehound is 
wearing a leather collar with a brass plate and buckle. The 
plate is engraved with the word “Swindle” in the Common 
Cant, the trade language most people speak, including the 
characters.
Beast Speech
If a character has access to the beast speech spell (Fantasy 
AGE Core, page 120), then they can literally talk to Toby 
and Swindle to learn more about what happened on board 
the Sea Scourge. Swindle can relate how the Unseen Terror 
first appeared when the captain was handling the amulet in 
his quarters under the quarterdeck. The creature attacked 
the crewman who came to the captain’s door to investigate, 
with the door then falling closed behind it and trapping 
Swindle in the room. Toby relates that the creature then 
systematically slaughtered the crew over the course of a 
night, although some of them managed to hide and hold 
out for a short while. Note that both beasts describe the 
Terror as “all claws, and teeth, and limbs, and eyes” if they 
are asked, but they are not aware the people aboard cannot 
see the creature.

Attempting to calm the knifehound rather than fight it is 
a TN 12 Communication (Animal Handling) advanced test 
with a 15 Success Threshold and a time of one round per

The Amulet
The shiny object on the floor near the captain’s corpse is a 
bronze amulet, circular and about two inches in diameter, 
engraved with arcane symbols and writing and strung on a 
thin bronze chain. A successful TN 11 Intelligence (Arcane 
Lore) test reveals that part of the writing on the amulet states 
“Fear Not.”
Any means of detecting magic reveals the amulet is magical 
in nature. The Unseen Terror is bound to this amulet and 
was accidentally summoned by the captain’s handling and 
examination of it. The amulet is still linked to the creature and 
can potentially be used to banish it back into the Deep. Once 
the party has encountered both the Unseen Terror and the 
amulet, any mage can realize the amulet can potentially be 
used to banish the creature. See Banishing under Defeating 
the Terror later in the adventure for more details.
Pirate’s Booty
In the trunk at the foot of the captain’s bed is a smaller wooden 
chest, which is locked, but the key is on the captain’s body 
in a vest pocket. (Otherwise, it is a TN 13 Dexterity (Lock 
Picking) test to open the chest.) Within the chest is a leather 
bag containing 60 gold pieces and 80 silver pieces, along 
with a velvet pouch holding assorted small gemstones worth 
an additional 500 silver pieces. (The GM should feel free to 
adjust these amounts based on their campaign.)
Also in the trunk, along with assorted clothing and 
personal items, is a book of navigational charts, showing the 
Sea Scourge’s travels to various Shores, which might be useful 
if the adventurers’ travels take them to other places (see the 
Epilogue and Loose Ends at the end of the adventure).
Trapped!
If the party came aboard from a launch or other vessel near 
the Sea Scourge, while they are on-deck or inside the cabins, 
the Unseen Terror cuts any moorings or ties, causing their 
vessel to drift away from the ship. Characters who go back 
to look find it gone, perhaps visible in the distance and far 
out of reach. If they left someone to guard their vessel while 
they explored, the Terror likely leaves it alone unless those 
left behind are especially anxious or fearful, which drives the 
creature to attack.
If there’s another ship that brought the characters to their 
encounter with the Sea Scourge, and there’s anyone left 
aboard, there’s also the question of what the remaining crew 
will do in their absence. If there are signs of trouble on board 
the derelict, there’s the possibility of a less scrupulous crew 
sailing on and abandoning the explorers to their fate—or 
even trying to destroy the Scourge as a cursed “plague ship” 
or the like. Play out these possibilities for added tension for 
the adventurers as you see fit.

Exploration/Combat Encounter
Hatches and steep wooden stairs lead from the top deck down 
into the lower decks of the ship. The Unseen Terror has been 
at work here as well, and no intelligent creature still lives on

lighting
Note that the lower decks were lit by oil lanterns, but they

lower Deck
When the characters descend into the lower deck, read the 
following to the players:
The lower deck of the ship is cloaked in shadows, the only 
light coming from vents and deck prisms to the top deck, 
casting small, checkered pools of dim illumination. It’s still 
enough to show the bodies scattered on the floor among 
the small crates, bags, and barrels in the central hold. Some 
lie with weapons close at-hand, most of them clad in what­
ever clothing they slept in. Empty hammocks are strung 
along the sides of the hold, swaying and creaking with the 
movement of the ship.
There are another ten bodies on this deck, all killed similarly 
to those on the top deck: savaged with ragged claw wounds 
and bites. One or two squeaking rats skitter away from the 
bodies when the characters approach (bolder members of the 
rat swarm scurry down to the lower hold; see The Hold on 
the next page). Exploring this deck turns up the following. 
Cabins
To the aft of the ship are a pair of small cabins that were 
primarily used to house sick or injured crew members or 
function as a brig, when needed, since the Sea Scourge rarely 
entertained guests or passengers. The two cabins have bunks 
and small fixtures bolted to the deck or the walls. There’s 
nothing of real interest in them, as they weren’t in use when 
the Terror was unleashed on board.
The Galley
A tiny galley is between the cabins and the hold, used by the 
ship’s cook to prepare food for the crew. It contains pots and 
pans and other cooking implements, along with some knives

The Hold
The lowermost deck of the ship is a somewhat cramped cargo 
hold. The ceiling is low here, barely two yards high, so tall 
characters have to crouch slightly to avoid hitting their heads 
against it. The bodies of three pirates lie amongst the dry 
goods and ship’s stores in the hold, and a swarm of bilge rats 
has been at all of them. The emboldened rats have claimed 
the central part of the hold for themselves, and they attack 
anyone who approaches. (See the Rat Swarm in the Adver­
saries & Allies section.)
Much of the food in the hold is beginning to spoil or has 
been despoiled by the rats, but the characters can find several 
still-sealed clay jugs of moonshine—potent, if not particularly 
high-quality.

Scene 4
The Terror
Combat Encounter
The Unseen Terror that killed the crew is still on-board the Sea 
Scourge. See the Adversaries & Allies section at the end of the 
adventure for its stats and details. The Terror likely picks up 
on the presence of the newcomers fairly quickly, and, once 
any of them begin experiencing fear or apprehension, it is 
driven to attack, clawing wildly at the characters.
Surprise!
The Terror almost certainly surprises them initially, gaining 
a free turn of actions before the initiative order begins. 
Once the adventurers know the creature is aboard, it might 
surprise them again: Test the Terror’s Dexterity (Stealth) with 
its Unseen quality against characters’ Perception test results. 
Note that many of the usual tricks to reveal an invisible foe—
such as looking for tracks, coating it with flour or water, 
or throwing a tarp over it—don’t work since the creature’s 
Unseen quality extends to those things associated with its 
presence as well.
Terror Tactics
If the Unseen Terror ever drops to 10 Health or less, it retreats 
from the combat to take a breather and recover, returning at 
full Health to attack again. Regardless, the adventurers have 
no evidence their attacks hurt the creature beyond it recoiling 
and retreating; the Terror doesn’t bleed or otherwise leave 
evidence that it is hurt.
At least initially, the Unseen Terror is given to toying with 
its prey. It makes hit-and-run attacks, always moving to 
disguise its location, withdrawing to recover if it is damaged 
before coming at the characters once more. It seeks to stoke 
their fear and anxiety and may trap them in part of the ship 
if they look to barricade themselves behind a door or the like.
Special Stunts
Characters fighting the Unseen Terror can perform the special 
stunts listed on the table at right. Make players aware of these 
options when they generate Stunt Points during the combat.
Ramping Things Down
If things are looking dire for the heroes and you want to give 
them a chance, you can have Swindle the knifehound attack 
the Terror and damage or distract it, perhaps even grap­
pling it. He can see the creature and, even if the knifehound 
isn’t particularly friendly towards the characters, he has an 
instinctive hatred for the beast. Toby the griffkin can likewise 
attack the Terror, but more likely as a distraction or to give 
the heroes a better idea where the creature is, reducing the 
penalty on their Attack Tests.
Ramping Things Up
The main way to ramp up the challenge posed by the Unseen 
Terror is to increase the damage of the creature’s Claw 
attacks and to increase its Health, allowing it to fight longer 
between breathers to recover and inflict more damage. As it 
is, however, the characters should not have an easy time over­
coming the Terror since they need to defeat it before it is able 
to retreat and recover to come at them again.
Defeating the Terror
There are a number of ways the characters can overcome the 
Unseen Terror, depending on what they have learned and 
acquired from their exploration of the Sea Scourge. 
Damage
The simplest means of victory is for the heroes to bring the 
Unseen Terror to 0 Health and Defeat it. Whatever Defeat 
Condition they choose to impose, the Defeat is sufficient to 
banish the Terror. The characters get a brief, shadowy glimpse 
of a mass of clawed limbs, teeth, and hateful eyes before it 
vanishes in a howl of rage and pain.
Characters who have befriended Toby and Swindle have 
an even better chance in combat against the Terror since their 
animal friends can see the creature. If you choose to have the 
animals join the fray, Toby lands on, and claws at, the Terror, 
revealing its position and halving the effects of its Unseen 
quality. Swindle savagely attacks the Terror and is a fair 
match for it. The Terror’s main advantage is it can fly out of 
reach of the knifehound, if necessary.

Although vicious and relentless, the Unseen Terror is not espe­
cially strong (no more so than a normal person) and characters 
may be able to trap it somewhere, either below decks, inside 
a cabin, or in a net or other trap made from rope and sailcloth

Epilogue
Eventually, the adventurers who boarded the Sea Scourge 
either defeat the Unseen Terror, are defeated and killed by 
it, or flee the ghost ship and manage to escape with their 
lives—and a hair-raising tale to tell in taverns for years to 
come. Although the Terror is a dangerous foe, the adventurers 
should be able to overcome it if they keep their wits about 
them and use their available resources. What happens after 
that is up to their decisions based on the situation.
Rewards
The party that overcomes the Unseen Terror can claim the Sea 
Scourge for themselves. It’s not easy to sail the ship with as 
small a crew as the party represents, but they should be able to 
manage to make landfall at some Shore known to them using 
the guidance in the captain’s navigational charts. They can 
decide whether to sell the ship as salvage or keep it. They can 
also claim whatever else they find on board, although much 
of the Scourge’s supplies and such are spoiled and should be 
dumped along with the bodies.
loose Ends
Where you take the adventures of your Fantasy AGE heroes 
from here is up to you, but you can spin-out some potential 
loose threads from Terror of the Ghost Ship to lead to further 
tales, if you wish, such as the following
Bad Reputation
The Sea Scourge is a known pirate vessel, so unless the party 
changes its name or appearance, others might mistake it and 
look to either capture or kill the “pirates” without knowing 
the ship has changed hands.
A Mysterious Talisman
The amulet that summoned the Unseen Terror might have 
other magical properties for the characters to discover. It might 
also have been stolen, and someone might come looking for it.
Treasure of Import
One or more of the gems found in the captain’s chest could 
have magical properties or special value.
Mysterious Maps
The captain’s chart book could contain courses to previously 
unknown Shores for the characters, or even a treasure map to 
a place where the Sea Scourge hid some of its ill-gotten gains.
Strange cargo
There could be secret or hidden compartments on the ship 
holding whatever you want, or the Scourge’s hold might 
contain other goods or treasures you want the charac­
ters to have.
Secrets of the Animals
Maybe Toby or Swindle are more than just the animals they 
appear to be: Either or both could be people under the effects 
of a shape-changing curse or spell, the former familiars or 
pets of a powerful mage, or something else altogether.
Adversaries & Allies
The adventurers can encounter the following creatures 
aboard the Sea Scourge. Feel free to modify their listed traits as 
you see fit to suit the party and the adventure.
Swindle the Knifehound
Swindle is a knifehound that belonged to the captain of the Sea 
Scourge. Now the knifehound is wary and trapped (although 
disturbingly well-fed), but it may be possible for the charac­
ters to befriend him if they approach him cautiously.

The Unseen Terror
An Unseen Terror is a magical creature from the Starry 
Deep summoned by arcane means, such as a spell or curse. 
It seems to have sharp claws, teeth, or both, and savagely 
attacks intelligent creatures. True to their name, Unseen 
Terrors cannot be seen by intelligent beings; not that they are 
truly invisible, just that those creatures are completely inca­
pable of visually perceiving the Terror. Animals and other 
non-intelligent creatures can see them—and largely avoid 
them when they do. Unseen Terrors also rapidly recover 
from any damage done to them. While not invulnerable, 
they are difficult to kill.
A little-known quality of Unseen Terrors is they react to 
fear. The fears of intelligent creatures nearby drive them into 
a killing frenzy which, of course, tends only to provoke more 
fear, until the Unseen Terror has killed all intelligent creatures 
nearby. For this reason, those who summon a Terror usually 
take steps to keep them under control or contained.
