Quickstart Guide
Welcome to Athanatoi - Immortals! This quickstart guide will provide you with everything you need to start playing in this 
dark fantasy world inspired by Ancient Greek Mythology. Based on the familiar rules of the 5e system, we’ve made lots of ex-
citing additions to enhance and extend the gameplay experience. Whether you’re a seasoned player or new to tabletop RPGs, 
this guide will help you dive right into the action.
The realm of Hades is in turmoil. The gates of Tartarus are open. The Titans roam free.
As vengeful Titans lay siege on Mount Olympus, the mortal world lies in ruin, consumed by the fires of a second Titanomachy. 
From the flames, one last generation of heroes steps forth, eager for a place in the halls of immortal fame.
Delve into a land where myth and horror dance together. Explore a vast and rich wilderness full of dangerous beasts and 
fabled relics. Do battle with the minions of the Titans, defend the Gods, or shun Kronos and Zeus both and carve your own 
path. Delve into the depths of Hades, and uncover the truth behind the unsealing of Tartarus. Win glory, commit hubris, and 
carve your name next to the heroes of old.

Introduction
Hysterophemia
The word Hysterophemia comes from the Greek words 
“ystera” which means “later” and the word “fēmi” which 
means “fame”. In “ancient Greece” immortality was only 
the prerogative of the gods and a few other minor excep-
tions. However this does not mean that “common mortals” 
were not after it. Mortals had only one way to pass into 
immortality and that was through their deeds, the “name” 
they would leave behind and how important those deeds 
would be so that they would be remembered and recalled 
long after their time.
The path to immortality is therefore the so-called Hystero-
phemia. The more important your actions in life, the great-
er your reputation will grow. And when you leave this world 
for the Underworld and meet your final judgment, the next 
generations will sing your name - and thus you will truly 
become “Immortal”!
The Hellenic Peninsula
Athanatoi takes place in a parallel mythic Ancient Greece, 
namely the Hellenic Peninsula, during its Bronze Age. A 
land that spans a myriad of biomes and terrains, from fro-
zen mountains, to dark forests, to sun-bathed plains and 
breezy island shores. A gift of the Gods to the mortals that 
inhabit it.
The human denizens of the Peninsula have formed the 
so-called City-States, city nations with their own form of 
government. Their governments range from monarchies, 
to oligarchies, to the newfound system called democracy. 
Among the mortals, though, exist mythic creatures like sa-
tyrs and centaurs who organize themselves in secret settle-
ments and tribal villages. 
The Hellenic Peninsula is not a unified nation. Each City-
State and settlement claims their own territory and numer-
ous political ploys and wars unfold through one’s lifetime in 
the name of their state’s expansion and prosperity. 
The vast Aegean Sea stretches from north to south, en-
gulfing dangerous trade routes filled with piracy, legendary 
civilizations and the promise of fortune to the daring ex-
plorers. 
The Olympian Gods & Titans
The Titans were children of Mother Gaia and Father Ou-
ranos. Under the leadership of Kronos they were the first 
Gods of the mortal world. 
But one child of Kronos, Zeus the Thunderer, rebelled 
against his father and an apocalyptic ten-year-long battle, 
the Titanomachy,  laid the foundations of a new dynasty: 
the Olympians. 
Just like the first Gods, the Olympians were 12 in number. 
They reigned over the mortal world from Mount Olympus, 
under the rule of Zeus, now King of the Gods.
The Titans that opposed Zeus in the Titanomachy were im-
prisoned in Tartarus, an infernal prison in the Underworld, 
the domain of Hades, God of the Dead. 
Ages later, in this setting’s present day, the Tartarian pris-
on was opened. The Titans and all the horrors trapped 
inside were unleashed and a second Titanomachy began. 
The Olympians turned on each other while trying to de-
fend Olympus from the Titan siege. Most of the Hellenic 
Peninsula lay in ruin, consumed by the beasts and spawns 
of Tartarus and the destruction the Titans brought in their 
path. The mortal survivors were forced to retreat in the last 
remaining City-States and settlements, in fear of the dark-
ness that transformed their lands into a wasteland lair of 
unspeakable horrors.
Alas, while Olympus laid under siege and the mortal world 
counted its final era, a last generation of heroes emerged. 
Mortals of various castes and origins bound to a single pur-
pose: Becoming Immortals.
Their deeds might lead them to assist the Gods against the 
Titans or join the armies of Kronos and depose Zeus and 
his kin. Descend into Hades to reclaim their fallen. They 
may even find themselves fighting to protect the mortal 
world from collapsing or claiming its final breath. 
One thing is certain though. Their names will be remem-
bered.

HUMANS
Humans are the most numerous of the races inhabiting the 
Hellenic Peninsula. Created by the Titan Prometheus to 
populate the earth, they were given the gift of fire by their 
creator, which he stole from Mount Olympus. The blessing 
granted humanity the power of technology and knowledge, 
but came at a great cost for the Titan himself, who was 
brutally punished by Zeus.
Humans are innovative, adaptable and culturally diverse. 
To survive in the wilderness, they formed societies and built 
City-States, also known as poleis-krati or, more simply, po-
leis – fortified sanctuaries that double as monuments to 
their civilizations. In their quest for new lands to call home, 
they built ships to navigate the waters of the Aegean and 
the other seas of the Mediterranean. Every society they 
built developed over time into a distinct and complex po-
litical entity.
To a human, his or her polis is their country, home, the 
place they belong and its law and traditions shape their 
characters. The City-States have also adopted a certain 
culture that also contributes to the habits, expression and 
lifestyle of their citizens. This diversity made humans ex-
tremely adaptable and welcoming of most other races and 
outsiders, rendering them ideal traders, politicians and 
diplomats.
Human appearance varies greatly in hue and features. 
There is even greater variation in their grooming. Humans 
make heavy use of ornaments, jewelry and clothing to ac-
centuate their appearance, all inspired by the culture each 
individual belongs to. Thus, a stark woman from the war-
like Kingdom of Sparta will dress quite differently from a 
citizen of the exuberant Athenian Republic.
Humans have the shortest lifespan among the races of 
Greece, but their driven and ambitious nature often pushes 
them to make the most out of it. The ever-nearing prospect 
of death pushes them, more than any other race, to the pur-
suit of immortal glory, which is often sought through war 
and conquest. 
Human settlements were arguably the most heavily af-
fected by the escape of the Titans from Tartarus and the 
Second Titanomachy that ensued. Many city-states, partic-
ularly those found near Thessaly and Macedonia and spe-
cifically those close to Mount Olympus and Mount Othrys, 
were brought to utter ruin, or fell under the sway of the 
Titans and their minions. 
These include the Thessalian cities of Pherae and Iolcos, 
the island polis of Thassos, and Amphipolis. Even oth-
er major city-states, such as Athens and Sparta, found 
themselves besieged and partly wrecked by the forces of 
the Titans. In this new paradigm, humans are no longer 
the self-appointed masters of the Hellenic Peninsula, and 
many have been forced to live within the confines of their 
diminished and weakened city-states.
Adventuring Goals
Partly due to their brief lifespans, humans who become ad-
venturers are almost always on the path of a grand destiny 
or immortal fame. However, this may not always be evident 
to the hero, as was the case with Heracles, whose twelve 
labours were intended to atone for his sins, and Theseus, 
who volunteered to enter the labyrinth to stop the minotaur 
from devouring Athenian youths. In other cases, it is ex-
plicitly the desire for eternal glory that drove many of their 
deeds.
For prospective heroes, the inciting incident that starts 
their adventure may vary from personal grudges, to a king’s 
command, to the quest for a new home after being exiled, 
or divine decree. How the journey begins, however, bears 
little on how it ends.

HEPHAESTIANS
Hephaestians, also known as Forgeborn, are creations of 
the blacksmith god, Hephaestus. They have a humanoid 
build, with dark red-brown skin and light red hair, which 
grows darker as they age. Their veins have magma instead 
of blood, and their touch is abnormally hot, as if they are 
feverish. Apart from these features, hephaestians are oth-
erwise similar in appearance to humans.
Hephaestians are descended from Pandora, who was 
born out of clay and magma in the forge of Hephaestus 
on Olympus. She came about by the command of Zeus, as 
punishment for Prometheus’ theft of the fire which he gave 
to humanity. Pandora was bestowed blessings by the Olym-
pians, last of which was Zeus’, a twinfold gift: a box whose 
contents were unknown, and curiosity. When this combi-
nation of boons inevitably caused Pandora to open the box 
and let loose all the sorrows of the world, Zeus cursed her, 
causing her progeny to be as coal upon birth. From this 
progeny, given life by fire, came the hephaestians.
Hephaestians are master artisans, shipwrights and the 
finest workers of leather and bronze in Greece. They are 
credited with such creations as the legendary fleet of the 
Minoan Kingdom, the enigmatic Antikythera Mechanism, 
and the Colossus of Rhodes. They also came up with the 
design of the linothorax, the breastplate in use by most 
armies of Greece.
The hephaestian seat is the Talusian Forge, a crater strong-
hold located deep beneath the Talean Mountain range in 
northern Crete. The Talean Mountains are, in fact, the very 
body of the Colossus Talos, slain by the deceit of Medea. 
The crater, which is the core of the Forge, is where the 
heart of Talos fell. 
There, it waits to be rekindled by fire – one of the core proj-
ects of the hephaestians. Hephaestian society flourished in 
the Talusian Forge for untold generations, until the release 
of the Titans from Tartarus in recent years. Following the 
Titans’ escape, hephaestians fell into discord, and in their 
infighting brought ruin to the very heart of the Forge, mak-
ing it impossible for them to both raise Talos and create 
more offspring.
Now, most hephaestians roam the Hellenic Peninsula, a 
vagabond people who sell their expertise for a living. All 
hephaestians belong to the Guild of Talos, an order that 
seeks to preserve and promote the skills and knowledge in 
danger of being lost following the destruction of the Forge.
Proud and hardy, hephaestians still bear the curse of Zeus 
that struck Pandora, resulting in their offspring’s coal-like 
birth. In the past, this flaw could be fixed in the flames at 
the heart of the Talusian Forge, whose scalding-hot fires 
could ignite life into their bodies. Now, no fire is hot enough 
to breathe life into their children. As such, the hephaes-
tians walk a slow but steady path toward certain extinction.
Adventuring Goals
Roaming the world and discovering new crafts and tech-
niques is encouraged in the hephaestian lifestyle. However, 
leaving the comfort of one’s Guild, and the tutelage of one’s 
master, to pursue curiosities and discover wonders, is a 
daunting notion. Hephaestians tend to utilize their craft’s 
knowledge and adapt it to other skills as they acclimate 
into the adventurer’s life. Many become warriors; others 
follow a more scholarly route; a few tap into innate gifts of 
magic. Adventuring may come at any stage of a hephaes-
tian’s life, but usually a hephaestian departs towards the 
unknown during and after their apprenticeship.
Regardless of their vocation, for most hephaestians the 
goal is simple. Push yourself. Expand your craft. Acquire 
the skills that will enable you to create wonders and etch 
your name in the books of history.

NYMPHS
Nymphs are a race of beautiful, human-looking na-
ture-dwellers who inhabit the remote and distant corners 
of the wilds. Though seemingly human, their heightened 
features mark them as unmistakably distinct from mortals. 
Nymphs possess a slew of exotic powers, most notable of 
which is their kinship with the natural world, a bond both 
spiritual and magical. In essence, each nymph is bonded 
to a sanctuary – a small corner of the natural world that 
is wholly given to the nymph. That sanctuary defines what 
kind of nymph she or he is. 
Thus, naiads are nymphs of the sea, oreads nymphs of the 
mountains, dryads nymphs of the forest, and epimeliads 
nymphs of the meadows. A nymph is always aware when 
her sanctuary is in danger, no matter how far away she may 
be from it, for whatever reason.
Regardless of their various subcategories, nymphs possess 
certain universal traits, including ethereal beauty, extreme-
ly long lifespans, and an affinity with nature. From a techni-
cal standpoint, they can be regarded as deities, albeit very 
minor ones. Many are descended from the Titans or are 
the result of other divine unions. A select, powerful few 
are even worshiped as protectors of certain sacred areas, 
which, in truth, are nothing but their sanctuary.
When nymphs visit the civilized world, they do not stay 
long. Nymphs have little use for cities and towns, and feel 
discomfort while away from nature. Sojourns to civilization 
are more common for nymphs who become adventurers, 
but still undesired.
In recent years, the unsealing of Tartarus and escape of the 
Titans has wrought disaster to the natural world, which has 
left many nymphs’ sanctuaries desecrated or destroyed. 
Left without an anchor to the world, these nymphs often go 
mad with grief or rage, and lose themselves entirely in the 
pursuit for revenge. 
However, even the nymphs that have been spared are af-
fected by the ravaging of the land. As a result, they as a 
people have been forced to inch closer to human cities for 
survival purposes.
Adventuring Goals
While few nymphs answer the call of adventure, those who 
do have reasons beyond a personal quest for glory. If glory 
is the goal, the nymph usually seeks to bestow it upon their 
sanctuary – which, to a nymph’s eyes, will be their true leg-
acy after death.
A particularly grim motivation for adventure is the viola-
tion, desecration or destruction of a nymph’s sanctuary 
– a traumatic event that leaves her untethered and unsta-
ble. Following the release of the Titans, such events have 
become much more common than they were in the past, 
when the sanctuary of a nymph was often regarded as sa-
cred. 
These nymphs are just as likely to take to the wilds to pre-
serve the memory of their ruined site as they are to go in-
sane.
Lastly, there are nymphs who become adventurers for rea-
sons unrelated to their sanctuary. Sometimes these are el-
derly nymphs who, sensing death’s approach, feel the urge 
to leave their mark upon their world while they still can.

SATYRS
Satyrs are pneumata – spiritual beings that have assumed 
a material form that resembles a mix between a man and a 
goat. Most satyrs have human bodies and goat legs, though 
it isn’t unheard of for satyrs to have human legs. Their fa-
cial features are savage and fey, strongly reminiscent of 
a goat, with prominent chins, bushy beards, large, sharp 
ears, squat noses and beady eyes. Their iconic features are 
the horns protruding from their heads, at times straight 
and long, at others slanted and curved.
In Hellenic Peninsula, the satyr race is the subject of much 
controversy among the other races. Hesiod describes them 
as “worthless and helpless”, a label that stems from their 
refusal to engage in labor as humans are meant to, but 
this criticism is not quite accurate. Satyrs mainly live in 
their own, secret societies, which they leave behind often 
in pursuit of raucous bacchanals, or to meet new faces and 
things, make love, or even experience thrilling adventures 
that will set their blood on fire.
In a word, satyrs are creatures of passion – and it is the 
expression of this passion that defines them above all else. 
Crafty, cunning and fond of trickery, they are prone to get-
ting themselves and their companions in trouble. Howev-
er, a satyr is more than a creature of fancy. Many satyrs 
are grave, solemn, curt and measured in word and deed. 
Though these contemplative members of their race are 
also bound to their various passions, they tend to indulge in 
them in secret, giving them a heightened aura of mystique 
and enigma. Almost all satyrs worship and exalt the God 
Dionysus, though exceptions do exist. The most common 
deviations include worshipers of Hecate and Aphrodite – 
the former for her espousal of witchcraft, the latter for her 
indulgence in a very common type of passion many satyrs 
are vulnerable to. Regardless, it is the patronage of Diony-
sus that keeps their secret settlements hidden. Their major 
cities, in particular – Thyrseia and Bacchia – can only be 
reached through a Drunken Passage – a magical corridor 
found between the aisles of a vineyard, if one knows where 
to look. Despite their exotic nature as pneumata, satyrs are 
often treated with scorn in human cities. And yet, though 
they may not partake in the human traditions of artisanship 
and labor, they are skilled enough to ensure a decent living. 
The most prominent of their gifts – apart from pranks and 
revelry – is magic, which is equal parts skill and innate tal-
ent for them.
Following the escape of the Titans from Tartarus, the sa-
tyrs’ frivolous way of life was interrupted by a calamity that 
rattled all of Mythic Greece to its core. Many of the dei-
ties’ religious mysteries (which satyrs were fond of, as they 
provided an excuse for revelry and debauchery) were no 
longer held, either because the sites where they took place 
were desecrated, or because the deities’ priesthoods were 
slaughtered. This included various Dionysian. 
To make matters worse, the Titans are able to see the Drunk-
en Passage, making the very act of traveling to the satyr cities 
dangerous and unsafe. As a result, many satyrs have found 
themselves flat-footed, uncertain how to proceed in a land that 
is increasingly spiraling into a brutal and warlike direction.
Adventuring Goals
Although satyrs age more slowly than mortal races, they 
possess the same irresistible urge to seize the day and 
live a life full of experiences. Questing is pivotal to a sa-
tyr, whether that is a quest for adventure, knowledge, or to 
understand one’s own self. A questing satyr can be a rhap-
sode, roaming the ends of the Earth, seeking tales to com-
pose a new epic poem, a hierophant, delving deep into the 
realm of Hades in pursuit of lost arcane lore, or a prankster 
seeking to find thrills through his or her numberless tricks 
performed on mortal and supernatural victims alike.

CENTAURS
The fabled centaurs, descendants of the mythical Centau-
rus, are creatures with the upper bodies of humans and the 
lower bodies and legs of horses. Wise, savage and power-
ful, centaurs used to rule over the harsh wilderness beyond 
Greece’s walled city-states. Their nature is twofold, com-
bining the grace, strength and affinity for nature of their 
equine half with a proclivity for wisdom, philosophy, knowl-
edge and music that stems from the human within.
Centaurs are oft labeled by the Greeks as primitive and 
savage, a perception they do not entirely reject, but which 
is also inaccurate. While centaur society lacks the civiliza-
tion-building focus of humans, it is quite complex, dividing 
along generational as well as ideological lines.
Each centaur follows a certain philosophy regarding their 
dual nature. These philosophies are named after famous 
centaurs who adopted them, and define many things about 
how a centaur looks at life. 
Some centaurs choose to embrace their animalistic half, 
eagerly playing the role of the fierce beasts hidden in for-
ests and hills. These centaurs are said to follow the Path of 
Nessus, a vicious centaur who indirectly caused the death 
of Heracles himself. They are sometimes called Nessian 
Centaurs. Most centaurs who live out in the wilderness 
away from civilization tend to follow this way of thinking.
Other centaurs hold the human half of their nature in high-
er regard, and hold the pursuit of knowledge as a founda-
tional aspect of who they are. They also tend to view their 
centaur nature as a curse, degrading their true potential. 
These centaurs are said to follow the Path of Pholus – a 
wise centaur who was a friend of Heracles, and who tend-
ed to eschew the warlike ways of other centaurs. Like their 
namesake, the Pholean Centaurs have respect for human-
ity and often play host to traveling heroes.
The last major philosophy followed by centaurs seeks to 
strike a balance between the savage nature of the Nessian 
school and the human affinity of the Pholean school. These 
centaurs embrace the animalistic aspect within them, but 
also hold civilization and values such as wisdom in high 
esteem. They follow the Path of Chiron, wisest among the 
centaurs, who himself tutored a bevy of legendary figures, 
including the heroes Achilles and Jason, the scholar of 
medicine Asclepius and the crafty Aristaeus. These are the 
Chironite Centaurs, and those who are tutored by them 
can expect to learn the ways of philosophy and mathemat-
ics, as well as hunting and warfare.
Centaurs have traditionally been tribal creatures, and even 
those who followed a more human-friendly philosophy 
tended to stay away from major city-states. However, the 
escape of the Titans from Tartarus in recent years brought 
about vast destruction upon the lands that many of these 
tribes called home, especially tribes native to the northerly 
regions of Thessaly and Macedon. These calamities, along 
with the increased presence of monsters loyal to the Ti-
tans all around the Hellenic Peninsula, has forced even the 
most reclusive tribes to inch closer to civilization.
Adventuring Goals
There are a great many number of reasons for a centaur to 
go adventuring. For the Nessians, it is the quest for battle, 
the desire to prove the superiority of his or her kind, or sim-
ply sheer instinct that puts them on the path of adventure. 
Pholean centaurs have more immediate motives, such as 
the desire to come into contact with faraway civilizations, 
the desire to explore the unknown, or simply the all-too-
human desire to carve the name into the bosom of history 
and become immortal, in the way that immortality is under-
stood in Mythic Greece.  Chironites adventure largely out 
of plain curiosity, or to obtain knowledge and experiences 
that will help them achieve balance of their dual nature.

TARTARIANS
Tartarians, also known as Titanborn, are humans whose 
bloodline contains traces of the influence of the Titans, the 
predecessors of the Olympians. Tall, grey-skinned and ei-
ther eerily beautiful or off-putting, there is something un-
canny about the tartarians, which inspires fascination and 
fear among commonfolk.
The term tartarian refers to Tartarus, the prison where the 
Titans were sealed after the Titanomachy, their war with 
the Olympian Gods. However, not all Titans fought against 
the Olympians, nor were they all imprisoned. Thus, while 
many tartarians are linked to Kronos, Iapetos, and the oth-
er prisoners of Tartarus, just as many hail from Theia, Leto 
or Rhea. Nevertheless, these facts often elude the common-
folk, who associate the term Titan with destruction – and 
tartarian with untrustworthiness.
Tartarians walk a lonely and often dangerous path. Aside 
from humans’ distrust of them, their very existence is also 
seen as an affront by many Olympians, including Zeus. 
Thus, they are often forced to devise ways to conceal their 
identity, or live on the fringes of society as perennial exiles. 
The latter often take to the road, pursuing a grand destiny 
as adventurers, having been denied an ordinary one.
Once, tartarians who found themselves exiled but did not 
wish to take to the road would head to Othrysion, the only 
notable tartarian settlement, located on Mount Othrys 
near Olympus. Othrysion was traditionally shielded from 
the Olympians’ ire, mainly by pledging allegiance to Zeus 
himself and exalting him in worship.  However, in the after-
math of the Titans’ escape, Othrysion’s unfortunate loca-
tion caused it to fall under the Titans’ influence, as Kronos 
and his companions reclaimed Mount Othrys, their histori-
cal seat of power. Those tartarians who managed to escape 
Othrysion were truly left without a place to call their own.
Appearance-wise, tartarians tend to stand out. Their skin 
ranges from marble-white to deep grey, though in rare cas-
es it may resemble human tones instead. They either have 
lean, smooth features, or very coarse and harsh ones, with 
little inbetween. While some can look remarkably human 
at first, they often possess various oddities that highlight 
their strange nature.
Adventuring Goals
Tartarians adventure for a number of reasons. Above all 
is the quest for immortal glory which, consciously or oth-
erwise, drives most adventurers. Beyond that, there are 
secondary reasons depending on the individual. Many 
tartarians seek to discover the truth about their titanic lin-
eage. Tartarians generally do not know whose blood runs 
through their veins, and as they often find themselves un-
welcome in human society, finding out that truth becomes 
a quest of identity.
Tartarians also often look to prove themselves, either 
to the human society that rejects them, or to the Gods. 
These quests for recognition differ based on the tartari-
an’s idiosyncrasies and abilities. A fighter wants to prove 
themselves in the art of warfare, a bard seeks an adventure 
worthy of a homeric epic, and a mage desires to unearth 
the lost secrets of magic. By becoming extraordinary, they 
hope to earn a place to belong among the ordinary.
However, not all tartarians are exiles. There are tartarians 
who are accepted by their human peers and are valued 
for their various skills or knowledge. Those tartarians are 
treated with deference by ordinary humans, who may see 
them as anything from blessed beings to demigods. For 
those individuals, adventure may be a matter of honing 
their abilities to continue serving their community, not un-
like ordinary humans.

Hubris
The word Hubris comes from the Greek word “Yvris” and 
is a basic concept of the worldview of the Ancient Greeks.
When mortals overestimate their abilities and power, be it 
physical, mental or political, and behave in an arrogant or 
insulting way towards others and especially towards the 
Gods, they are considered to be committing Hubris.
The unwritten divine law enforces a clear separation be-
tween mortals and Gods and places limits on mortal ac-
tion. So whoever attempts to transcend his or her mortal 
nature and reach the Gods commits blasphemy and soon 
attracts the attention, insult or even the wrath of the Gods.
In most cases, heroes will not commit blasphemy because 
they simply want to provoke or anger the Gods. It is done 
mainly in their attempt to gain Glory and stand out among 
their peers. Of course, there are also those cases where 
the characters will do actions that are unthinkable, unlaw-
ful and even condemned by their social status. Either way, 
whether intentionally or unintentionally, hubris is a dark 
nebula that surrounds every hero, and if the characters 
don’t treat it with due respect, it will soon become a blade 
that prematurely ends their path to immortality.
Hubris & Nemesis Mechanic
Hubris in Immortals is captured like Hysterophemia , on a 
scale of 0 to 10. A character with Hubris 0 is someone who 
devoutly respects the laws of gods and men.
On the other hand, a character with 8-9 hubris has angered 
the Gods so much that their followers have dedicated their 
existence to punishing them. While a character with Hu-
bris 10 has secured an eternal punishment in Tartarus by 
Zeus himself.
The table below shows the Hubris level as well as some 
examples that correspond to each level as well as their ef-
fects.
Unlike Hysterophemia, Hubris scales harder at first and 
gets easier as you commit Hubris over and over again. The 
lower the Hubris Level, the harder it is for a character to at-
tract the attention and displeasure of the gods, but should 
it get higher, your name has grasped their attention and the 
less forgiving they will be.
Advancing your Hubris
All characters start with hubris between 0 - 2 depending 
on their race, while in rare cases it can be increased by 
some background. Every time a character commits hubris 
they get a Hubris Point. By accumulating enough Hubris 
Points, characters gain Hubris Levels. How many Hubris 
Points are required to raise a Hubris Level is determined 
by your current Hubris level. The lower your Hubris lev-
el, the more Hubris Points you need to start annoying the 
gods and getting closer to divine punishment..
Starting from Hubris Level 0 a character needs 10 Hubris 
points to go up to Hubris Level 1, while from 1Hubris Level

Magic & Special Abilities
Magic in Immortals has its own distinct flavor and sys-
tem, inspired from the ancient understanding of magic in 
Greece. Here’s a brief overview:
Aetheric Spellcasting
Spellcasting is the ability of a character to pierce the veil 
of reality and reshape part of the cosmos on his own will. 
Spells are the incarnation of that change in the world of 
Immortals. This section provides the rules for casting 
spells. Different character classes have distinctive points 
of view about spells and their origin but regardless of its 
source, a spell follows the rules described below.
Aether is the breathing air of the gods. Aether alone is 
intangible, immovable and invisible, but those who learn 
to manipulate it well can create a volcano out of a spark; 
gold out of lead; or invoke the spirits of the dead and bind 
them to their will. Some claim that Gods themselves are 
made out of pure Aether, giving them innate powers over 
substance and reality.
Aether is an invisible element. Only Gods, Titans, 
God-blooded creatures and spellcasters are able to per-
ceive Aether and make use of it to create spells. In plain 
terms, Aether is the fuel to all “magical” effects of the world. 
All spellcasters have an aether pool, which is similar to 
his level. For example a 10 Level Cleric has 10 ether points 
to spend while casting his spells. The amount of Aether 
Points a spellcaster can spend on a single cast is half his 
pool rounded up. So a 10 level Cleric may use up 5 Aether 
Points on a single cast. Aether Points may be recovered 
by various ways. A spellcaster regains all of his Aether 
Points after completing a long rest or 1 Aether after a 
short rest.
Spell Power & DC
Every spell has a Spellcasting Difficulty (SD) to be cast. 
A spell’s DC is a general indicator of how powerful it is, 
with the lowly (but still impressive) Tracian Might at DC 8 
and the earth-‑shaking Wrath of Zeus at DC 36. Cantrips, 
simple but powerful spells that characters can cast almost 
by rote have DC 6. The higher a spell’s DC is, the higher 
level a spellcaster must be to use that spell.
Deviation from Spell Slots & Spell 
Preparation
In this approach to spellcasting, there are no spell levels 
or prepared spells. The more potent a spell is, the more it 
twists and warps reality, thus making it more complicated 
to perform and increasing its Spellcasting Difficulty (SD).
This is the major fundamental distinction of spells in Im-
mortals. 
Additionally, contrary to other systems, spellcasters in Im-
mortals do not need to prepare their spells before a fight or 
at the start of the day. Any spells they have learnt through-
out their life (either as apprentice mages, by tapping into 
the powers of a node or unriddling old magical texts) are 
always available to them.
Casting a Spell
There are certain universal rules that apply when a char-
acter casts a spell. These rules apply regardless of the 
character’s class or the spell’s effects. All spell descriptions 
contain certain information, including the spell’s name, 
school of magic, its casting time, the range of the spell, its 
components, duration, and its Spellcasting Difficulty (SD). 
dditionally, some spells may include specific tags that spec-
ify alternative casting rules, such as (ritual), (philtron) or 
(prayer). 
Achieving a Spell’s DC
Magic in Immortals, however powerful, is also difficult and 
unreliable. It is far from guaranteed that a mage, even an 
experienced one, will be able to reliably tap into the aether 
and cast the spells they wish to do. To successfully cast a 
spell, a spellcaster needs to make a special check called 
a Spellcasting Difficulty check, whose DC is written in 
the spell description. Instead of using a d20, this check 
uses a pool of six-sided (d6) dice, depending on the spell-
caster’s Primary Spellcasting Ability and their Secondary 
Spellcasting Ability.
The number of d6s that a spellcaster rolls is equal to 
their Primary Spellcasting Ability modifier. However, 
they only keep the results of a number of dice equal to 
their Secondary Spellcasting Ability, adding the total 
sum of these dice and comparing it to the Spellcasting Dif-
ficulty (DC). The Primary Spellcasting Ability of each class 
is set. However, they may select their Secondary Ability.
For example, let’s say the magos Eteocles wishes to cast 
the spell Wrath of Zeus, which has a spellcasting difficulty 
DC of 14. Being a magus, he uses his Spellcasting Ability 
modifier (Intelligence) to determine how many d6s he will 
roll. Since he has an Intelligence score of 18 and a modifier 
of +4, he rolls 4d6s, which yield results of 6, 1, 4, 5. Then, 
he keeps a number of dice equal to his Secondary Attribute 
(Wisdom 16, aka a +3 modifier) and tallies the result. Tak-
ing the dice that resulted in 6, 5 and 4 and adding them up, 
he gets a result of 15, which surpasses the spell’s spellcast-
ing difficulty DC. The spell succeeds.
Spending Aether Points
As has already been said, aether is what fuels the creation 
of a spell. Spellcasters can use their aether pool, which 
from a flavor standpoint represents aether contained with-
in and around them, to make their spellcasting better and 
more reliable. This pool is made up of aether points. For 
each aether point that a spellcaster invests when casting a 
spell, they can choose to gain one of the following benefits:
	
►
They roll one additional d6 as part of their spellcasting 
difficulty check, as if their Spellcasting Ability modifier 
was 1 higher.
	
►
They keep one additional d6 from the ones they rolled 
as part of their spellcasting difficulty check, adding it 
to the sum total of their check, as if their Secondary 
Ability modifier was 1 higher.
A spellcaster must choose how many Aether Points to 
spend and which benefit to gain from each one before mak-
ing the spellcasting difficulty check.

Seafaring & Sailing in Mythi-
cal Greece
Shipbuilding and seafaring are a major component of 
Mythic Greece. As the entire country is almost fully sur-
rounded by sea, it is impossible for seafaring not to play 
a huge role in Greek culture, economy and everyday life. 
From the flourishing trade that the Minoans conduct with 
faraway realms to the east and south, to the thousands of 
fishermen whose work sustains the island societies of the 
Aegean and Ionian Sea, almost every aspect of the Greek 
family is somehow connected to the sea.
Some of the most well-known Greek epics feature seafar-
ing adventures and the sea in general. The most iconic 
of these include the Argonauts’ campaign to recover the 
Golden Fleece, the setting sail for the Trojan War, and the 
Odyssey. Thus, it is a chapter of special significance to Im-
mortals.
Sea Traveling
The primary thing one needs to travel by sea is a vessel. 
The choice of ship is instrumental and depends on the pur-
pose and expected duration of the trip. Naturally, no one 
expects to travel from Crete all the way to Athens on a tiny 
papyrella, nor is anyone crazy enough to try crossing the 
straits of Chalkis in strong winds with a large warship.
Roles
Though having a ship is an important first step to crossing 
the seas, it alone is not enough. An expert crew is needed 
to operate the vessel, outfitted with a combination of skills 
necessary to get a ship from one point to another.
Thus, upon deciding to sail a ship (as more than passen-
gers, that is), the party must take on a number of roles, 
reflecting their responsibilities throughout the voyage. Tak-
ing a certain role is done before the start of a voyage and 
remains until the end, unless otherwise stated.
If the party do not have sufficient numbers to cover all the 
primary roles of running ship, or if they do not wish to take 
on one of the roles, they will have to find (usually hire) 
someone to do the job.
Helmsman: The Helmsman is responsible for handling the 
steering wheel of a ship. Whether the helmsman holds the 
wheel himself or herself, or instructs another character to 
do so under his or her guidance, it is the helmsman’s skill 
and knowledge that will keep a ship off the rocks, allow it 
to maneuver at the right time, follow the flow of the waters 
and the wind to sail faster to their destination.
Navigator: The Navigator is the ship’s guide and instructor. 
He or she plots the ship’s course, reads the nautical charts, 
the sun and stars, and sets the intended path that will wind 
along the seaways to take the ship to its destination. A Nav-
igator also interprets the will of the winds and advises the 
Helmsman on which direction to take to avoid the treach-
ery and wrath of waves seeking to throw them off course.
Quartermaster: The Quartermaster, in many ways, en-
sures the safety of the ship during the voyage. He or she 
organizes the shifts of the crew, is responsible for fending 
off an attack by pirates or sea-creatures, and manages lo-
gistical matters, such as supplies. A good Quartermaster 
should be prudent, and seek to avoid trouble, rather than 
confront it.
Naval Hexes
Immortals uses a naval hex system to aid GMs and players 
in their adventures. The seas are divided into hexagons, 
which determine the special conditions that affect a ship’s 
passage. Each hexagon includes 3 values (Handling, Navi-
gation, Enccounter), each one bearing a specific value.
The process by which a ship passes through a hexagon and 
proceeds to the next one during its journey is referred to 
as a Naval Round. A Naval Round consists of three phases 
which relate to the values given above. These phases are:
Handling Phase. Here, the Helmsman makes a check to 
steer through the waters, 	 avoiding natural hazards pres-
ent in the area. Failing on the check 	means the ship is 
thrown off-course. Either way, proceed to the Navigation 
Phase.
Navigation Phase. Here, the Navigator makes a check to 
plot the course of the ship, 	determining which hex it will 
reach at the start of the next Naval Round. Failing on the 
check means the ship follows the wrong course, which the 
Navigator may or may not realize. Either way, proceed to 
the Encounter Phase.
Encounter Phase. Here, the Quartermaster makes a check 
to avoid the living perils of the sea – pirates and monsters 
of the deep. Failing on the check means it’s time to roll Ini-
tiative! Once the Phase ends, a new Naval Round begins.
Handling (roll d20 + Seafaring + Ship Maneuverability): 
The handling value of a hexagon represents how difficult 
and dangerous it is to navigate due to its geomorphological 
elements. This includes various obstacles, such as rocky 
waters, shallow reefs, narrow passages and so on. As is 
shown in the map above, the hexagons located in the sea 
lanes have less difficulty as the sea lanes are drawn right 
on the optimal and safe routes.
The Handling Check is made solely by the person holding 
the Helmsman role against the indicated DC. The Han-
dling Check is a d20 roll which adds the Helmsman’s Wis-
dom (Seafaring) modifier + the Ship’s Maneuverability. A 
successful roll leads sailing to phase 2 which is Navigation 
while a failed roll requires the Helmsman to make a skatter 
roll. 
The skatter die is a special die with direction markings. A 
failed roll means that the Helmsman has temporarily lost 
control of the ship and it has found itself going in a differ-
ent direction than the helmsman originally intended. The 
ship is moved to the adjacent hexagon indicated by the die, 
before proceeding to the navigation phase.


26
For example, if an area has a listed Handling value of 15, 
it means that to successfully cross the hexagon the Helms-
man rolls a Wisdom (Seafaring) check and adds the ship’s 
maneuverability to the result. If the sum is greater than 15, 
the Handling Check is considered successful. If the total is 
less than 15, the Helmsman rolls the skatter die and moves 
the ship on the map to an adjacent hexagon in the direction 
of the skatter die.
Navigation (roll d20 + Nature): The Navigation value in a 
hexagon represents how difficult it is for the Navigator to 
maintain the correct course to his or her destination. This 
can mean interpreting the direction and strength of the 
wind, reading the sky or other signs, identifying the ship-
ping line or even more specialized signs for new uncharted 
waters. The Navigation Check is done exclusively by the 
one with the role of Navigator targeting the listed DC. The 
Navigation Check is a d20 check which adds the Naviga-
tor’s Nature Skill + any material that can provide assistance 
to the navigator (eg a map, compass etc.). A successful roll 
takes the sailing to Phase 3, which is Encounter while a 
failed roll requires the Helmsman to make a skatter roll.
Unlike Handling, on a failed Navigation roll, it is not nec-
essary that its failure be noticed. A different environment 
than expected, or washing up in an area that the ship 
shouldn’t have landed in can lead to conclusions but only 
with a successful navigation roll in the next hexagon can 
the Navigator realize they are off course.
In both cases of failed roll (Handling & Navigation), if the 
ship ends up in a hex with land, an island or islets , the ship 
takes damage equal to its speed, as it slams uncontrollably 
into the rock. If the total damage exceeds the ship’s durabil-
ity then it sinks or is completely destroyed.
Encounter (roll d20 + Warfare): The Encounter value in 
a hexagon represents how likely a ship is to be attacked 
as it passes through the hex. Whether it is mere pirates, 
or some other chthonic creature that lives or happens to 
be passing through the area, the ship can become a target 
and it’s the Quartermaster’s job to avoid or deal with the 
danger. The higher the Encounter value in a hexagon, the 
more dangerous an area is for passing ships. 
As you’ll notice on the hexed map, hexes crossed by trade 
routes have a higher risk of being attacked, as pirates are 
known to often come with ships full of cargo. At the same 
time, several unexplored areas or very deep waters are also 
dangerous and usually attract creatures that can descend 
to great depths to avoid sunlight.

Adventure Overview
In the sacred yet beleaguered island of Leros, once favored 
by Artemis, a dire call for help echoes through the air. With 
crops failing and famine looming, the islanders have sent 
for aid from the Confederacy of Thebes. A seasoned tri-
reme, the Melibea, laden with grain, wine and cured lamb, 
was dispatched weeks ago, but it has vanished without a 
trace. 
The players answer the call to investigate the fate of the 
Melibea, journeying to Lebinthus, a naval trading outpost. 
However, as they approach the port, a cataclysmic storm 
erupts, hurling their ship onto the jagged shores of Lebin-
thus. There, they discover the wreckage of the Melibea, its 
cargo lost, its crew gone. 
As they navigate the treacherous wilderness in search of 
survivors, they encounter the true horrors unleashed by the 
breach of Tartarus. Battling twisted spawn from the abyss, 
they make their way to the ruined town of Lebinthus, where 
a few souls remain, led by the captain of the Melibea. He 
reveals that the storm was no mere tempest, but a breach 
to Tartarus itself, sealing the island in a curse. 
With the surrounding waters teeming with deadly crea-
tures and lethal waves, the players face a harrowing choice: 
fight against the relentless horrors or sail away aboard a 
battered ship that awaits in the harbor, and pray they steer 
clear of the vortex conjured during the storm.
Chapter 1-The Woes of Leros
The adventure begins on the sacred island of Leros, once a 
peaceful haven under the watchful eye of Artemis, goddess 
of the hunt, which has fallen into desperate times. After the 
Titans and their horrid spawn re-emerged from Tartarus 
and entered the mortal world, the entire Hellenic Peninsu-
la struggled to survive. 
Leros, one of the few still habitable islands, survived 
through trade with Anatolia and the Greek mainland, hunt-
ing and farming. In recent months, however, the island’s 
crops have withered, game has grown scarce, and famine 
casts its shadow over the people. In their time of need, Le-
ros called upon the merchant guilds of the Confederacy 
of Thebes, an oligarchical city-state and the foremost eco-
nomic power in the Peninsula. Salvation seemed close at 
hand. 
The Melibea, a mighty trireme, embarked from Thebes, 
carrying food and wine. Yet, two long months have passed, 
and the ship—along with its precious cargo—has vanished 
without a trace. The people of Leros are nearing starva-
tion. Some have even abandoned the sanctuary of the is-
land and tried to find hope elsewhere, in other settlements 
and city-states, risking their lives in the transit. 
Now, the island’s survival hangs by a thread, and the lo-
cal Archons Council, the leaders of Leros, have issued a 
reward to anyone who steps up to assist their people. A 
chance for anyone who wishes to be remembered to earn 
fame and to make a living.
The council hired a Mythonaut Satyr named Marsyas to 
lead the voyage. The rest of the party needs to meet him 
in Phoebe’s Kapeleion, a popular local tavern to plan the 
journey. He will be accompanying the party on their quest 
as a guide, played by the GM.
The Island of Leros
Leros is a large island with three main settlements: the Port 
Town of Lakkion, which functions as the capital settlement 
in the island center; the village of Drimon on the north side; 
and the Temple of Artemis, a fortified holy site to the south. 
Leros has the classic architecture found in most islands, 
with leveling white-stone houses and establishments form-
ing a “theater” up a hill overseeing the harbor. 
Leros doesn’t have fortifications with the exception of a 
watchtower in the harbor that also functions as a light-
house. There is also a large structure uphill that serves as 
the council chambers and militia barracks. Across the port 
town, one can see various statues and murals dedicated to 
the Goddess of the Hunt, matron of the island. The island 
is home to large forests and low hills and plains, hosting a 
variety of fauna, from deadly vipers to wild boars and deer. 
Both Lakkion and Drimon have farmlands, now withered, 
yielding minimal harvest. 
Grove Tenders from Anatolia attribute the scarcity of ani-
mals in the island’s hunting grounds to the Olympians’ war 
against the Titans intensifying, likely distracting the God-
dess. But this is small comfort to the locals…

GM’s Note: Roleplaying the Dead
Death affects both mind and soul. In time, the dead come 
to feel disconnected from life, and their desire to return to 
the world of the living fades. Though this does not happen 
fast enough to influence the characters, they do instantly 
feel the tug of death. However they may wish to return to 
the realm of the living, they do feel a subtle, but pervasive, 
sense of connectedness with Hades.
Feel free to communicate feelings of wistfulness, distract-
edness, and the beginning stages of a strange apathy to the 
party. How they feel about their death is entirely on them, 
but they do know that if they stay here too long, they may 
never leave.
Kalmyra
None living can enter Hades without first reaching Kalmy-
ra, as per the ancient protocol the Gods set for the afterlife. 
Thus, those who remain unburied are doomed to dwell 
there for all eternity as pariahs, belonging neither to the 
living nor the dead.
As the players look around, leaving the banks of Ache-
ron, they take in Kalmyra in full. It is a small town with 
few buildings, mostly built of weathered grey stone, some 
embedded in the towering, cavernous walls that frame the 
pier on either side. The rest dot the granite-like rocky land-
scape. Disturbingly, a few of the buildings are made out of 
still-writhing human flesh, taken from once-living heroes 
that foolishly dared descend into Hades for glory or other 
reasons.
The wretches that dwell in Kalmyra are a vicious lot. 
Dressed in tattered grey, the dead of Kalmyra look as one 
does who has not drunk water in a long time. With dry skin, 
parched lips and sunken eyes, they are halfway between 
pitiful and repugnant. These damned have created a small 
society, with the mightiest imposing their will upon their 
lesser fellows. As the party enter the town, they see men 
and women in shackles, toiling with pickaxes, shovels and 
other tools under the gaze of their oppressors, or else sim-

The Crone of Kalmyra
Should the characters visit the Whispering Wall and start 
searching for the Crone they may find her in a hut made of 
flesh and stone in the outskirts of Kalmyra.
There are many rumors surrounding this figure. By ask-
ing around, one can learn of a woman practicing dark 
magicks, for which she was cursed by the Gods upon her 
death. Another rumor states that this Crone was always 
here in Kalmyra, even before the first denizens – the first 
coinless dead. Another states that the Crone was a Titan 
or is related to one.
No definitive answer describes the origins of the Crone 
but what they all agree upon is that no one who met her 
was ever found again. The dead of Kalmyra never visit her 
or approach her hut.The Crone’s hut is a horrifying amal-
gam of animal bones, human flesh and mortar made of the 
ashen sands of Acheron. Its architecture is crooked and 
there is no obvious evidence that someone actually resides 
there. The hut is located on an obsidian pillar with a series 
of stone steps imbued in the rock, leading up to it.
Upon reaching the Crone’s hut, the players can spot a 
small garden of asphodels surrounding it. A DC 12 Intel-
ligence (Mythos: Gods) check can remind the players of 
the Asphodel Fields located past the Acheron River, one of 
three places where souls would spend eternity after their 
judgement, alongside Elysium and Tartarus. Soon after 
their arrival, the players will hear a soft elderly voice ap-
proaching from inside the hut. 
A toothless old lady steps out. Her visage is uncanny: the 
irises of her eyes are faded and have a misty white tone. 
She is missing various fingers and is almost bald, save for 
a few, oily, grey hair locks. She says: “This one doesn’t have 
many visitors. What is it you seek here, friends?”
