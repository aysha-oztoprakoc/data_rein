Castles in the Sky: Cloud casting at the TTRPG Table
by gothHoblin

Those of us who play 5th Edition and are familiar with the cleric class will also be familiar with the Augury spell (PHB p.215). A second level spell, it enables the caster to contact an "otherworldly entity" in order to help them make an imminent decision (interestingly, this usage implies it doesn't need to be their deity). Utilising some form of divination tool such as cards, runes, bones or anything else the player chooses for their character, after casting the spell they are able to interpret the results as an omen telling of a good outcome, bad outcome, an outcome that is both, or neither.
Of course, like many things present in our games, augury itself did not originate within the minds of the writers of a world-famous tabletop roleplaying game. It is an ancient religious practice where a practitioner (known as an augur or auspex) would observe and interpret the movement and behaviour of birds to receive their omens, which were widely understood to convey the will of the gods.

Though it is commonly linked to the classical era of Ancient Greece and Rome, augury is also thought of as being older than Rome itself, attributed by some historians to the Etruscans, and even the Old Testament mentions it being used by the Philistines (Isaiah 2:6). However, the famous example of Romulus and Remus using augury as a means to determine where to build the city of Rome has done much to cement it as a part of Roman culture.

Since these ancient practices, the word augury has expanded to take on a more general meaning of divination, or by extension, the omen or prophecy itself. This gives way to a much wider range of fortune telling methodologies to the practice, and as we return back to the concept of augury in TTRPGs, makes for a much more customisable spellcasting. Not to mention, of course, that not all locations our characters visit will have birds in them to watch!

The spell itself states that the divination tools the character uses are open to variety; the mentioned bones, cards, and sticks are merely suggestions. So what other ancient practices could we use for this spell (or something similar in a different system) that would work for our particular flavour of cleric? 

One example might be bibliomancy, which is what I use for my own Arcana domain cleric, who has a love of academia and the study of magical theory. A Death domain cleric may choose to use bone-throwing or ossomancy. A Forge domain cleric might be interested in plumbomancy, which is divination by interpreting the shapes that molten lead forms when poured into water. Another example, which is similar to the original concept in that it requires our augur to lift their gaze to the skies, is that of nephomancy, or cloud casting, which could be a favourite of the Tempest, Nature, Light or even Twilight domains.

Gazing at clouds and picking out shapes is something familiar to many people, a fond childhood memory or simple pleasure that many of us still enjoy as adults. People look to the sky instinctively for many reasons - to attempt to predict changes in weather, to figure out whether they should bring their cattle or laundry inside, to pray, in relief, to find a sense of calm, or when things don't go according to plan. It is a popular concept among many religions that the gods live above us, and so it makes sense that when attempting to find guidance from them, we might look towards where they dwell. 

Nephromancy is as much about the types of the clouds themselves as it is the shapes presented within them, and provides a rich foundation for a DM to describe the omens a querying character may receive, allowing for extra creativity beyond the simple one-liners that the spell description provides. I've provided a few examples with their descriptions, and what they could mean if spotted as a result of casting such a spell.

Altocumulus 
Omen: Good results
Altocumulus clouds, or cloudlets, are small, fluffy, patchy clouds that are arranged in such a way that they almost look like fleece. In weather, they may either bring showers or break apart to create room for the sun to shine. When seen in response to a casting of the augury spell they communicate good results, for the worst that can happen is that one gets only briefly wet. They indicate to the querent that this path may bring about some uncertainty, but that ultimately they are the ones with the power, for they can create rain or sun through their choices.

Aurora
Omen: Good results
Auroras are stunning displays of light in the sky. Able to present in a range of colours, they are often a unique, never to be repeated experience that inspires awe in the viewer. These breathtaking curtains, rays and flickers usually appear in a dark sky, but don't let this hold you back - after all, these are gods we're talking about. 
Seeing an aurora communicates exceptional results to the querent. It is indicative of the chosen action being, or resulting, in a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity and should not be ignored. You might even want to go into further detail and ascribe further meaning to the particular colours that appear.

Arcus 
Omen: Bad results
Arcus clouds are a spectacular cloud formation, consisting of a thick, clearly defined shelf of cloud that quickly rolls in at the approach of a storm, and is often backed by high winds. Though they are awe-inspiring to see, they are often attached to a raging cumulonimbus and bring severe, swift and dangerous weather conditions with them. 
Spotting an arcus cloud in response to an augury spell indicates bad results, particularly pertaining to personal safety.

Castellanus
Omen: Bad results
Castellanus clouds resemble castle turrets, with tall formations that can be present among many different cloud types. They generally form where the atmosphere is unstable, and may also be associated with lightning, which arcs between the uppermost protrusions, jumping from cloud to cloud. The appearance of these clouds on an otherwise fine day indicates a developing storm; in response to an augury spell, much like the idiom 'castles in the sky', they should be taken as a warning not to place your faith in the chosen action, for it is unrealistic and has very little chance of succeeding.

Calvus
Omen: Good and bad results
Tall clouds with puffy tops that somewhat resemble cauliflower, calvus clouds are a part of the cumulonimbus family - the "big bads" of the cloud world - but have not yet reached their potential. They are a much lighter version and though they have the capability to develop into heavier rain clouds, they usually bring much lighter rain in the form of showers. 
Calvus clouds in response to an augury spell indicate both good and bad results, should the  chosen course of action be carried out.

Cirrus
Omen: Neither good nor bad
Wispy and delicate, Cirrus clouds almost look like feathers in the sky, with many thin strands. They are bright white in colour during the day, but when seen at sunrise or sunset will often take on the colours of the sun as it sinks below or rises above the horizon. 
These clouds indicate a change in the weather is imminent, and when seen after an augury casting indicate neither good nor bad results, as the future is changeable, creative, and inevitable.

Altostratus 
Omen: Unclear results
Altostratus clouds are thin layers of grey cloud that form a lightly overcast sky. They are not so thick as to obscure the light of the sun or moon behind them, but instead appear flat and hazy. They do not let enough light through to create strong shadows. It seems that they are light enough to not bring rain, but at the same time they stand as a precursor to darker skies, and thus may trick you with their uncertainty. These clouds indicate unclear results.

Celtic Cryptology: Incorporating Ogam respectfully into our games
by Penny Blake

Many people today believe that Ogam is an ancient Druidic tree alphabet used by the Celts in divination and other magical practices. This has lead to Ogam being used in gaming and fantasy fiction in a way that lacks authenticity and is disrespectful to those of Gaelic heritage. It's a great shame as the truth about Ogam, its origins and uses, actually makes it perfect for including in RPG and fantasy worlds, providing we separate the fact from the fiction first…

WHAT IS OGAM?
Ogam (Old Irish) or Ogham (Modern Irish) is the earliest written record of the Gaelic languages and its usage dates from around the 4th Century AD/CE, when it consisted of 20 characters, each constructed from a group of parallel lines (a further five characters were added about a hundred years later.)

The characters of ogam are written in relation to a base-line and read vertically and it was most commonly carved onto hard surfaces, such as standing stones, in order to mark the boundary of territories and commemorate important events such as battles and the deaths of famous public figures.

In manuscript form, its use was largely cryptographic and poets, law students, and medical students were all expected to master the use of its many variants as part of their earliest training. One of the most famous and visually stunning examples of Ogam manuscript is in Lebor Ogaim (The Book of Ogam),  which can be found in the fourteenth-century educational text 'The Book of Ballymote.' and dates from around 1390.

While some of the characters are named after trees, many are not and one ancient text even states that they were named after the students who particularly excelled at learning and using them. Despite this fact, Ogam is still commonly assumed to be a magical tree alphabet in modern popular culture, and this inaccuracy often carries into RPGs and Fantasy fiction.

OGAM AND DRUIDRY
Amongst scholars and historians, the popular association between Ogam and a 'Celtic Tree Calendar,' or Druidry in general, is widely accepted to be an erroneous one, first hinted at by Ruaidhrí Ó Flaithbheartaigh, in his book Ogygia - an  Irish history, published in 1685, which was loudly criticised by his contemporaries.

However, Robert Graves decided to base his pseudo-academic book – The White Goddess, published in 1948  - largely around  Ó Flaithbheartaigh's ideas and it is to him that we now owe the modern association of Ogam with ancient Celtic magic.
Despite the fact that Graves freely admits that his work is fictional and that his ogam theories were speculative at best, the belief that Ogam was originally a Tree Calendar linked to the cycles of the moon, and druidic nature-based magic in general, remains popular in modern Pagan and New Age circles.

A more scholarly study of the possible link between Ogam and Druidry has been conducted by Niall MacCoitir, in 2003, although his findings are interesting and sometimes very persuasive, even he concludes that the actual evidence is insufficient to draw a solid conclusion on the matter.

Therefore, despite the fact that many people today incorporate ogam into their magical practices, as players and game designers it's important to keep in mind that ogam is an ancient Gaelic written language, part of the cultural heritage of people of Gaelic descent, and that there is no conclusive evidence for it being used in any form of ancient magic.

CAN WE USE OGAM IN OUR GAMES?
Yes! (But let's do it right). Many RPGs incorporate Ogam and some do it more respectfully and authentically than others. If you would like to incorporate Ogam into your games, whether you are designing your own or using an established system, here are some ideas to help you do so in a way that is both authentic and respectful.

Time Period
Ogam was in use around the 4th century AD/ CE so it's an excellent fit to include as a written language for any game set in or around this time period, or even fantasy settings inspired by this era of Celtic history.

Origins
Ogam is the earliest form of written Gaelic that has been discovered but exactly how and why it developed is not clear. It's use in cryptography might suggest that it was developed in order to be able to conceal meaning from those who were fluent only in latin, but this is merely speculation.

Cyphers and Cryptography
One thing we know for certain about Ogam is that it was a cypher used prolifically in a wide range of Irish medieval educational texts and was commonly used by students in their note taking as well. This makes it a perfect tool to work into a game as a secret language or code which players may either have to break, or could perhaps learn and use. To this end, there are several excellent Ogam fonts and translators online (see links at the end of this article).
Standing Stones

Another indisputable fact, is that ogam was commonly carved into standing stones to mark the boundary of territories and to commemorate important events and people, so including standing stones marked with Ogam text in a game would be very authentic.
There are hundreds of standing stones and manuscripts which contain ogam but here are a few of the most famous/impressive examples. Provided we consider the context, there is no harm in referencing specific, non-religious texts and artefacts in a game.

Magic
Magic spells in games often contain a written element and it may be tempting to use ogam in that regard, especially in a Celtic or medieval-themed setting. However, as there is no substantial evidence to suggest that ogam was ever used as part of any system of magical or religious practice, to do so would lack authenticity and may even be considered disrespectful or otherwise problematic.

Props
Props always enhance our gaming spaces and ogam lends itself readily to being used in this way. The arrangement of simple strokes in easy to mark onto a hard surface such as wooden disks, staffs, sticks or pebbles, for example, to create tactile resources for your players to engage with.


GAMES WHICH INCLUDE OGAM
In light of the information given so far, I'd now like to invite you to look critically at the following games to decide for yourself how authentically and respectfully they have included ogam in their lore and mechanics. 

Some do it very well indeed while others could make some improvements. This isn't an invitation to be unkind, to make harsh judgements or to engage in bullying behaviour, it is an encouragement for us all to practise engaging with games with our 'eyes open' in regard to sensitivity issues and to learn and grow as a community.

Ancient Artifacts: System Agnostic Magic Items

Helm of the Ferocious Roar 
by Thogreer

Whether the ancient warlord, Viaquin Xiar, led loyal followers, recruited troops, or hired mercenaries in battle, tales persist of frightening sounds emanating from the frontal assault. A mighty cry, resembling the roar of a lion, barraged the ears of all on the battlefield. The sound weakened the resolve of Xiar's enemies, but emboldened allies for a time. Often, such an advantage could turn the tide of an encounter, even amidst the most dangerous of foes.

Once per day, the wearer of the helm may produce an enormous roar sounding like a fierce beast of their choice. The roar penetrates non-magical barriers, and can only be avoided entirely by those who are temporarily or permanently deafened. Enemies without the willpower to withstand such an affront will suffer the effects of a great fear and flee, and even those who withstand the initial shock, temporarily lose the will to attack the wearer. Allies able to hear the roar will temporarily become emboldened, and immune to becoming frightened by any others for a time.


Darumjal's Curse
by Thogreer

Only one of the 13 ceremonial daggers of Darumjal has ever left the evil deity’s temple walls. 

Stolen by a fierce warrior looking to enact revenge against the cult of Darumjal’s worshipers for their misdeeds, it accomplished its purpose to destroy the wielder’s enemies, but did so at a terrible price. For each time the warrior used the dagger in battle, a tiny piece of their vitality seemed to drain away from them. So possessed by their thirst for revenge and bloodshed, the warrior continued to earn victory after victory until wiping out every living member of Darumjal’s cult and every other worshiper they could find. Only in dispatching their final victim had enough of their vitality diminished that their body collapsed, never to rise again.

Darumjal’s Curse remains a viable tool to achieve one’s aims, yet still enacts that heavy price. Utilizing the weapon in battle yields an enhanced destructive effect, where the damage escalates upon each successful attack. If the dagger is used to strike a fatal blow to an enemy, however, a portion of the wielder’s life essence is permanently lost, where the more powerful the enemy, the greater the cost.


Tablet of the Fates
by Neil aka. Fey Earth

A cuneiform-styled tablet carved from a single slab of lapis lazuli, the stone's origin lies unknown beyond the stories that whisper its discovery by the gods before the very time of creation. Though legends persist that the writing contains wisdom of these gods, no mortal has found the talent or understanding to read it. Over the generations, the tablet has become a sign of royalty, and historically, whoever possesses it can claim a divine right as king or queen. However, the tablet's true power lies in its ability of its wielder to rewrite fate itself.

Once per day, the user can change any recent event for its outcome to become whatever they wish. In doing so, one taps the power of the gods - an act beyond the capabilities of mortal minds to grasp. When using this power, the user's willpower will be tested to some extent. The greater the change made, the higher the difficulty of the test, where failure allows the knowledge of the gods to overcome one's mortal mind, besetting them with madness. Perhaps more distressing, failure of the test transforms some of the tablet's energy into a beacon for all celestial or infernal beings who wish to possess this power for themselves.


Khopesh of Conviction
by Thogreer

This ceremonial blade given to ancient rulers at the time of their ascendance is said to grant powers of persuasion and enticement to whomever carries it. During times of strife between noble families, warring nations, or even supernatural conflicts, whoever possessed the khopesh could enhance their ability to solve conflict with marvelous diplomacy. In those rare instances where words failed to do so, however, the blade served to send another message, that extreme might backed up the driving force behind its owner.  Representatives could be sent into faraway lands with nothing but the blade and a few meager resources, yet return with declarations of peace between nations - sometimes with written agreements, sometimes with the head of the rival sovereign authority.

The wielder of this blade may enhance a single skill that can be used to help convince others to go along with their desires so long as they maintain possession of it. This may take the form of a more articulable persuasion or a threat of force, as best suits the wielder. Once per day, if the blade is utilized in combat, the wielder may attempt to invoke an end to the encounter by testing their enhanced ability and convincing their opponent that working together will be more beneficial than continuing the fight.


Amulet of Godly Intent
by Thogreer

Crafted by a wily lesser god seeking to gain a foothold of power amidst a great pantheon, this amulet was imbued with the power to foresee a future choice from any being, from the lowliest insect to the mightiest deity. Whether such information can actually be used to change the future or simply confirms what is already to be, none can say. Since it was ultimately lost or abandoned by its creator, surely there are limitations on its ultimate power.

Each day, the wearer of the amulet may accurately predict a choice that will be made by another. They need only speak the choice aloud to instantly know the outcome. If posing a choice that will never be encountered, no answer will come and the ability to predict is lost for the day. While the outcome of a choice can be predicted, the details, timing, or consequences can not so easily be determined.

The amulet requires practice to remain viable at its predicting power. If the wearer fails to pose a predictable choice, the amulet must be recharged before it can be used again. If the wielder fails to recharge the amulet for three consecutive days, the amulet will never work for that wearer again, but may be passed on to another.


Icosahedron of Shifting Devotion
by Thogreer

When a scholar discovered this 20-sided stone carving, they recognized many of the symbols as names of the gods. After considerable research, others were deciphered as lesser-known members of the pantheon, but despite attempts by historians and theologians, some names remained unrecognizable. One day, the object was placed too hastily upon its resting place and gently rolled over with one of the unknown names facing upward. Almost immediately a strange being arrived, claiming ownership of the symbol and offering amazing truths. The next day, the unrecognized deity and the item disappeared, leaving only a warning that it could be quite powerful and dangerous in the wrong hands.

If rolled, the wielder may summon the deity whose name or symbol appears on the upward side. The symbols will always reflect members of a pantheon within the plane where it resides. While summoned, the deity may provide advice, powers, perspective on the nature of existence to the wielder, or any information related to their domain. Speaking only in truth, they may do so in ways that the summoner does not understand, and follow no orders of any other kind. Such immense concentration is required by the deity during this period that any actions or spells that rely upon the deity’s power are suspended for the entire plane until they have been dismissed, one day has passed, or the icosahedron is rolled by another.

The Old Gods
by Ben Newbon

Whilst many fictional worlds have their own deities interwoven into their settings, many take inspiration from ancient cultures from the real world. Even amongst them, there are many that have uncanny similarities and sometimes just straight up crossovers! When you’re creating worlds and settings for your games there are a plethora of examples you can borrow from, whether you want a handful of mighty powers or entire family trees with their own intermingling histories. Take a look here through some short summaries of the oldest cultures from Earth’s history and see what inspirations you can take for your own creations.

Greek
The pantheon of Ancient Greece is an entire universe of its own! The family trees are all over the place and the dramas are unending. A notable feature of the Greek gods is just how directly they interacted with the human world, creating many many demi-gods during their liaisons. Not all gods have to be aloof and above it all - will yours come to visit the characters in your games in person? What aftermath do they leave behind them when they do?

Egyptian
Ancient Egypt had a relatively stable deific system descending from the Creator God Atum with children like the Sun God Ra and the Underworld God Anubis but there were well over 1500 minor gods beyond the big names many know like Isis or Horus. These gods all had set responsibilities and interests, sometimes overlapping with others, which covered literally every aspect of ancient life and many are still revered to this day. Do your characters have some very specific difficulties in their duties? Who’s the god of gardening in your world? Is there a god of handbaskets?

Norse
Interestingly, in Nordic mythology the gods were not the first beings but came after the giants and even then there were two distinct families of gods. Many modern retellings of these stories have exposed people to some of the tales of Odin and his sons, but the lore of the Æsir and Vanir runs deep. As with many pantheons, the dramas were varied and ongoing but the number of these gods are relatively few, so it’s not too overbearing to follow. What is the history of your gods like? Are there rivalries? Deific wars? How do their interdeity conflicts affect the world?

Hindu
Tripartites are common in religions and the ancient Hindu trio of Gods of Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva head up a collection of thousands with various names and avatars. Brahma is the God of Creation, Vishnu preservation and Shiva the destroyer, forming a codependent cycle with a delicate balance. Do your gods work together? Do they team up or rely on each other for their differing strengths? Do their appearances and how they’re referred to change as you travel to distinct regions of the world?

Aztec
Some cultures structure their deities less as hierarchies and more represented as layers of the world - for the Aztecs and the pan-Mesoamerican cultures preceding them, this took the form of the heavens (Taloc the Storm God), the world of humans and nature and the underworld(Tlaltechutli, the earth monster). When fleshing out the gods in your game consider in which plane they might reside. Are they above, looking down? Are the scheming from the underworld? Do they walk amongst the people or hide in the lands themself?

Celtic
Gods are often mixed in with the Earth and nature, particularly in more druidic leaning cultures like the Celts and Gauls. Life, after all, must surely be the work of higher beings, like the antlered Celtic Forest God, Cernunnos, or Airmed the Goddess of Herbal Healing. There is certainly plenty of domain within nature for gods to preside over, for example the Sun Goddess, Grian, or Sea God, Manannán. Do your gods look after natural growths? Are they responsible for the passing of the day? Of all time? How intertwined are they with the natural processes of your world?

Babylonian
Incorporating some of the oldest writings and cultures in human history, the Babylonian pantheon has many recognisable tales and names, like the great flood and Tiamat. Significant to the Babylonian culture was the importance of physical worship and items like statues and idols, which conferred power and reverence to their gods. When crafting the world around your gods, think about how their people treat them. Is active worship common? Are there effigies to them? Do their physical representations hold real power in the world?

Chinese
In ancient Chinese tradition there is a concept of a universal god and while there are many names used to refer to them depending on the context, the idea is that their god of Heaven is an omnipresent divinity. Of course there is a complex hierarchy of deities and various other immortals below that, incorporating different aspects depending on the region, but often with themes of balance and the elements. How ubiquitous are the deities in your game? Are they all connected? Over what do they have power? Are they all-knowing, all-seeing?

Japanese
Most creation stories centre around chaos becoming order in some manner and in Japan’s the first plant becomes the first god, bringing with it an agricultural vibe to their pantheon. Many of the Kami, as they are referred to in Japanese, are treated like a kind of spirit energy, sometimes considered the manifestations of ancient ancestors or clans as well as being part of nature itself. Consider in your game how the gods came about? What forms them and what do they draw their power from?

The Shadows of Runevakt
by Ailis Williams

An eerie mist shrouds Runevakt, whispered about by locals. It has been so long that hardly anyone remembers the names of those who once manipulated and twisted the energies there, leaving nothing but darkness and terror emanating from the lonely burial mound in the secluded clearing. It's been an eternity since anyone could utter the names of those who desecrated the grave. The elders claim that a curse follows anyone daring to enter the ancient resting place.

Those who defy the horror and venture into both the clearing and the grave are met with a gruesome sight. Bloody drag marks, decaying bones, and body parts suggest the ordeal suffered by the unfortunate. Ancient books and scribbled records point to those who, whether ages ago or recently— who can say for sure — engaged in nefarious activities and communicated with dark forces.

DM NOTES
Encourage players to test their occult knowledge. Those well-versed immediately notice that the clearing's shape mirrors an ancient magical rune. As per tradition, it signifies karma, secrets, the potency of destiny, and occult wisdom.
Challenge your players' courage. Entering the clearing induces shivers; approaching the burial mound seems impossible due to past horrors.

Incomplete body parts raise questions — weapons for goblins or transformed creatures waiting in the shadows?
Records reveal the sect/group/order's plans. Players may decipher a remorseful member's diary.
Use this location for a one-shot or an adventure pursuing an ancient demon; your choices shape the tale 🖤

The Perils of Magical Archaeology
by Corey Davies

Lost temples, undercities buried beneath a thriving metropolis, or when they go grave robbing for perfectly noble purposes - in any number of TTRPGs the party delves amongst the ancient wonders of a long-dead culture. 

When they do, they may find themselves in possession of ancient and powerful artefacts which belong to an ancient and unstudied form of magic. There are players who love to unravel mysteries and players who love to access impressive and unexpected new powers. All of them are sure to enjoy finding items of archaic magic - and the complications which come along with that.

There are the old reliable complications which a party may face any time they pick up something of value - curses, NPCs who want it for themselves, and so on. However, when players uncover something ancient and forgotten then tailoring the peril they face to what they find really enhances the narrative.

Unfamiliar Magic
One of the most captivating things about ancient cultures unearthed in the real world is what rituals they performed and why. In a TTRPG filled with spells and rituals which give players the power to change the universe around them this kind of mystery is doubly tantalising because they come away with a new power after uncovering it.

When players unearth a long lost ritual or spell it can tell them about the world as well as give them an interesting and unique new power to use. When they find ancient magic they can enact themselves this is the clearest place you can draw from real world archaeology in how the party will be able to relate to their findings - we don’t know exactly what exact significance a lot of ancient rituals had, but we can get a rough idea from the context they’re found in and what else we know about the culture.

Keeping this in mind, a thematic complication for players to encounter with new magic learned from ancient sites would be to have the translation the players think they have be slightly wrong. They might know that the magic draws power from a divine source, only to find out it’s actually draining a god as they use it, causing widespread issues for their worshippers and a shift in the balance of power in a pantheon which are bound to affect the party.

Treasure
As well as abstract magics carved into walls, ancient magic is also often imbued into an item of value, left behind, and long forgotten. Players love to find new and interesting gear to wear when they pick through ruins and it gives a chance for some truly unique descriptions of items.

Perils specific to treasure taken from ancient sites are best derived from the fact they’ve been left undiscovered for so long. No matter whether a sword of ancient providence or a set of rune engraved gems, there’s a reason they’ve been left behind.

Whilst they might be connected to a great evil or a terrible curse, things taken away from ancient sites also clearly identify the people as having strayed into ancient ruins. The consequences of this might be less tangible than direct harm from using an item but people who recognise the provenance may avoid, ostracise, or even attack the party because of it.

Keeping it fun
When including any consequences it’s important to keep in mind that players are there to have fun, and whilst being horribly punished for meddling in ancient magic might make a good horror movie, it’s no fun to unexpectedly become that protagonist.
When you’re deciding on how ancient magic is going to backfire, be sure to think about what the players enjoy about their character, and be sure the consequences don’t take that away from them. Ideally the way ancient magic they uncover causes problems should present them with an interesting choice about whether to use the benefits of the magic despite the consequences.

It doesn’t matter what kind of world we live in. Give a little to the magic of old, why don’t you? See what it gives back.

Designed for any system, choose one of the four ancient elements and then roll 1d6 on each column to determine what is to be given, and what might be gained. Remember, the more that is given, the greater the reward. 

The Soil
The Soil beneath our feet is stable and nourishing. Its demands are physical, laborious, and borne of toil. But the gifts it gives are rewards ripe for the picking.

The Soil demands…
1 - The soil shifts hungrily for a taste of that warm blood which sustains you, so that it too may be sustained…
2 - A lock of hair, some nail clippings, even a tooth or two can prove a tasty treat as they rot into the filth…

4 - Sow the seeds. Till the land. A single sprout or an entire field, your sweat and patience will be rewarded.

The Embers always hunger, and take ruthlessly. It dances wherever it is called and leaves soot, smoke, and devastation in its wake. But it is a powerful force to wield if it is pleased.

The Embers demand…
1 - A child’s drawing. A lover’s locket. A meaningful token of any kind can be fed to the Embers. Its loss fuels their generosity.
2- Stoke the flames. An hour, a day, a week. Let it flare and crackle for just a little longer. Don’t let it die.

Pentacles and Paganism
by Jodie Boyce

Often misconstrued as a sign of misfortune, evil and sometimes even “Satan”, pentacles are quite the opposite. Pentacles or pentagrams have been used as far back as Sumer, Liangzhu and Ancient Greece - sometimes as a symbol of the gods, sometimes as a sign of charity and well-being. In recent history, however, the pentacle has strong roots in Paganism and Wiccan practice - being used as symbols of protection. They can be carved into athames, drawn in the air during spellcraft or created as physical objects that can be hung by doors or windows - and because of their practical uses, this means they can easily be translated to have their uses in-game too.

Pentacle engraving
You could allow your player a boon for having a pentacle engraved onto their weapons or shields. If a player carves a pentacle of protection onto their hilt or inside of the shield, you could offer a multitude of options depending on the game and the character. Give them one use of the shield spell per long rest for D&D, or have it increase AC against undead/spirits are just some examples! 

Pentacle glyph
Pentacles are the perfect flavour for casting glyphs of warding - placing a glyph that alerts you of anyone passing in or through would be perfect as a pentacle, or to bring more flavour from its pagan use of keeping away negative spirits and energies: have the pentacle glyph block any entry from undead/fiends/spirits.

Pentacle hanging
If your characters have a long-term camp or home they work from, consider a boon for having a pentacle hanging inside their home/camp. Perhaps a pentacle by the door would work to keep vampires and spawn out, or stop spirits/undead from entering the home too. It could even act as its own glyph, perhaps waking the attuned character if anyone passes through.

Creating a pentacle
If you want to use pentacles in your game, you can easily make a pentacle as a table prop (or if you’re a witch like me, just have them hanging through your home). I harvest lavender, as lavender also carries protective properties and it dries well making it a piece that will last. Make five small bundles, all in similar lengths, and tie them together with twine at the base of the blooms and the bottom. Now each bundle is secure, you can place them in the shape of the pentacle and begin tying each bundle to each other into the shape of the five-pointed star. Tie some extra twine to the top, with a point reaching upwards, and you have a pentacle just like the artwork.

Dying of the Light
A Short Story
by Kyle Tam

A little known fact about the world - it has heartbeats. Not just two, or ten, but hundreds of thousands, each pulsing at different tempos and audible only if you have the right ears to hear them. Some of them are little more than the murmurs of butterfly wings flapping idly in the distance. Some of them roar and surge with the pulsing of the waves, the tide calling the waters in and pulling back out. All of them tangled up in each other in a globe-spanning web that regulates all that is visible and invisible.

And now I stand at a nexus, one of a thousand young witches all over the world, sitting at the gathering points of magic and power, rooting around for something to sell.

We sense her. We feel her. One among the many. All at once she is ours, connected to us. Connected to the world.
To become one of the chosen you have to be someone exceptionally attuned to the world. At one with that which makes it work, with the processes that ebb and flow between us. You have to have spent a hundred days meditating under a waterfall, or a thousand buried in the earth, or something else that extreme. Perfectly in tune and synchronized with that which is your element.

For the rest of us we’re just here to fill seats. Pad numbers. Even if our own focus pales in comparison to the others, our arcane energy is enough to form a bridge between the world and its people. A fiber optic network of muscles, nerves, and arcane energy. Ever since humanity has existed, witches have existed too. And unfortunately, witching doesn’t pay bills, unless you’re pretty enough and charming enough to make it a racket. So instead I have to rummage for old knick knacks and so-called artifacts to pay my rent.

Her fingertips brush against us and we can feel the crackling, the spark of new life. How many years has it been - a decade? A century? Maybe longer, even. The world sinks even as it grows, the air thrumming with a new energy. Transmissions. Waves. Magic old and new.

The box isn’t special because it’s engraved with ancient runes or encrusted with gems or made of onyx or ivory. It’s special because it isn’t. In my grandmother’s attic, stuffed to the brim with artefacts of the distant ages, this plain little box is trying its best not stand out visually. But when I bring my fingertips close to it, it feels like it’s emitting some kind of static. Tingling along the little ridges and whorls, tracing a path of energy arcing through my arteries, my veins.
Everything I have ever watched, read, or heard as a piece of advice tells me that to open the box is the height of foolishness. I would unleash some catastrophic evil. But at the same time, I’m starting to understand that deep magnetism which draws us towards that which could harm us.

Do you feel me, crackling beneath your fingertips? Surging in arcs in the lines that connect the world? Below the waves and surging through the skies?

It’s almost as if there’s something calling to me. Not a voice, not quite, but a collection of sounds that I can understand. There’s no keyhole on the box, just a little rust that feels rough under my fingertips. In a way, that’s a good sign. If it was truly dangerous it would be under lock and key, buried six feet beneath the ground, tossed into the ocean never to return.
 But instead it is here, nestled around the remnants of my grandmother’s magic.

The box is asking, begging to be opened. Whatever is inside here feels as if it has been here as long as memory. Perhaps longer. And hey, it’s not as if this dinky little thing was going to go for much, anyhow. All it takes is one little push. Just to trip the latch, remove it, and slowly pry the lid open.

We emerge from confinement in a burst of a million hues. We are at once everywhere and nowhere as we embrace the heat of this miniature sun, drawing its energy through us and within us. This is the taste of freedom.

My vision is stolen for a moment by the light that springs forth from the box, and it’s all I can do  to bring my arms up and cover my eyes. Wait for the light to fade into darkness, wait a moment for the pain to subside. The first thought I have is that the reason the box was laying around is that it’s probably someone’s stupid idea of a prank. They’ve placed some kind of light spell within its confines, allowing it to dazzle and obscure. Real funny. But then… but then…

What is your name, little one?

“Aida,” I answer, because when you are faced with a voice that is no voice but the strands of the world being plucked to manifest noise, you suddenly answer questions very quickly.  Squinting at the light shimmering across the room in every spectrum, I cannot make out anything within the light. 

Alda… Alda, you have awoken us in this world of strange illumination. Tell us, show us, what has become of the light we came from. Of the lights that came after our time.

“The lights that came after you? I can… I can try…”

We live on a planet of billions of lights, after all. So I sit and make myself comfortable as I talk to this being of pure light about the world of lights I live in. It begins with fire.

Fire and I are sisters, we who create and nourish each other, blazing brightly.

After that I talk about lamps. Lanterns.

Trapping the flames in order to better control the light. Is it cruel? Is it comfort?

And then lightbulbs.

Lightning created by the hands of man, not summoned from the skies above. We cannot help but flicker and pulse as we hear it - what use is there for us, for our kind, when the world can so easily keep us shackled?

The light is beginning to fade, the hues dropping away one by one until only the palest of them remain. I could allow this… thing, this phenomenon, to fade into the darkness. But I can’t help it. I can’t help but reach out to touch it. To feel-
The connections that run throughout the world, the web that spans the seas and skies, the forces that compel the world into motion, as they always were and as they always will be. And we are one of many, the spirits that gather to make and be made. At one with their element, at once progenitor and progeny alike. Fading in the knowledge that we, too, are dying. Unless.

Unless?

Unless you bond with us, Aida. Unless our fate is tied to another. You grant us safety and we grant you the oldest power. The brightest power. For where is the world without the light to shine upon it?

For those of us who live in the shadow, there are few things that will stir us to action like a call to come into the light. To do something. To be something. Perhaps this is a trap. Perhaps this ancient magic is nothing more than my own delusion. But if the world is calling out for me , would it not be wrong if I didn’t answer?

So I reach once more towards the shimmering lights.

And the world goes white.

Gone with the Wind - A system agnostic encounter
by Armaan Babu

The Hook
This encounter is system agnostic, and can be triggered in any location that the party wanders through that is far removed from civilization. While wandering through this area, three arrows shoot out from the shadows, possibly injuring the party. Before the party can launch a counter-attack, a voice shouts out: “Halt!”

The voice belongs to Poorna, who introduces herself as a sage from an ancient sect long forgotten by history due to their reclusive nature. Poorna is accompanied by three disciples, each armed, who step out from their hiding places, looking none too happy to see the party. 

If the party chooses to continue the fight, it will be a short battle. Poorna has the ability to repel blade, bruise and destabilize magic. If any of the disciples are harmed, Poorna will transform them and herself into gusts of wind, which scatter and reform as far away from the party as possible.

If the party chooses to allow Poorna to talk instead, the disciples will urge Poorna not to trust them, as they could be demons in disguise. Poorna dismisses these concerns, and explains that she is an ascetic — someone who eschews all earthly comforts to gain greater spiritual understanding. This has given many of her sect fantastic powers, and Poorna herself believes that she is on the verge of discovering the ultimate truth of the universe through her meditations — but because of that, demons have been sent to stop her, fearing the power humanity may gain. Her disciples have been keeping her safe up to this point, but perhaps with the party’s help, she could take care of the demons once and for all?

The Demons/Disciples
The three disciples - Bhim, Bho and Bharra - all appear heavily injured. In truth, their injuries are all a ruse. Unbeknownst to Poorna, her “disciples” are actually demons in disguise. They have indeed been sent to keep her from achieving true enlightenment. They are, however, caught in a bit of a stalemate; they fear Poorna’s powers may destroy them if they attack her outright. Though Poorna herself has taken on a vow of non-violence, they are unwilling to test it, and have put on this ruse instead.

Every night, one of them leads Poorna to a makeshift underground shelter for “protection”, as the other two turn into their demonic forms and create many sounds of fights, growls, and screams. After a while, they revert to their human forms, although versions of themselves that appear greatly injured.

The arrival of the party has thrown a wrench in their plans, and they will do all in their power to dispose of them. They will attempt to either split the party to deal with them, or use trickery to set up a trap for them, later in the day.
The demons are creatures of medium difficulty to fight, one-on-one. A full party should have little trouble dispatching them if it comes to a direct fight — but the demons’ carefully laid traps can give them the upper hand. They are immune to fire, and their magical traps make use of that.


Sage Poorna
Poorna welcomes questions, and enjoys talking about philosophy, spirituality, and the ways of her sect. Though she keeps the details of her sect a secret, she speaks of their practices: isolation, and extreme abstinence. Her sect believes that both suffering and pleasure tie mortals down to the earth, if they seek divinity, they must move beyond those feelings. As such, they deny themselves pleasure, and endure great suffering so that they may move beyond it. Poorna’s meditations have given her unique powers — she is impervious to most forms of harm, and has the ability to transform herself into the elements.

It has been the work of her life to see the underlying truth of the universe, and she believes she is on the verge of doing so. She just needs time - alone.

Members of the party who talk with Poorna can influence the truth she ultimately sees. There are six possible outcomes, as listed below. Add a 1+ to each every time a party member fulfills that condition:

Star: Add a +1 every time Poorna is offered a hopeful vision of the future.

Fire: Add a +1 every time a party member illustrates the power of magic, and the interconnectedness of all things.

Mist: Add a +1 whenever Poorna is guided to more abstract thoughts or conversations, or is told of an instance of the bizarre, yet positive.

Eyes: Add a +1 whenever Poorna is reminded or convinced of the dangers of demonic influence.

Poorna can be convinced to attack the demons with her powers, but she does so with extreme reluctance, as she has taken a vow of non-violence. She will only do so if she believes lives are in danger, and even then, the chances of her doing so are extremely slim.

The Plan
Given that the demons only attack at night, the party has plenty of time to prepare an ambush for them. During this time, the party can get to know Poorna, as well as her three “disciples,” who have their own agenda in mind.

The disciples will ask the party to help out with various tasks - scouting the area, setting up traps, hunting for food, etc. - in the hopes of splitting the party up. If any member of the party is alone, and out of earshot of the main camp, the disciples will take on their demonic forms and attempt to slaughter the lone party member immediately.

The party can also use this time to set up for a fight — but the demons will attempt to sabotage traps, or lay their own traps to turn the tide of an upcoming battle in their favour. One of the demons will surreptitiously place fire traps around the ambush site, if not spotted by any member of the party.

A clever party can gather clues that will help them discover clues that may help reveal the disciples’ true nature: 

The disciples appeared the same day that the first demonic attacks began.

Poorna has never actually seen the disciples fight the demons — just the aftermath of the battles.

Poorna mentions that, in addition to the nightly demonic attacks, it has been hard to focus on her meditations as the disciples are in constant need of assistance, or have questions for her - there is always something.

When alone, the disciples discuss their plans in a language that more learned party members may recognize as demonic.

Plant life tends to die around any area a disciple has been around for too long.

They avoid and/or react adversely to any holy/god-blessed items the party have with them.


Resolution
If the party has not discovered the demons’ plans/true forms before nightfall, then they will ask one member of the party to accompany Poorna into the underground shelter and stay locked inside until it is safe again.

The disciples will then transform into their demonic selves and attempt to slaughter the party. If they have managed to place traps earlier, they will erupt in a fountain of flames. The demons are physically strong, and clever, but they can be fooled or intimidated into leaving without resorting to violence, with a good enough plan.

Once the demons are dealt with, one way or the other, Poorna will ask the party to keep watch over her as she meditates for a night and a day. At the end of which, depending on how the party has or has not influenced her, she will see the truth of the universe - and grant one party member a special ability based on how she was influenced. She chooses the party member that’s spent the most time with her, or the one whose mind she feels is most open to the universe’s inner workings.

Star: Poorna has seen that time is an illusion, and that the past, present, and future are all one. She imparts one person the ability to see glimpses of the future, when they sleep.

Fire: Poorna has seen the inner weave of the universe, and sees how the strands of reality can be easily twisted. She teaches one party member how to enhance their magical abilities - how they are enhanced is up to the GM, and dependent on what game system you’re using.

Mist: Poorna has seen that the line between reality and dream is an illusion. She grants one person the ability to walk through dreams, and pull objects from those dreams out into the real world..

Eyes: Poorna has seen that the world is slowly being corrupted by hellish influences. She grants one party member the ability to detect when something has been demonically influence.

She will then part ways with the party, turning into wind to return to her sect, to share what she has seen.

The Ferrous Square
by Terrehdactyl

A brief history of magic squares
The earliest recorded mentions of magic squares are attributed to Chinese mathematicians in 190 BCE. From China, they spread east to India, the Middle East, North Africa, and Muslim Iberia before finally reaching Europe and Japan in the Middle Ages. Each culture had different uses for magic squares from fertility charms, to alchemy, divination and astrology.

In some Western Occult Traditions, each planetary body is assigned various attributes including a series of numbers. One such way of displaying these numbers is within a magic square. 

Below we’ll be using the attributes of Mars to show how a magic square can be used as a puzzle in your games. This could easily work as part of a magical lock or it may play a part in a larger puzzle. 

Attributes commonly associated with Mars include the metal iron, the numbers 5, 25, 65, and 325, the oak tree, and the animals boar, ram, and wolf. 

Setting the scene
No matter where you place this puzzle whether in the middle of a dungeon, within a room containing an orrery, the study of an occult author, or anywhere you have need of a puzzle you can use these pieces to place within your world.

A five-by-five grid has been carved into the front face of the board.

Depending on the system you are using, your players may attempt a medium difficulty check relating to history or an intelligence-based check to identify that this device was not created for the room that it is currently housed in, and is in fact at least several hundred years old.

Hints and Tips
If your party is not fond of puzzles, you can use any of these hints, or tips to make solving the puzzle easier. 

Depending on the system you use, a hard intelligence or relevant knowledge check may reveal that this is a magic square so that all columns, rows, and diagonals add up to the same number.

If you wish to make the puzzle harder for those groups who are particularly good at puzzles, you may add a limited number of tries to the puzzle before they are locked out. In keeping with the Mars theme you could go with 5 attempts before the puzzle cannot be attempted again for 24 hours.

Ancient Magic in Fantasy and Slavic Rituals
by Nightwonderer

Ancient magic is always a fun addition to a game – strange rituals, chanting in dead tongues, it all adds great flavor - and gravity - to a story. It might seem easier to implement in a modern setting than a fantasy one, but this is not necessarily the case. 

If a fantasy setting has spell-slinging wizards, consider their methods – precise incantations, delicate hand movements, rare spell components; all of that could be considered “modern” magic. Magic as science: clean, exact, marked by clear and specific intent and effect rooted in causality. 

Ancient magic, by comparison, is different: it is more visceral, powerful, savage, bound not in words and symbols but in blood and bone, fuelled by life and death and as predictable as a raging tempest. Possess a part of someone and you control them. Sacrifice a life, spill blood, be at a specific place at a specific time, make your wish clear hope that your will is strong enough to endure all that might come from that wish coming true. 

To bring old magic into a system is to appeal at the same time to that unadulterated fear of the unknown and the primal being inside of us that rages against the banal unfairness of the world and that will risk much just to get the upper hand. 
Interwoven into the tales of old, masked as superstitious rituals and traditions, old magic is incredibly easy to use – all it takes is some knowledge of the way the world works. 

For something to be so accessible and yet so unpredictable, it would necessitate any development of the craft to try and hide its origins. Perhaps this could be the theme surrounding ancient magic in your game – someone is using the old ways and they are powerful, but they leave chaos in their wake. 

Or perhaps the more complex, “modern” form of magic is an attempt at elitism, trying to reduce the number of magic users in the world, thus hiding the old ways from the general populace. There are many ways to weave this theme into the story you are telling. The best way to evoke the feeling of ancient magic is to formulate it in simple rituals that require unique components, to give it unpredictable results, and to make it powerful.

With these ideas in mind, following are a few examples of magic rituals that could be used to represent what such ancient arts might entail. These are rooted in old beliefs and rituals from my own heritage, from a time of Slavic polytheism before the advent of Christianity, and altered to better suit the purposes of role playing games.

Hearth Guardian
As simple as it is grisly, this effect can be called into being during the construction of a house. If a person who has worked on the house is cemented into one of its walls, alive or dead, their spirit is bound to the building and will protect it – unless they were forced to this sacrifice, in which case their spirit will do as much as it can to render it uninhabitable. 
The spirit may not have much in the way of affecting the physical world, but it can invade the thoughts of others within the house, make them forget or hallucinate, cause them harm or frighten them. 

An alternative method to gain this effect is to bury a person who was born, lived, and died in the building beneath the building’s main entrance.

Curse Shirt
Nine old women weave a shirt on a night of the full moon. This process must happen in utter silence, without a single word spoken. Hairs from nine different deceased individuals need be woven into the fabric. Once done, it is given to the youngest of the women to keep. She can thereafter give it to a person to wear. 

Those who have worn this shirt are “adopted” by a supernatural entity that brings death and disease, thus becoming protected from both. Such a shirt can be used multiple times on multiple individuals, but it must always be given willingly by the woman keeping it safe. 

While the effects may be beneficial during the individual’s life, it also means that the entity that has adopted them will find them once they depart the mortal coil. Whether this is a good thing or a bad one, remains unclear. Certainly, no one has returned to testify.

Chainbreaker Charm
A special plant grows in fields of lichen on a certain night of the year. On this night a person may bind their arms in chains and go searching for it. If they come close to it, the chains might fall to the ground on their own. In the area where this happens, there will be a flower. 

If the person picks this flower and sews it beneath the skin of their palm, they will thereafter become able to open any door and unlock any lock they touch with that hand. The flower, if eaten, is also capable of breaking any curses that might plague the individual.

Tongue of Beasts
As chthonic beings, snakes are able to slither with various treasures from the underworld to the world of the living. If such a snake finds itself trapped in a circle of fire on its way back from the underworld and an individual should be around either to save it or to witness it reveal its legs to get away from that fire, the individual can demand of the snake to gift them with the Tongue of Beasts.
 
At this point, both the individual and the snake need bite each other, after which the individual becomes able to speak with all animals. In addition, they understand all languages, and plants whisper to them the healing or toxic properties they might have.

The person may never speak of this ability, and they may never reveal having it, or how one might come to possess it, to anyone. If they do, they draw the attention of the underworld to them, making the knowledge that they possess, which was never meant for the living, so heavy that they instantly die.

Untouched Water
Untouched water is a powerful tool of healing and protection. For it to be untouched, water has to stem from the depths of the earth, where the sun never reaches, or alternatively from a spring in the early hours of the morning, before the sun has risen. It is to be collected in a stone or metal receptacle and carried home without being used or shined upon by the sun. 

Once this is complete, the water can be used to treat wounds and diseases, as well as in baking and cooking to create meals that will provide those who eat them with a degree of protection from curses and sickness. Untouched water can be used as a powerful component of other rituals.

Water of Oblivion
If Untouched Water is frozen and then thawed, its properties change. From this point onward, whenever consumed, it can have one of two effects: either the person drinking it forgets who they are and lose all their memories, or they become forgotten by everyone who once knew them.

Possession
To have a part of someone means to have power over them. A strand of hair, a tooth, some nail clippings, any small part of another person is enough to provide a magic user of strong will with everything they need to take control of that individual. 
The parts in question are ground with some of the caster’s blood into a fine paste, and then hardened in a fire at midnight. The caster can then wear the stone that results from this process around their neck and influence the thoughts and actions of the victim. If the caster swallows the stone, they fall unconscious and immediately take full control over the victim, but this only lasts until the victim spends an hour in sunlight or crosses running water.

If the victim can find and swallow the stone that was made to control them, the creator of the stone dies instantly.

Out of a mixture of fear and curiosity, tales have persisted for generations that practitioners of witchcraft and other rituals have gathered at special locations for so-called Witches’ Sabbaths. One such location, Łysa Góra or Bald Mountain, has greatly inspired story and song over the years, with evidence that prehistoric temples were built to honor any number of the ancient deities - Perun (chief god, domain over the sky, lightning, & thunder), Radogost (god of hospitality), or Svetovit (god of war & abundance), or perhaps instead, worshipers of the new rising force of Chernabog (god of misfortune). 
But before the myths and legends of history, what existed here? Who or what traversed these areas and deemed them suitable to acknowledge their power? Your story can make that discovery as you explore the caverns beneath, uncover its secrets, face its occupants, and influence history.

The Homeric Oracle: Using Rhapsodomancy in TTRPGs
by gothHoblin

Rhapsodomancy is a form of divination that uses randomly chosen lines or passages from poetry to obtain answers to a question. Similar to Bibliomancy, which is mostly concerned with sacred texts, it is widespread across many different cultures of the world, and the methods by which it can be performed vary. Read on to learn about the Homeric Oracle and how it or something similar can be used in your TTRPG games.

Hierocles: Say, what oracle authorized you to burn thighs for the gods?
Trygeas: The very fine one that Homer composed, of course.
Peace - Aristophanes

One of the most popular texts to use for rhapsodomancy was the I Ching, an ancient Chinese divination text, and Greek Oracles used both collections of the Sybilline Books before their destruction.Texts by the poets Virgil and Homer were also used, known as the sortes Virgilianae and sortes Homericae respectively. The sortes system was a popular method for divination during ancient Roman times which involved drawing of lots to obtain guidance; for these texts specifically, the verses were written on small tablets and then drawn out of an urn. The sortes Sanctorum is another antique text that was used for divination by means of throwing dice.

The Homeric Oracle, also known as Homeromanteion, was a method of divination found in Greek Magical Papyrus 121. Made up of lines of Homer's poetry - from both the Iliad and the Odyssey - sorted by triple digits, the querent would partake in certain ritual preparations, including an appeal to the god Apollo, before rolling a dice 3 times, then matching the results to find the corresponding verse, which is then re-contextualised out of the book and into their life.

Using an oracle like this can be a fun addition to your TTRPG games - whether as the underlying mechanic for a divination spell or perhaps to help a GM deliver cryptic fortunes through an oracle or seer-like NPC. I've provided the Homeric oracle instructions and text below, which you could use as-is, or write your own using random passages from something else relevant to your game!

Instructions for use:

1.	Consult the table of days and hours in which the oracle can be performed. 

What time of day does the deity or spirit(s) being consulted prefer to receive prayers? Timing can be a tricky thing when it comes to TTRPGs. A group is not always going to be in the right place at the right time. Including this element can mean that a party will need to try again at another time. 

Also consider whether there might be consequences for attempting a divination at the wrong time of day. Aside from receiving no answer, they could receive an incorrect answer, or an answer from a different entity entirely.

2.	Utter a prayer addressed to Apollo. While reciting the prayer, think about the question you wish to be answered.

Obviously, you may substitute Apollo for the deity or entity of choice, or leave out the prayer entirely if that is more appropriate. The person performing the divination should make some sort of dedication or appeal to the forces that are powering their spell or ability, while the person seeking guidance should keep their query in their mind while this is happening. If they are the same person, then these two actions can of course be intertwined into one.

3.	Throw a dice three times.

The dice to be used here are D6's, and it can either be one thrown three times, or 3D6 rolled at once. If the three dice are rolled at once, then the numbers on them should be read left to right. Do not add the results up; together they create a 3 digit number.

The number of three digits must be located throughout the series of numbers followed by a Homeric verse.

Consult the table to find the three digit number on the dice. These start at 111 (the lowest able to be generated) and end at 666, and cover all of the possible results of throwing a dice three times. The resulting passage beside it is the oracular answer to the question. If you encounter a number with nothing beside it, this indicates an unclear result.
