Introduction
"Hiding behind a shop stall you hear steps of your pursuers 
growing distant. What do you do?" Such questions and their 
answers make up the dialogue at the core of any fantasy 
tabletop RPG, including Fatebenders. It's a game where you 
get together with your friends to delve into another world, 
living whole lives as ﬁctional characters of your co-created 
story. There is no other experience like it! 
How to play?
In Fatebenders one of you takes up the lead as the GM 
(game master) and others play the PCs (player characters) - 
protagonists who form the party of adventurers. 
Before your ﬁrst game session (when you get together to 
play) the GM outlines a world and creates the starting city 
using the Fantasy kingdom and settlement generator. 
During the ﬁrst session: 
1 The GM introduces the world, the starting settlement and 
its factions. 
2 Players pick one of the factions as their starting faction.
3 Each player creates their character and deﬁnes their 
character's current Goal.
4
Players come up with Bonds that unite their characters. 
 
Before each following session the GM prepares encounters 
with NPCs (non-player characters) to challenge PC Goals. 
If the party decides to visit a location or talk to an NPC that 
the GM hasn't prepared, the GM improvises the encounter 
using NPCs generated previously for that city district or 
generates new NPCs on the spot.
Both prepared and improvised encounters play out the 
same way:
1 First the GM describes the encounter - Where are the 
PCs? What do they see? Who do they meet? 
2 Then players describe what their characters do. If the GM 
thinks that a described action can both succeed and fail, 
they will ask the player to roll dice to see how it goes. 
3 The GM describes results of these actions and reactions 
of NPCs. If an NPC acts against a PC, then dice are rolled 
to determine the result.
4
Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the party wants to leave the 
encounter and nothing is stopping them.
5
Players describe where the party goes next. Repeat from 
step 1 or end the game session to continue next time. 
And so the GM enacts the whole world except for the PCs, 
one encounter at a time. When combat starts during an 
encounter, the play cycle changes as described in the 
Combat and stealth chapter (p. 22).

What is Fatebenders about?
In Fatebenders you are a band of friends getting in trouble 
and trying to sneak, talk and ﬁght your way out of it using 
your wits, skills and freeform control over the elements.  
You play ﬂawed and vulnerable characters facing not 
monsters, but other characters or beasts whose goals just 
happen to be at odds with yours. Knowing what these goals 
are might help you avoid a ﬁght. But if a ﬁght does break 
out, it's.. 
› Dangerous - Characters start dying upon suﬀering their 
third Wound (p. 19). Wounds take time to heal. 
› Fast-paced - No damage rolls. Players do their turns 
together in any order. 
› Tactically engaging - Weapon reach (p. 29) and cover 
(p. 22) matters. Anyone can shove, disarm, grapple, hurl, 
intimidate and taunt (p. 25), which can trigger or prevent 
Opportunity Attacks, making these actions situationally 
better than attacking and making positioning important. 
What makes this RPG different?
When I decided to start a new campaign, I looked for a 
game that oﬀers… 
› Abilities to control the elements. 
› No magic that would trivialize otherwise interesting 
decisions or make skills obsolete. 
› No character classes. Freedom for characters to learn 
and do anything any other character can. 
› No single optimal character progression path that 
would make other paths feel weak or obsolete, funnel 
players into the same choices or result in immersion 
breaking munchkins instead of believable characters. 
› Support for alternative approaches to conﬂicts. 
Negotiation, charm, deception, stealth or a test of skill 
should be just as viable as violence. 
› Less rules. Other RPGs tend to be over 300 pages long! 
› Tactically engaging combat that ends quickly to get on 
with the adventure. 
› Minimum time spent doing arithmetic. 
› No stray arrow deaths but also no safety behind a 
wall of hit points. Players invest in their characters 
emotionally. Letting PCs die without a good reason or 
dramatic eﬀect would be a waste, but resorting to 
violence should always be a real risk for them. 
› No accountant work. No need to count coins, track 
encumbrance nor other boring simulation that everyone 
forgets to keep track of. 
› No incentive to become a murder hobo death squad - 
A group that is constantly on the move to ﬁnd, slay and 
loot bigger and badder opponents as their main means 
of income and progression. Players shouldn't fear 
missing out if they don't want to loot corpses. They 
should get just as much XP while exploring, searching for 
clues, for love, for revenge, for fame, for a challenging 
opponent, a teacher, a benevolent ruler, for inspiration to 
compose the perfect song or ingredients to brew the 
perfect batch of ale! 
That is quite a list. So naturally, I didn't ﬁnd such a game and 
ended up making my own RPG that strives to fulﬁll these 
needs while staying readable, self-consistent, unambiguous 
and playable with no prior tabletop RPG experience.  
What do you need for playing?
› 1 to 5 players (ideally 3) having 3 to 5 hours to play. 
› An inspired GM who knows the rules and has a secret 
document, where they write ideas for encounters before 
each session. The rules are not meant to be read during 
play. If the GM or players can't remember or agree on 
how a rule works, the GM can overrule it on the ﬂy, then 
look it up after the session and let the players know how 
they will rule it in future sessions. 
› Things for an oﬀline session: 
»
A character sheet, pencil and eraser for each player. 
»
At least two twenty-sided dice (d20) and a tray to 
roll your dice in. 
»
Coins or game tokens to use as Drama tokens (p. 17).
»
The Descriptive Decks - 3 decks with 4 cards in each 
deck with a question written on each card. Either 
print out the cards at the end of this PDF and cut 
them out or simply write the same questions on 
pieces of paper by hand. 
»
A few other things to play out combat (p. 55). 
› In case of an online session - an account on a virtual 
tabletop site like roll20.net or foundryvtt.com. 
The Game Master's guide (p. 44) provides steps on how to 
prepare and run game sessions, gives advice on how to 
make encounters more interesting and how to handle 
situations that might come up during play. You are also 
welcome to join Fatebenders subreddit and Discord server.
How to read these rules?
Every game rule is a bullet point. Paragraphs between them 
are commentaries and examples. Every new concept is 
either bolded and explained or linked to a following 
chapter explaining it. 
All rules are tied to concepts, so if you remember that a 
concept like Opportunity Attack or Drama token exists, 
Ctrl+F it here to ﬁnd rules related to it. If a word has a 
special meaning in Fatebenders, it is capitalized when used 
in this special sense (e.g. Skill vs skill).

First game session
During the ﬁrst session the GM will walk you through these 
steps:
1 If you have new players in your group, then everyone can 
introduce themselves and their favorite fantasy worlds. 
2 Together you decide on what themes, phobia triggers, 
creatures or inventions should be absent from the game. 
3 The GM outlines the game world, starting kingdom, 
starting city and its factions - organizations that actively 
pursue their leader's goals in this city. 
4
Players pick their starting faction, their party's role in 
that faction and come up with the starting scene with the 
GM. See the next chapter for example roles and scenes. 
5
Then players create their characters, pick Bonds between 
them and together answer questions about their Bonds. 
6
Players create their characters and send their character 
sheet or a photo of it to the GM.
7
Agree on a weekday and time of future sessions. 
These steps conclude the ﬁrst session. Before the next 
session the GM will prepare the starting scene and some 
other encounters to challenge the Goals of player 
characters (p. 13). 
 
Starting scene and faction
In the castle
Let's say you decided to play the few remaining loyal 
servants, guards, advisors and spies of the royal family. The 
king is on his deathbed and his children still too young to 
lead the state. 
Then your starting scene could be that the queen has 
gathered you to present evidence of an attempt on her life 
and to charge you with the safety of her children. 
Or let's say you decided to play the viscount and his family 
and that the viscount was appointed by the emperor to rule 
over this freshly conquered city state. You ﬁnd yourself 
surrounded by resentful but beaten noble families and 
uncooperative faction leaders. A game of intrigue, politics, 
betrayals and grand events. Your family is expected to hold 
court events, pass judgement over legal disputes, lead local 
festivities and decide over distribution of grain and other 
natural resources. 
The starting scene could be that one of your guards ﬁnds 
the dead body of your family's trusted scribe and the tax 
collection records missing.

In the lower city
Let's say you decided to play as street urchins, poor 
homeless kids stealing to survive. You have a hideout, 
several secret paths and stashes around the city and only 
each other to rely on. 
Your starting scene could be in a dark alley where you are 
cornered by a nasty gang of older boys threatening to 
clobber you if you don't hand over today's earnings. 
Or you could decide to play as a cell of a rebel movement 
preparing for a revolt or a raid on another faction. 
You could also start in a dark alley - this time running from 
city guards who were tipped oﬀ about your usual meeting 
spot and came looking at the wrong time. 
In the docks
If you picked the smuggling ring, then you have some goods 
that you specialize in smuggling into this city while working 
as sailors, ﬁshers, carters or caravan guards. 
A member of another faction sits at your table, orders you a 
drink and asks for your price to smuggle a person into the 
city. 
In the market
You could play as merchants, guards, porters, laborers, 
sailors or cartmen of a foreign merchant guild. 
Your starting scene could be that your merchant ship or 
caravan arrives in this city for the ﬁrst time to establish a 
new guild oﬀice here. You are received by the local toll 
oﬀicial accompanied by some guards.
Or you could play as a small mercenary unit accepting 
irregular jobs like convoy, escort and investigation. 
You could start at the apartment that you rent as your 
headquarters - A client has come late in the evening asking 
you to ﬁnd a missing person. 
In the workshops
You are the laborers, shopkeepers and apprentices under a 
common master. Decide what goods are produced in your 
workshop? It's a game of mastery and rivalry.  
Your master has just received an order from the king to 
produce a masterpiece or a batch with unusual quality 
requirements. Apparently the city's pride is on the line. You 
know what materials you need, but not where to get them 
and to produce the desired result you will need to 
experiment to ﬁnd a suitable manufacturing process. 
On the temple hill
You could decide to play as monks, nuns, priests, 
priestesses or caretakers of a temple.
Some misfortune has befallen your city. Its victims saw it as 
a matter of your god's domain and they have come to your 
temple for guidance and salvation. 
You could decide to play as inquisitors who hunt mystics of 
some Attunement that is outlawed in your kingdom. 
You might start with a confrontation with such a Mystic. 
In the entertainment district
You could play a troupe of travelling musicians or circus or 
theater performers.
Your troupe could be indebted to the local crime lord and 
the starting scene could be where one of you just found a 
message saying that you are not to leave the city until your 
debt is paid. 
You could play as a gang of thieves.
You could start at a secret meeting with a member of 
another faction who promises you more money than you 
ever had if you steal a well guarded treasure from the castle 
and deliver it to them. 
In the barracks
You could be a patrol unit assigned to the entertainment 
district. If there are Water Attuned characters among you, 
then you are also assigned to Freezer duty - putting out ﬁres 
and freezing water into large blocks of ice in all the food 
cellars of the district to keep food from spoiling. 
The starting scene could be that you are called to a tavern 
nearby to break up a ﬁght that started between members of 
two factions over some recent event. 
You could be a special unit tasked with investigating crimes 
commited using Elemental Abilities and hunting down 
powerful criminal Mystics.
You could start by arriving at a murder scene.
You could be a band of hunters, scouts or rangers assigned 
to patrolling the dangerous wilds around the city. 
You ﬁnd a wounded person during your patrol and they are 
asking you to ﬁnd their companion who got separated when 
a beast attacked them. 
In the upper city
You could be members of an inﬂuential noble house, their 
guards, spies or servants - a game of intrigue, politics, 
betrayals and grand events.
Your lord has gathered you to announce that he is leaving 
for war with the king's army and tasks you with his 
responsibilities while he is away and warning against 
factions that might try to beneﬁt from his absence. 
In the academy
You could be mystic students, military cadets, martial arts 
students or athletes in training. It's a game of mastery, 
rivalry, personal struggles, mystery and experimentation. 
You have gathered up after a lesson to discuss the 
disappearance of a student whom you all knew.

Character creation
Now that you know the party's starting faction and your 
character's role in it, it's time to create your character. 
In Fatebenders there are no rules for species. It doesn't 
mean that the only sapient species are humans. You can 
play as any humanoid your GM allows you to. They are just 
not treated diﬀerently by the rules than other species. Your 
GM can allow a species to have some special ability like 
darkvision or water breathing and then balance it with a 
weakness that would come up during play just as frequently 
as the special ability and adjust its exact eﬀect during play. 
Take a character sheet. On it you can write your character's 
name, species and faction, describe their Bonds, Motives 
and their current Goal (p. 13), track their level in each of the 8 
Professions and Skills under each Profession (p. 13), the 
Wounds (p. 19) they are waiting to heal, their broken and 
mended Armour Points (p. 20), the Fatigue (p. 20) they are 
enduring, the items they carry (p. 30), the Lifestyle (p. 19) 
their party can aﬀord, your adventuring notes, remaining 
Drama tokens (p. 17) and total and remaining XP (p. 13). 
1. Bonds
First create a Bond with at least one other player character, 
discuss your idea with the player. Together pick a Bond from 
the below list and answer some questions about it. 
If you have an idea that isn't covered here, discuss it and 
ﬂesh it out. A good Bond sparks interactions between PCs 
and gives them a good reason to stay together and come to 
an agreement when faced with a conﬂict. 
You two share a favourite activity
› What is it? 
› What is your favourite place that you visit together to 
engage in this activity? 
› What is the one incredible story that happened to you 
two there that people won't shut up about? 
› What did people start calling you two after that? 
You two are siblings or a couple
› What is the most embarrassing fact that you know about 
your sibling/partner? 
› What teasing nickname do you call them by to remind 
them of this embarrassing fact? 
› What is the one thing that they do in certain situations 
that you can't stand? 
› Why are you secretly proud of them?
 
You two are rivals
› In what skill or goal? 
› How do you show oﬀ your skill or achievements? 
› How did each of you last win against the other? 
› What was the winner's prize or the loser's wager? 
You two are a mentor and a student
Questions for the mentor:
› You once took on an ambitious goal. You failed to 
achieve it, but it taught you the limits of your ability. What 
was that goal and how did you fail?
› Why do you feel responsible for your student?
› What is the one mistake you are afraid that your student 
will repeat after you? 
Questions for the student:
› What do you admire about your mentor? 
› What wise advice are you constantly ignoring until you 
learn it the hard way?
› What prevents you from reaching your full potential?
You two are fugitives
You are hiding from the leader of another faction after 
causing them public humiliation, ﬁnancial loss or grief. 
› Who are you two hiding from?
› What was your part in it?
› Why do you not regret doing it?
› What recognizable features do you have that identify you 
as the culprit? 
› What would likely happen if you two got caught?
You two are business partners
One of you crafts a product or provides a service, the other 
one promotes it, ﬁnds buyers and haggles over the price. 
› What are you selling? 
› Who is your single biggest competitor in this city? 
› What makes your product or service stand out? 
› Who supplies you with irreplaceable materials or 
inspiration in this city?

2. Motives
The unﬁnished sentences on your character sheet are called 
Motives. Finish them to give more detail to your character's 
personality and their past. Reading them at the start of each 
session is a good reminder of who you are playing. They will 
also help the GM and other players imagine and relate to 
your character. 
3. Goal
Your PC's Goal is what they want to accomplish within the 
next few days. If your character has a longer term goal, then 
think what would be the next step in achieving it and 
describe the next step as the Goal.
4. Professions and Attunements
Ask your GM how experienced the player characters start 
out as: 
› Children - One Profession at level 3, one at 2 and one at 1 
or 10 XP (p. 13) *. Up to 1 Elemental Attunement (p. 33). 
› Youngsters - One Profession at level 4, one at 3, one at 2, 
one at 1 or 20 XP *. Up to 1 Attunement. 
› Young professionals - Professions at levels 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 
or 35 XP *. Up to 2 Attunements. 
› Seasoned professionals - Professions at levels 6, 5, 4, 3 
and 2 or 55 XP *. Up to 3 Attunements. 
› Accomplished professionals - Professions at levels 7, 5, 
5, 4, 3 and 3 or 80 XP *. Up to 4 Attunements. 
› Idols to look up to, key historical ﬁgures - Professions at 
levels 9, 7, 6, 5, 5, 4, 3 and 2 or 143 XP *. Up to 6 
Attunements. 
* - If your GM allows "point buy", then instead of starting 
with an array of Profession levels you can start at 0 in every 
Profession and buy levels as described in the Character 
Progression chapter (p. 13). If you are left with remaining XP, 
record it on the character sheet for later use. 
Start by deciding which of the Professions on your character 
sheet deﬁnes your character the most: Give it your highest 
starting Profession level and ﬁll that many of its circles. 
Then pick the second most deﬁning Profession and so on 
until you've allocated all starting Profession levels. 
› If you took levels in the Mystic Profession, then choose a 
number of Elements (p. 35) that your character is Attuned 
to up to your Mystic Profession level, but no more than 
allowed by your starting experience. 
If you have a cool idea for an Ability that doesn't match any 
existing ones, describe your concept to your GM and if they 
approve it, together you can come up with a new Ability by 
following steps in the Game Master's Guide (p. 44).
5. Abilities
Acquaint yourself with the Abilities of these Elements and 
list them on the back of your character sheet. There you can 
also note what Forms these Abilities can be used in and 
keywords of what they do. 
6. Lifestyle
Ask your GM what Lifestyle the party starts on. If it's 
Aﬀordable, ﬁll the Cheap Lifestyle circles. If it's Expensive, 
ﬁll both Cheap and Aﬀordable Lifestyle circles. 
7. Equipment

If your character wears leather armour, they have 1 Armour

Character progression 
Players can increase their character's Profession levels by 
spending XP (experience points). 
› To increase the level of any Profession by one, spend XP 
equal to the new Profession level. 
For example, to increase the Scholar Profession's level from 
2 to 3, spend 3 XP. Fill a circle under that Profession. Take 
care not to ﬁll the Skill level circles. Only Profession levels 
can be bought with XP. 
The character sheet only has 10 circles for marking each 
level, but any Profession or Skill can increase endlessly. 
Please write levels above 10 as numbers instead. 
Skills
Skills listed on the character sheet cover all game-relevant 
activities a player might want to engage in. When the GM 
asks you to do a Skill check (p. 15), they will pick one with 
the name most appropriate to what your character is 
attempting. If you want your character to regularly do 
something not covered by existing Skills, agree with your 
GM on which existing Skill to expand to include this activity 
(for example by including Fishing in the Survival Skill). 
› All PCs start the game with 0 levels in each Skill and only 
gain Skill levels by Critically failing (p. 17) Skill checks. 
They learn from their mistake and increase the Skill by 1. 
Leveling up Skills this way softens streaks of bad rolls and 
leaves a mark from each major failure that the PC has gone 
through, reﬂecting their true experience. 
› Every character can use every Skill without levels in that 
Skill or its corresponding Profession, except for Skills of 
the Mystic Profession, which can only be used once the 
character Attunes to an Element (p. 33) corresponding to 
that Skill or while they are training with a teacher 
Attuned to this Element. 
Professions and Attributes
A PC's physical Attributes don't exist as separate statistics, 
but are instead equal to a corresponding Profession level 
marked on the character sheet. For example the Athlete 
Profession determines Strength, the Scholar Profession 
determines Intelligence, etc. This reﬂects how talent only 
manifests itself through practice. 
A Profession is a group of Skills that improve together. For 
example leveling up the Mystic Profession improves the 
chances of all your Fire, Illusion and other Mystic Skill 
checks, which include all Ability checks (p. 34).
Levels in a Profession don't mean that your character has 
practiced that exact profession, but they probably have 
experience with something similar. You decide what. 
 
 
Goals and gaining XP
Your character's Goal is the single most pressing matter 
that you want your character to solve or accomplish during 
the next game session. If you have several interests, pick 
the one that your PC can achieve the soonest. If you won't 
complete it during the next session, it will remain your Goal 
until you either complete it or come up with a new Goal. 
› You can come up with a new Goal for your PC at any time 
for any reason, but it will only become active on the next 
session after you've let your GM know what it is. It will 
then substitute your current Goal.
This way the GM will have time to prepare some challenges 
for your character to overcome in pursuit of this Goal. 
› You can't use a Goal that contains or is contained in the 
current Goal of another PC. Partial overlap is allowed. 
› When the GM decides that a PC's Goal is completed, all 
PCs of players attending the session gain 1 XP. 
› Every time you attend a session your character gains 1 XP.
Add the XP you gained to both the total and remaining XP 
on your character sheet. You can then spend the remaining 
XP. If every player attends every session, they should have 
the same total XP. 
Sometimes a PC has a secret Goal. To avoid spoiling it to 
other players, the PC's player can also keep it a secret by 
only sharing it with the GM. If you do so, be mindful of why 
your character keeps it a secret. Is it just a prideful or 
embarrassing matter that they prefer to deal with on their 
own or would your PC lose the trust of other party members 
if they found out about it? You are free to do anything, but 
as soon as you turn against the party, be ready to go out 
with an epic ﬁght or perhaps become an NPC and make 
yourself a new character to join the party. 
The game master's narration always follows the party. 
› If a PC leaves the party without an intent to rejoin it after 
a few encounters, they become an NPC. Their player can 
create a new PC with the same total XP. The GM can 
introduce them to the party at ﬁrst opportunity.

Attempt resolution
When the GM asks what do you do, describe what is your 
character trying to achieve and how? If the GM thinks that 
this attempt is certain to fail and that your character knows 
it, the GM will explain why, so you could change your mind. 
If the GM thinks that this attempt can both succeed and 
fail with interesting consequences, they can ask for a 
check: 
› To do a check, roll a d20 and, depending on the kind of 
check, add either a Skill and a Profession level or only a 
Profession level to your roll and compare the sum to the 
DC (diﬀiculty class) - a target number given by the GM. 
The character's attempt succeeds, if the check result is 
greater than or equal to the DC and fails otherwise. 
In case a character searches and ﬁnds nothing or tries to be 
stealthy and doesn't see anyone alarmed, they don't know if 
it's because they failed to ﬁnd something, succeeded in 
staying unnoticed or because there was simply nothing to 
ﬁnd or no one to notice them. If a player did a check for 
such an attempt, they would know its result and would have 
a hard time playing a character who doesn't. 
› Hidden check - When a PC attempts something and 
their failure can seem like a success to them, the GM 
should do the check for this attempt without revealing its 
result to the players.
If this attempt has no chance to succeed or to fail, it's still a 
good idea for the GM to roll a die so player's wouldn't know 
that no check was actually done.

Opposed check
› When a character's attempt is opposed by others, the 
character does an Opposed Skill, Profession or Attribute 
check against them. 
› When an NPC Opposes a check, the DC of the check = 11 
+ Opposing NPC's level. 
› When a PC Opposes a check, its DC = 11 + Opposing PC's 
Attribute level, Profession level or Skill + Profession 
level, depending on whether the PC Opposes the check 
with their Attribute, Profession or Skill respectively. 
› If the rules deﬁne Skills or Attributes that a PC can use to 
Oppose a check, pick one of them. Otherwise your GM 
decides what you Oppose the check with.
Unopposed checks
› When an NPC attempts something that is not Opposed 
by anyone or is Opposed only by other NPCs, then 
instead of rolling dice for a check, the GM is encouraged 
to decide what happens based on what would be most 
fun in the current situation. 
› When a PC's attempt is not Opposed by anyone, the GM 
decides on the appropriate DC. 
An appropriate DC can be picked based on how likely is the 
attempt to succeed. For example a character with 0 levels in 
a Skill and its Profession succeeds the Skill, Profession and 
related Attribute checks: 
› 95% of the time with DC 2;
› 80% of the time with DC 5; 
› 65% of the time with DC 8; 
› 50% of the time with DC 11; 
› 35% of the time with DC 14; 
› 20% of the time with DC 17; 
› 5% of the time with DC 20. 
A task with DC 20 is hopelessly hard for such an 
incompetent character, but a professional with e.g. a level 6 
in the associated Profession and Skill would have a fair 
chance at it. For them it is just as diﬀicult as a DC 8 check is 
for the incompetent character, because a +6 for the Skill and 
the Profession is equivalent to subtracting 12 from the 
check's DC.
 
Opposed checks of social Skills
The Combat and stealth chapter (p. 22) explains how to 
resolve Opposed checks in a physical confrontation. Here's 
how to resolve confrontation that hasn't resorted to 
physical violence: 
› When you are trying to get a hunch of an NPC's level, 
Attitude (p. 14) or emotional state or see if your character 
notices their attempt to Charm or Bluﬀ someone else, 
the GM will simply tell you if it is obvious. Otherwise the 
GM will do an Opposed Insight Skill check for your PC in 
secret from you and will describe the impression your 
character got based on the result, which is either the 
truth or good acting. 
When trying to inﬂuence a character, just like you would 
with any other attempt, describe how you are attempting it 
and what you are trying to achieve with it. In addition you 
can describe: 
› Are you using any knowledge of the target's desires, 
fears or weaknesses? 
› What arguments and circumstances are in your favor? 
This gives depth to your character and helps avoid 
misunderstandings both in and out of the game. You can 
inspire other players by acting out important parts of the 
interaction in ﬁrst person if you want. Don't worry though, 
your performance will not aﬀect the check success. It is 
determined by your character's social skills and your ability 
to convey their intent and reasoning. 
Like with other checks, if based on your description the GM 
decides that this attempt has a chance to fail with 
interesting consequences, they'll ask you to do an Opposed 
Charming, Taunting, Bluﬀing, Intimidation, Leadership, 
Animal Handling or Persuasion Skill check as appropriate. 
Persuasion is such a broad term that it should only be used 
when no other Skill applies. 
You can ask the GM to turn this check into an Extended Test 
(p. 18) if your character is trying to use more than one of the 
NPC's desires, fears or weakness or other circumstances to 
their advantage. 
When your character fails to Oppose an NPC attempting to 
inﬂuence them, please trust your GM and play along - 
Decide on how much this inﬂuence changes your character's 
behavior, play it out and maybe get awarded with a Drama 
token (p. 17). 
Social Skill checks are not magical - They don't undo the 
character's existing values, convictions nor experiences, but 
rather refocus on them from a new perspective, based on 
new information. The DC of such attempts should reﬂect 
how big a change you are trying to incite.

Perception related checks
There are no "perception", "search", "spot" or "investigate" 
Skills - perception in Fatebenders is less about having sharp 
senses and more about knowing what to look for. For 
example the GM might ask you for a Concealment check to 
spot a trap or an ambush, a Sneaking check to hear 
someone's footsteps over your own noise or a Smithing 
check to guess how many Armour Points (p. 20) an 
opponent's armour might have. 
To increase your chances of recognizing or ﬁnding 
something take speciﬁc actions like asking locals, looking 
through a keyhole, listening at a wall or giving the GM a 
description of what your PC is searching for. The GM might 
then ask you for an appropriate check like Intelligence to 
recall someone's appearance or Climbing to scale a high 
ledge for better visibility.
Advantage and Disadvantage
Sometimes a character has a situational advantage or a 
disadvantage to succeed on a check that is not reﬂected in 
their Skill or Profession levels used for this check. For 
example their attempt targets an NPC in a way that exploits 
the NPC's weakness. Instead of modifying the diﬀiculty class 
of the check, the GM can give an Advantage or a 
Disadvantage on the check: 
› For every Advantage that applies to your check, roll one 
more d20 and use the highest roll. 
› For every Disadvantage that applies to your check, 
cancel out one Advantage. If there is no Advantage to 
cancel out, then instead roll one more d20 and use the 
lowest roll. 
› A PC gets an Advantage on attempts to preserve their 
Bond (p. 11) and a Disadvantage on attempts that 
endanger their Bond with another PC. The GM decides 
whether any attempt qualiﬁes as such. 
Critical rolls
› Critical success happens when you roll a 20. The check 
succeeds even if it doesn't beat the check DC and you get 
lucky in some way besides succeeding as described in 
the rules or decided by the GM.
› Critical failure happens when you roll a 1. The check fails 
even if it beats the check DC. The GM describes a 
misfortune that befalls your character aside from failing 
the check or lets you describe your own mishap.  
A good mishap is one that makes the party's situation more 
interesting.  
Borderline check results
› Success With a Setback happens when your check 
result equals its DC. You can describe some minor 
misfortune that happens in addition to succeeding this 
attempt. If your character's next check is the same Skill, 
Profession or Attribute check as this one, it gets a 
Disadvantage. 
› Failure Forward happens when your check result is 
lower than its DC by 1. You can describe some minor 
fortune that happens in addition to failing this attempt. If 
your character's next check is the same Skill, Profession 
or Attribute check as this one, it gains an Advantage. 
Cooperative checks
› When PCs unite their eﬀorts to overcome a common 
hurdle, the GM can ask them to do a Cooperative check 
by asking each participating player to do a check 
corresponding to the way they are trying to help and 
uses only the best check result. Critical rolls are not 
considered Critical for the outcomes nor for leveling up 
the Skill associated with the check.
Drama tokens
› If you make the game more fun in whatever way, the GM 
can award you with a Drama token. 
The GM can use the Goal and Motives you have described 
on your character sheet to make your character face 
dramatic dilemmas, intense conﬂicts or smaller kinds of 
trouble and award you with Drama tokens proportionally to 
how much trouble they caused you. You can initiate this 
trouble yourself by roleplaying your PC's Motives or making 
them act in character in a situation, where acting out of 
character would be safer or more eﬀective. 
› At the end of each gaming session the GM and each 
player can vote for one other player. Players who got the 
highest number of votes gain a Drama token. 
› You can give the GM a Drama token to reroll your 
character's check result before it is narrated. 
› No check can be rerolled more than once. 
Drama tokens don't expire and can even be used to reroll 
Death checks.  
Keep track of your remaining number of Drama tokens on 
the "Drama" ﬁeld on your character sheet.

Extended Tests
Achieving some goals is more a question of skill, time and 
resources than of chance. In these cases the GM can ask you 
to do an Extended Test instead of a single check to achieve 
your goal piece by piece. A time-consuming activity like 
lockpicking, deciphering or breaking a structure could be 
worth doing even during combat, if it tips the scales of 
battle in your favor. 
› An Extended Test is when one or more PCs do a series 
of attempts to attain a desired result described by the 
players. 
› Each attempt takes one round. The GM decides on the 
duration of a round. 
› On each round players, whose characters are 
contributing to the Extended Test, describe what their 
character does to progress toward the desired result. 
Other players describe what their character does in the 
meantime. The GM asks for suitable Skill, Profession or 
Attribute checks. 
After every success or failure the GM can describe how it 
changed your progress. For example "The campﬁre you 
found yesterday was a day old, but these tracks were made 
after today's snowfall." or "By the words you have 
deciphered you can now guess the real meaning of a third 
of this poem. It seems to be an order to kidnap someone 
from this villa." 
› If the PCs reach 3 successful checks before they reach 3 
failed checks on this Extended Test, they attain the 
described result. 
› A Critical roll counts as 2 successes or failures.
› If multiple PCs are contributing to the Extended Test, 
they can cooperate and do a Cooperative check. Its result 
counts as one success or failure on the Extended Test. 
The GM can use the same DC for Cooperative and non-
Cooperative Extended Tests as they would use for a regular 
check to achieve the same in-game result. 
› PCs contributing to an Extended Test during combat have 
to spend one Combat action (p. 25) on each turn that 
they contribute. 
› If time is not an issue during an Extended Test, then 
instead of doing a check every turn you can do it 
whenever you have spare time and resources and mark 
down the result. The GM decides whether you have 
enough resources for a check and how much time each 
check takes. Thanks to this you apply your skill more 
reliably - Critical rolls are not considered Critical for the 
outcomes nor for leveling up Skills. 
Following are some examples of Extended Tests.  
Example: crafting and creating art
You describe a desired creation, be it a song, a sword, a 
speech, a mural, a garden or a batch of craft beer. Describe 
what impression you want it to leave. 
The GM decides on a suitable Skill, a DC based on how ﬁne 
craftsmanship it requires and on the duration of a round so 
that 3 rounds would be enough to complete it. 
You could attempt to enrich the end result. For example by 
using a new ingredient, for which the GM might ask for a 
Brewing Skill check to prepare the ingredient. 
Or you could attempt to expand your understanding by 
leaving your comfort zone. For example by meeting some of 
the local citizens whom you intend to incite with the speech 
you are writing. The GM might ask you to do an Insight Skill 
check to understand what matters to them. 
Failing the Extended Test represents running out of 
materials in case they are scarce, time in case there is a 
deadline or money in case the materials are costly. 
› If the GM thinks the party can retry the Extended Test by 
spending more money, the players can decide to reduce 
their party's Lifestyle (p. 19) by 1 point to erase a failure. 
Example: pursuit and escape
The PCs do an Opposed Cooperative Running, Riding, 
Swimming, Tracking, Navigation or other appropriate Skill 
check to ﬁnd out if they gain on the target or lose them, 
escape or get caught. As with all checks Opposed by NPCs, 
DC = 11 + NPC level. 
Each round lasts 6 seconds or 1 minute for close chases and 
an hour or a day for tracking pursuit, so a handful of rounds 
would be enough to catch up or escape. 
› If the Extended Test is a chase, the party can accept 1 
level of Fatigue (p. 20) per PC, distributed among them as 
they see ﬁt, to erase one failure on the Extended Test. 
Each round the GM may tell you how the conditions of the 
chase have changed and hint at opportunities that you have 
as the pursuer to track your opponent, predict their 
destination, ﬁnd a shortcut or a faster mode of transport 
and as the pursued to disguise yourself, lay low, set up a 
trap or misleading tracks or take an unexpected path.

Lifestyle
› The shared Lifestyle of the PC party is either Cheap, 
Aﬀordable or Expensive and is determined by the total 
currency the PCs carry with them. Every product and 
service that is being sold belongs to one of these 
Lifestyles and can be bought or picked as starting gear 
by a PC with a Lifestyle of equal or higher level. 
So with an Aﬀordable Lifestyle the PCs can aﬀord 
everything Cheap and Aﬀordable. No more counting coins. 
› To move from a Cheap Lifestyle to Aﬀordable or from 
Aﬀordable to Expensive, the party has to gain 10 
Lifestyle Points of their current Lifestyle. 
› When you earn a reward or sell loot, the GM will let you 
know if that gained your party a Lifestyle Point. Precious 
loot and wealthy patrons might earn more than 1 point. 
› The GM can ask you for an Appraisal Skill check when 
you attempt to appraise how many Lifestyle Points is an 
item worth or for a social Skill check to prevent a 
borderline expenditure from reducing a Lifestyle Point. 
› Each time the party spends a large sum, the GM decides 
on a number of Lifestyle Points they have to deduce from 
their Lifestyle.  Ordering custom-made armour (p. 20) is a 
special case.
› The party can have more wealth than 10 points of 
Expensive Lifestyle, but the PCs can't carry it around and 
will have to deposit or invest it.

Fatigue and sleeping
Generally the player characters are assumed to have taken 
care of their basic needs according to the party's Lifestyle 
and to have equipped themselves with enough food, water 
and clothing needed to travel to the destination they have 
in mind. But if the GM presents situations that deprive them 
of resources and the party's survival in the wild is called into 
question, the GM will decide how many days worth of water 
or rations the party has left and will draw attention to it. 
› Unless a character succeeds on an Endurance Attribute 
check, they gain 1 Fatigue for every minute without 
oxygen (O), every hour in a harsh environment causing 
hypo- or hyperthermia (H), every day without sleep (S) or 
water (W), every week without food (F) and every day 
doing exhausting work (E). 
Use a pencil to mark the letter of the Fatigue source into the 
next clear Fatigue box on your character sheet or an NPC 
tracker. 
Using an Ability (p. 33) or being aﬀected by certain Abilities 
can also cause Fatigue (A). Doing the Overexert Simple 
action (p. 25) always causes a level of Fatigue (E). 
› Upon receiving each Fatigue above 2 the character 
suﬀers a Wound, which symbolizes tissue damage and 
inﬂammation. The DC to treat this Wound is 11 + the new 
Fatigue level. Write it down in a clear Heart on the 
character sheet. 
› A full night of keeping watch and sleeping (or whatever 
your character’s species does to rest/recharge instead of 
sleeping) after removing a source of Fatigue gets rid of 
all Fatigue caused by this source. Clear related Fatigue 
boxes on the character sheet. 
› To remove Fatigue caused by use of Abilities a character 
has to refrain from using Abilities during rest.

Dying
› A Dying character falls prone (p. 26), can't stand up and 
can do two less Combat actions on their turn. 
This means that they can still do Reactions (p. 24) and 
Simple actions (p. 25), but the only way for them to do a 
Combat action is to Overexert and they can't Move because 
they can't stand up. 
› If an NPC starts Dying, the GM can decide that they died 
right away without doing Death checks. 
› At the start of a Dying character's turn do a Death check: 
Roll a d20 against DC 11. 
»
On a 3rd success they stabilize and stop Dying. 
»
On a Critical success they instantly heal their latest 
Wound: "It was just a ﬂesh wound". 
»
Critical failure counts as 2 failures. 
»
On a 3rd failure they die (and are no longer Dying). 
Think of death as an opportunity to try out any other 
character concepts you might have. If you were deeply 
invested in the dead character, you can create a new one 
with a similar personality and interests, but a diﬀerent past, 
appearance, new equipment and of course none of the 
dead character's private knowledge. The GM will then think 
of how to acquaint them with the party. 
 
› If an ally enters your space, holds up your head and yells 
"Don't die on me!" as Handle and Communicate actions, 
you don't do Death checks while their character stays 
conscious in your space. The ally can continue ﬁghting 
and can drag (p. 25) your character along when they 
Move. 
› Instead of a Death check you can decide to do The Last 
Stand. Your character stops Dying, gains 3 Armour Points 
and an Advantage on all checks until the end of this 
combat. Every move they make feels like torture, but 
they force through. If they are alive by the end of this 
combat, they die. 
› If a Dying character remains on the combat map by the 
end of combat, they stop Dying and remain at the mercy 
of the victors.

Combat and stealth
Stealth
› At the start of an encounter do a Sneaking Skill check for 
each character or group of NPCs who is trying to remain 
unnoticed. This check is opposed by the opponent with 
the lowest Sneaking + Thief or NPC level and determines 
which noisiest character on each side alerts the other 
side ﬁrst. If this check is opposed by concealed 
characters who are waiting in ambush, then do a 
Concealment Skill check instead of Sneaking to 
recognize ﬂaws in their Concealment. 
»
Characters who fail this check or who do not attempt 
it become Spotted (p. 23) until this encounter ends. 
»
Characters who succeed on this check manage to 
remain unspotted and get a chance to observe the 
encountered opponents and listen in on their 
conversations from behind Cover (p. 23).
Combat and Surprise
In Fatebenders the only encounter that starts as a combat is 
an ambush, because one side is already aware of the other 
and has had enough time to evaluate their opponent, 
decide to attack and set up an ambush. All other encounters 
start with the GM telling where the party is located and 
what characters or objects of danger or interest do they see. 
Then players describe the location and the NPCs that their 
characters try talking to using the Descriptive Decks (p. 14). 
Even if the NPCs see the party as an immediate threat and 
even if their behaviour options are as simple as that of a 
golem or an insect, it still takes them a moment to decide 
on how to handle this threat. 
› When the party Spots apparently hostile NPCs in an 
encounter, there is always enough time for at least one 
attempt to talk to these NPCs before a combat or a chase 
(p. 18) can start. Naturally the NPCs don't have to answer.
› Combat starts when someone in an encounter is about to 
use violence against someone else and the GM thinks 
that the party might not come out unharmed. 
› At the start of combat, before any violent actions are 
resolved, the GM asks all PCs who have a chance to be 
caught oﬀ guard by this combat to do an Opposed 
Insight Skill check against a hostile NPC with the lowest 
NPC level to notice their hostile intent in time to react. 
PCs who fail this check are Surprised - they are 
unprepared for combat and skip their ﬁrst combat turn. 
› Combat ends when only one of the sides is able to 
continue ﬁghting or when all sides decide not to ﬁght. 
Rules describe how to play out combat on a grid map, but 
depending on the nature of each encounter the GM can 
decide to instead play it out in zones, in theater of the mind 
or without a grid as described in the Playing out combat 
chapter (p. 55). 
 
Rounds and turns
› Time in combat is split into rounds. A round lasts about 6 
seconds of in-game time. During each round: 
»
First each player takes a turn with their PC.
»
Then the GM takes a turn with each opposing NPC 
and then with each allied NPC. 
»
Then the player responsible for tracking character 
conditions removes expired conditions. 
»
Then the next round begins round after round until 
combat ends.  
There is no order of initiative. This makes teamwork and 
formations quite powerful for both sides. Players can agree 
on a diﬀerent turn order among their characters each 
round. Also, players who immediately know what they want 
to do don't need to wait and players who need time to think 
can take their time while others do their turns. 
Allied NPCs take their turn after opposing NPCs to increase 
the importance of player character actions and help allied 
NPCs stay in a reactive sidekick role where they belong. 
Combat map
› To set up the combat map:
»
The player who described the room's layout or the 
climbing/hiding opportunities draws the map and 
marks all the Rough Terrain, Obstacles and Cover
»
 The player who described the danger in the 
environment or the things that can be dropped, 
released or set on ﬁre marks them on the map.
»
The GM marks all apparent room entrances and 
places a token or a mini on the map for each 
character on the PC side and for each Spotted (p. 23) 
character on the opposing side, and marks 
characters on the PC side who have not yet been 
Spotted.
› If this combat is played out on a grid, then each square 
on a square grid and each hex on a hexagonal grid 
counts as one space. After a character Moves (p. 25) or is 
moved, they have to end within a single space if they ﬁt 
onto one. Each space maps to an area about 1.5 meters 
or 5 feet wide in the game world. 
› Reach of Movement, Abilities (p. 33) and weapons (p. 29) 
is also expressed in a number of spaces. Each space of 
movement and reach allows a character to move or 
reach into one farther hex on a hexagonal grid or one 
farther adjacent or diagonal square on a square grid. 
› Characters can Move into a space with Rough Terrain, 
but each such space costs as much as moving 2 spaces. 
› A space with a prone character acts as Rough Terrain. 


23
› An Obstacle is a structure, vegetation, furniture, hole in 
the ground or something else in a space that doesn't let 
you just Move through it. Instead you have to climb or 
jump over it, Attack to destroy it or use a diﬀerent path. 
› Every standing character is also an Obstacle and only 
allows their allies to Move into their space, but the allies 
can't end their Move action in that space and the space 
counts as Rough Terrain for them. 
› Cover is a solid opaque Obstacle. Characters can't see 
(p. 23), Spot, Attack (p. 27) nor use Abilities through 
Cover. Abilities have to travel around Cover to reach a 
space behind it. 
Visibility
› A character can see into a space on the combat map if 
they are not blind, blinded, blindfolded, unconscious or 
in total darkness and if there is no Cover, cloud or illusion 
obscuring this space, the character's space nor any space 
between them that would be cut by a straight line 
connecting their centers. 
This also means that as soon as combat starts, all 
characters become aware of their surroundings and see in 
every direction - There are no facing rules in Fatebenders. 
› A character gets a Disadvantage on Attacks against a 
target that they can not see if the character mainly relies 
on sight to sense opponents. 
› A character gets an Advantage on Attacks against a 
target that they can see if the target can not see the 
attacker and mainly relies on sight to sense opponents. 
So characters ﬁghting each other in the dark would do all 
their Attacks with a Disadvantage. 
Spotting and Sneak Attacks
› A character can only be Attacked if they were Spotted by 
the opposing side at any time during this encounter.  
› You get Spotted and all opponents become aware of you 
until the end of the encounter if…
»
You Move or do any other action (p. 25) besides 
Ready or Steady Aim in a space seen by an 
opponent and that opponent survives until their turn 
to alert their allies; 
»
Or an opponent starts their turn in a space from 
which they see you or Move into such a space, 
recognize your concealment, in case you are 
concealed (p. 23), and alert their allies; 
»
Or an opponent hears, smells or in other way senses 
your location, recognizes you as dangerous and 
survives until their turn to alert their allies. 
So it is possible to stay still and concealed under an 
opponent's nose if that nose is not too sharp. 
It also means that if you started your turn unspotted, then 
you can't be Attacked with an Opportunity Attack nor a 
Readied action until an opponent starts their turn and alerts 
their allies. 
When an opposing NPC is Spotted, the GM adds their mini 
or token onto the combat map. When an allied character is 
Spotted, remove the marker that marks them as unspotted. 
› Sneak Attack: If a character starts their combat turn not 
Spotted, then all Opposed checks that they do during 
this turn gain an Advantage. 
»
If any of these checks is a Brawling or Short weapon 
Attack, they can forgo the Advantage to aim for the 
back of the target's head. If this Attack hits and the 
target can be knocked unconscious, then in addition 
to other on-hit eﬀects the target is knocked 
unconscious (falls prone, can't do actions and can't 
see) until the GM decides they wake up. 
Concealment
› You can spend an hour to camouﬂage a number of 
objects or willing characters up to your Concealment + 
Hunter level to conceal their appearance. 
› Until this concealment is physically ruined (for example 
removed before going to sleep), characters moving into 
a space from which they can see the concealed character 
or object only Spot it if they succeed on an Opposed 
Concealment Skill check against you. On a failure they 
can't retry the check to Spot the same camouﬂaged 
character or object as long as it stays in the same space. 
So concealment prevents a character from Spotting you 
only when they Move or are moved into a space from which 
they can see you and is useless against them Spotting you 
when you move or act while camouﬂaged or give yourself 
away with sound or smell.

Traps
› You can spend an hour to make, set and conceal a 
number of traps up to your Traps + Hunter level. Each 
trap occupies one space on the combat map. Describe 
how they work and how they are constructed. 
› If you trigger a trap, you suﬀer a Wound unless you 
succeed on a Dodging Skill check Opposed by the Traps 
Skill of the one who set it. The DC to treat this Wound is 
the same as the DC to dodge the trap. Write the DC in a 
clear Heart on your character sheet. Treatment checks 
get a Disadvantage if the trap was venomous - add a "D" 
to the Wound's DC to indicate this. 
› You can ﬁnd all traps in an area by carefully inspecting it. 
Do a Concealment Skill check Opposed by the one who 
set the traps. You ﬁnd all of them regardless of your 
result but on failure you trigger one in the process. 
› To disarm a trap, describe what your PC does to disarm 
it. The GM might call for a Traps Skill check if your 
described actions have a chance to fail. On failure you 
trigger the trap. 
› Falling can cause more than one Wound at the GM's 
discretion. A character avoids taking one of these 
Wounds on a successful Acrobatics check. 
Flanking
› Two allied characters gain an Advantage on Attack 
checks  (p. 27) against an opponent if both of them can 
see the opponent and can Attack the opponent from 
their current spaces with a Short or Long weapon or a 
Brawling Attack, and a line connecting the centers of 
their spaces cuts the opponent's space. This Advantage is 
called Flanking.  
Opportunity Attacks
In combat your enemies are looking for gaps in your 
defenses. They strike when you expose yourself or waver 
within their weapon's reach. Opportunity Attacks emulate…
› The danger of trying to close with an enemy through 
their weapon's superior reach. 
› The danger of shooting a ranged weapon when someone 
is hacking at you. 
› The reality of opponents trying to hit each other at the 
same time. 
› The speed advantage that balanced swords and bows 
with lighter draw weight have. 
› And the way that making a mistake can put you in a 
vulnerable position. 
Opportunity Attacks also reward teamwork and make your 
position in a ﬁght important. 
› Every character can only do one Reaction before the 
start of their every turn. 
› When you provoke an Opportunity Attack from an 
opponent, they can spend their Reaction to do an Attack, 
Disarm, Shove, Grapple or Hurl Combat action against 
you if they want to. For that they have to see you and be 
able to do that action against you from their current 
space and with their currently equipped weapon. 
› Before a conscious character Moves (p. 25) or is moved 
out of reach of an opponent's Brawling Attack or 
equipped Short or Long weapon, they provoke an 
Opportunity Attack from that opponent. 
› Before a conscious character Moves or is moved into an 
area within 1 space of an opponent equipped with a Long 
weapon or within 2 spaces of an opponent equipped 
with a hunting bow (p. 30), they provoke an Opportunity 
Attack from that opponent. 
Rules on Ranged weapon Attacks (p. 29), Critically 
succeeding and failing Attacks (p. 27) and Critically failing 
Intimidation (p. 26) also describe ways to provoke 
Opportunity Attacks. What they have in common is… 
› When your Combat action provokes Opportunity Attacks 
against you, these Opportunity Attacks are done right 
after the action that provoked them. 
So Opportunity Attacks are done before the movement or 
after the Combat action that triggered them. 
› If a Mounted character provokes an Opportunity Attack, 
each provoked opponent decides whether to Attack the 
character or their mount.

Actions
› During their turn in combat a character can do 1 of each 
Simple actions and any 2 Combat actions in any order.
All the Simple and Combat actions are deﬁned below. 
Simple actions
› Communicate concisely. 
› Handle objects within your space: 
»
Swap - Sheath, put away or drop items equipped in 
your hands and equip other items in their stead.
»
Grab an object or a prone (p. 26) character with a 
free hand.
»
Interact with an object in another way not covered 
by any Combat action. 
› Move - When you do a Move action, you either 
disengage, run, swim, climb or ride. 
»
With a disengaging Move action you can walk up to 
2 spaces and any Opportunity Attacks you provoke 
with this Move action get a Disadvantage. 
»
With a running Move action you can run up to 4 
spaces or a number of spaces equal to your Running 
+ Athlete, whichever is higher. 
»
With a swimming Move action you can swim up to 2 
spaces or up to a number of spaces equal to half 
your Swimming + Athlete, whichever is higher. 
»
With a riding Move action you can ride up to 8 
spaces or up to a number of spaces equal to double 
your Riding + Hunter, whichever is higher. For that 
you have to be mounted on any creature that the GM 
thinks is willing and able to work as your mount. 
»
With a climbing Move action and a successful 
Climbing check you progress up to 1 meter or up to a 
number of meters equal to a third of your Climbing + 
Athlete, whichever is higher. 
»
It costs double the number of spaces to Move while 
dragging a character you are Grappling (p. 26) or 
something you have grabbed with the Handle action 
like a heavy object or a prone character. 
Diﬀerent slowing eﬀects multiply. For example with Riding 3 
and Hunter 7 you can ride up to 20 spaces per Move action. 
Riding against a stormwind (p. 35) reduces it to 10 spaces. 
Adding Rough Terrain (p. 22) reduces it to 5 spaces. 
Dragging a prone character reduces it to "up to" 2.5 spaces, 
which is 2 spaces in practice, as you have to end your 
movement in a space. 
»
To jump over an Obstacle, make one or more 
running Move actions that cross it. The GM can ask 
for a Jumping Skill check. For example if it's a gap, 
use DC = number of spaces jumped over * 5. The GM 
decides what happens if you fail. 
»
Moving over Rough Terrain might require a check - 
A Swimming Skill check to avoid Fatigue from 
swimming in a stormy sea or an Acrobatics Skill 
check to avoid falling oﬀ or prone  onto a narrow, 
slippery, yielding or moving path. One can also use 
Swimming or Acrobatics to attempt to ignore the 
slowing eﬀect of Rough Terrain. 
»
You can also use Acrobatics to add ﬂair to your 
movement with a slide, salto, roll, etc. Describe how 
impressive you want the result to be for the GM to 
give you a fair DC on an Acrobatics Skill check. 
Because it's just a ﬂair, failing it only means that you 
don't look as impressive as you described while still 
moving where you want. On a Critical fail you might 
fall prone in the destination space. 
»
You can do Simple and Combat actions mid Move in 
any space on your way. Do your Combat action, wait 
for opponents to do their Opportunity Attacks and 
use the spaces remaining from your Move action to 
move where you want. 
› Overexert - Suﬀer a level of Fatigue to do one more 
Combat action. You can also Overexert to gain an 
additional Reaction even outside of your turn. 
Combat actions
› Do an additional Simple action. 
› Use an Ability of an Attuned Element (p. 33).  
› Attack (p. 27) a Spotted opponent. 
› Break Grapple - Succeed on an Opposed Brawling Skill 
check to break a Grapple aﬀecting you or an adjacent 
character. 
› Command - Do a Tactics Skill check Opposed by a 
conscious opponent with the highest NPC level. Success 
reduces the level of all opposing NPCs by 1 until the end 
of the next round. A commanding opposing NPC instead 
increases their own level and level of their allied NPCs.
Describe how you command your allies to take a formation 
that takes advantage of the current terrain or of a weakness 
in the equipment or formation of the opponents or a 
tactical maneuver to gain higher ground, better Cover or 
visibility or to have the wind or sunlight at your backs. 
› Defend - Pick an opponent to defend against - They get 
a Disadvantage on all Attacks against you until the start 
of your next turn. If you do multiple Defend actions, you 
can pick either the same or diﬀerent opponents. 
This is a good action to default to when a player takes too 
long to decide on their turn or is away from the table. 
› Disarm - Do a Brawling, Short or Long weapon Attack. 
On hit instead of Wounding the target you force them to 
drop an object from their hands to the ground into your 
space. A Disarmed NPC with low morale will likely ﬂee 
this combat. A well prepared one might carry a sidearm.
› Extinguish all ﬁre in a space. To extinguish a burning 
character without using water, succeed on a DC 11 
Composure Attribute check. 


26
› Grapple - Do a Brawling Attack with both hands 
unoccupied. On hit instead of Wounding the opponent 
you Disarm and grapple them until you fall unconscious, 
release them or until your opponent Breaks the Grapple. 
While Grappling: 
»
You can release the Grapple at any time even outside 
of your turn. 
»
Short and Long weapon Attacks and Brawling 
Attacks gain an Advantage against both of you. 
»
While Grappled, the opponent can only do the 
Communicate, Use an Ability, Ready, Break Grapple, 
Persuade, Taunt, Command and Attempt Something 
Else actions. 
»
As the Grappler, you can only do the actions that 
your Grappled opponent can as well as the Move, 
Fall Prone (they automatically fall prone together 
with you), Intimidate and Inspire actions. 
»
If at the end of a round a character is Grappled and 
has no Spotted, conscious, non-Dying and non-
Grappled allies left on the combat map, they are 
considered taken prisoner - they are unable to resist 
and go where the winning side takes them. 
› Hurl - Do a Brawling Attack with both hands unoccupied. 
On hit instead of Wounding the opponent you pull them 
into your space, successfully Disarm and then Shove 
them into an adjacent space where they land prone. If the 
opponent is heavier than you, the GM might give you one 
or more Disadvantages on Hurling them. 
› Inspire - Do a Leadership Skill check Opposed by 
Composure Attribute of your allies. The allies who you 
succeeded against are inspired until the end of this 
encounter. Inspired allies gain an Advantage on all 
Composure Attribute checks. 
»
On a Critical fail of the Leadership check they get a 
Disadvantage instead. 
»
On a Critical success all allied NPCs will endure one 
more Wound before ﬂeeing this combat. 
› Intimidate - All opponents that can hear or see you 
within a number of spaces equal to your Intimidation + 
Athlete level have to do a Composure Attribute check 
Opposed by your Intimidation Skill to resist your 
Intimidation. Those who fail lose any Readied actions 
they had and are forced to immediately take a running 
Move action away from you. 
»
On a Critical fail they will endure one less Wound 
before ﬂeeing combat. If they were already at their 
limit, they instead ﬂee right away. 
»
On a Critical success you instead provoke their 
Opportunity Attack. 
› Mount or Dismount. 
You can put a coin or something else under the character's 
mini to indicate that they are mounted. 
› Persuade opposing NPCs to stand down - describe it as

this could halt the combat, they will ask you to do a 
Persuasion Skill check Opposed by the opponents with 
the highest NPC level. On success you convince all 
opponents who are currently not Taunted to stand down 
and start talking. 
› Progress on an Extended Test. You can only progress on 
the same Extended Test once per round. 
› Ready - Declare the name of a Simple or Combat action 
that you are readying. You can interrupt any other 
character's turn, even mid movement, to perform this 
action once before your next turn begins. After your 
interruption the character continues their turn. 
› Shove - On a successful Opposed Strength Attribute 
check against an adjacent character you shove them one 
space away from you. If there are other characters in a 
row behind them and your Strength Attribute check 
exceeds all of their DCs as well, they are all shoved, 
otherwise no one is. 
› Stand Up or Fall Prone. 
»
A prone character can't do Attacks (except with a 
loaded crossbow, see p. 32), mount, Move, Shove, 
Intimidate nor Inspire, falls oﬀ their mount and falls 
to the ground, if they were ﬂying. 
»
Brawling, Short and Long weapon Attacks against 
prone opponents gain an Advantage. 
»
When falling prone you can feign your death. Do a 
single Bluﬀing Skill check Opposed by everyone 
seeing this (including your allies if they didn't expect 
this). Those who you succeed against as well as 
those who didn't see you fall prone believe that you 
are dead. For them you are no longer Spotted (p. 23) 
and, as if you were successfully concealed from 
them, they can't Spot you just by seeing you lay still. 
If you are Spotted again, they all realize that you are 
alive. 
› Steady Aim to gain an Advantage on your next Attack 
before you Handle anything, Move, are moved or 
Disarmed. 
› Taunt either all opponents that can hear you within a 
number of spaces equal to your Taunting + Performer 
level or one character who can hear you call them out by 
name. If both you and they are still conscious at the 
beginning of their next turn, they have to do a 
Composure Attribute check Opposed by your Taunting 
Skill to resist the Taunt. If they fail, they are forced to 
spend that turn Attacking you. 
»
If they won't be able to reach you with an Attack 
during that turn, they will ignore your Taunt. 
»
When Taunted out of combat, a character who 
doesn't want to ﬁght will instead Intimidate you, call 
for backup or retreat and later soil your reputation 
behind your back. 
› Attempt Something Else. The GM might ask for a check 
and narrate the result.

Narrating your actions
› When your PC does an action that calls for a check, 
instead of declaring the check result and waiting for the 
GM to see if it is a success or failure and to narrate your 
character's action, do it yourself. 
Don't spare the details on how awesome a success or 
failure it is. This takes the burden of narrating every move 
oﬀ from your GM and helps you roleplay your character in 
combat. Don't worry about your descriptions prolonging 
combat. They make combat fun, creative and set it apart 
from things you could otherwise do in a computer game. 
Ask the GM to reveal the NPC levels of all the Spotted 
opponents so that you could determine the success or 
failure of any Opposed check by yourself. 
Attack action
With the Attack Combat action you inﬂict a Wound on a 
successful Opposed Skill check called an Attack check. 
An Attack check is just like any other Opposed Skill check 
with few additional rules for diﬀerent weapons and 
situations. One Attack action can be imagined not as a 
single thrust of a weapon, but as a clash between two 
combatants initiated by the attacker, complete with faints, 
counters, balancing and maintaining distance. 
› If you use an equipped weapon to do the Attack, follow 
the rules of its weapon type (p. 29). 
› Otherwise you use the Brawling Skill to do a Brawling 
Attack against an adjacent opponent. 
»
Brawling Attacks are always nonlethal (see below), 
unless they are claw, bite or other Attacks that 
predatory beasts use to kill, or Attacks with a dagger 
equipped due to the Lethal property of daggers.
› When an Attack hits a character, they suﬀer a Wound. 
Write the Attack check result into a clear Heart on their 
character sheet. This is now the DC to treat this Wound. 
Treatment checks get a Disadvantage if the Attack was 
venomous - add a "D" to the Wound's DC to indicate this.
»
If the attacker decides that this Wound is nonlethal, 
then it heals in 1 minute. Instead of the Attack check 
result write an "N" in a clear Heart on the target's 
character sheet. If they would start Dying from this 
Wound, they are instead knocked unconscious. 
› A PC can choose what Skill to use to Oppose (p. 16) an 
Attack. They can use... 
»
Their Dodging Skill to dodge it, setting the Attack 
check's DC to 11 + Thief Profession + Dodging Skill; 
»
Their equipped shield and the Shields Skill to block 
it, setting the Attack check DC to 11 + Soldier 
Profession + Shields Skill level; 
»
Or their Brawling Skill or their equipped Short or 
Long weapon and the Short or Long Weapons Skill 
to parry it, but only if it is also a Brawling, Short or 
Long weapon Attack they are trying to parry. 
Write the dodge, shield block and highest parry DCs on 
your character sheet so you won't need to calculate them 
each time. 
› When Critically failing an Attack check describe how 
one of the following misfortunes aﬀects your character: 
»
They break their weapon.  
»
They slip or stumble and fall prone. 
»
They provoke an Opportunity Attack from an 
opponent chosen by the GM. 
»
Some other misfortune determined by the GM.
› Critical success on an Attack check turns into 
misfortune for your target. Describe an eﬀect: 
»
The Attack goes past the target's Armour Points. 
»
Pick one ally. You create an opening for them, 
forcing your target to provoke an Opportunity Attack 
from this ally. 
»
You trip the target prone.
»
You successfully Disarm (p. 25) the target.

Weapon types
› An equipped Short weapon occupies one hand and 
allows using the Short Weapons Skill to Attack up to 1 
space away. 
› An equipped Long weapon occupies two hands and 
allows using the Long Weapons Skill to Attack 2 spaces 
away, but not closer unless a weapon property allows it. 
› An equipped shield occupies one hand and can be used 
as an improvised weapon. 
› The GM can allow Attacking using a weighty solid object 
as an improvised weapon. Do a Short, Long or Thrown 
Weapons Skill check as appropriate. If the Attack hits 
with an odd number on the check result, the object 
breaks or becomes unusable as a weapon. 
› An equipped Thrown weapon occupies one hand and 
allows using the Thrown Weapons Skill to Attack as 
many spaces away as is the weapon's range (see Thrown 
weapon ranges in the item tables p. 30) or at a 
Disadvantage within double its range. This Attack 
unequips the weapon, but it can be equipped again in the 
space where it landed if the weapon remained intact. 
› An equipped Ranged weapon occupies two hands and 
allows Attacking into any space on the combat map 
using the Ranged Weapons Skill. This provokes all 
opponents to do an Opportunity Attack with an 
equipped Short or Long weapon or a Brawling Attack. 
› Ranged and Thrown weapon Attacks get a Disadvantage 
per each space with an Obstacle which the missile has to 
cross to reach its target and which is not adjacent to the 
attacker (because the attacker can lean out from behind 
an adjacent Obstacle before attacking). Remember that 
standing characters are also Obstacles. 
»
Such an Obstacle gets hit instead of the target if the 
Attack misses by 1. In case of multiple Obstacles, the 
second Obstacle from the attacker gets hit on a miss 
by 2, third Obstacle on a miss by 3, etc. 
Ranged weapons in Fatebenders have a shooting rate 
about 2 to 4 times faster than in real life. This is to 
compensate for the fact that physical and virtual maps 
where combat is played out are so small that they can't ﬁt 
the eﬀective ranges of Ranged weapons. As a result most 
combats start so close to the opponents that a reasonable 
combatant with a realistic shooting rate would almost 
never use any Ranged weapon.

Abilities 
Some characters and even some non-sapient creatures in 
Fatebenders have learned to freely control aspects of the 
world called Elements by Attuning to them. This control 
manifests in the form of Abilities. Characters using Abilities 
are called Mystics. Abilities give them power, but to use this 
power against their opponents, Mystics have to get 
dangerously close, as Abilities only work at short distances. 
 
Elemental Attunement
› To use any Ability, a character has to be Attuned to the 
Ability's Element and has to perform visible movements 
unique to this Ability. 
› A character can be Attuned to up to as many diﬀerent 
Elements as is their Mystic Profession level. 
› To Attune to an Element, a character has to learn for at 
least a week from a Mystic who is already Attuned to it. 
In rare cases a character with natural aﬀinity towards an 
Element discovers their ability to inﬂuence it by accident 
and then learns to control it through trial and error. This 
might be how elemental Attunement was repeatedly 
rediscovered through history. Although Attuning this way 
with no one to guide you takes a lot more time and eﬀort. 
› Once you Attune to an Element, you gain a new Skill of 
the same name under the Mystic Profession, for example 
"Water". Add it to your character sheet and level it up like 
any other Skill - through Critically failing Skill checks. 
Ability Forms
Every Ability has several Forms it can be used in, which are 
listed in its description. Possible forms are Self, Touch, 
Imbue, Hit, Chain, Cone and Sphere. 
› Every time you use an Ability, choose one of the Forms 
listed in its description.
› Self Form means that the Ability only aﬀects its user and 
their equipment. 
› Touch Form means that the Ability aﬀects an object or a 
willing character touched by the user. It can't be used on 
an unwilling character or any object worn by them. 
› Hit Form means that the Ability is used as an empowered 
Attack, Shove, Disarm, Hurl or Grapple Combat action. In 
case of an Attack action, any weapon can be used. If the 
action succeeds, its target and their equipment are 
aﬀected by the Ability in addition to all eﬀects of the 
Combat action. You can roll the Combat action check and 
the Ability check together to save time. 
› Imbue Form means that you touch a willing character 
and give them the power to use this Ability in Hit Form 
without doing Ability checks or needing to be Attuned to 
the Ability's Element until the end of the next round. 
 
› In Chain Form the Ability travels from its user through a 
number of consecutive spaces up to the Ability's reach, 
aﬀecting characters that fail to resist the Ability (p. 34), 
objects worn by those characters and objects that are 
not worn. 
› In Cone Form the Ability travels from the center of its 
user's space in a 90 degree cone in a direction picked by 
its user. It reaches all spaces within its reach that are 
covered or cut by this cone, aﬀecting all characters who 
fail to resist it, objects worn by those characters and 
objects that are not worn. 
› In Sphere Form the Ability travels in every direction from 
the center of its user's space. It aﬀects all characters 
within its reach who fail to resist it, objects worn by those 
characters and objects that are not worn. 
› If a character uses an Ability in a Chain, Cone or Sphere 
Form and then moves to another space, the Form doesn't 
move along with the character unless the Ability says so. 
Ability's reach
› An Ability in a Chain, Cone or Sphere Form can reach a 
space if the shortest path from the Ability's user to that 
space is no more spaces away than the user's Mystic 
Profession + Skill level in that Ability's Element. If the 
user is an NPC, use their NPC level instead. This shortest 
path can't pass through Cover or leave the Ability's Form. 
For example with 3 levels in the Mystic Profession and 1 in 
the Fire Skill you can use the Burn Ability in Chain, Cone or 
Sphere Form to reach a space up to 3 + 1 = 4 spaces away.
If you use Burn in Sphere Form in an area without Cover, 
then on a hex grid it would look more like a hexagon than a 
circle and on a square grid it would be either a square or 
something closer to a circle, if your GM prefers counting 
every other space Moved diagonally as 2 spaces. 
› A character can choose to hold back and use their Ability 
with a shorter reach, for example to avoid hitting their 
allies, but cannot exclude any speciﬁc spaces from the 
Form except for their own space. 
So every time a character uses an Ability in a Chain, Cone or 
Sphere Form they can include or exclude themselves from 
the Form.

› To resist an Ability in Chain, Cone or Sphere Form a 
character has to succeed on an Opposed check against 
the Ability's user. 
Chapters describing the Abilities of each Element also 
deﬁne what Attribute can be used to resist the Abilities of

to resist the Ability without a check. 
› If multiple NPCs with the same level are caught in the 
same Ability, the GM can roll once for all of them to 
speed up the encounter.

Procedural game prep
Leading Fatebenders game sessions is just as fun as playing 
in them.
Major chapters of this guide start by walking you through 
the steps needed to prepare or to lead your next game 
session and then follow up with chapters that give advice 
on how to make sessions more interesting or challenging, 
or how to handle situations that might come up during play. 
Steps for preparing sessions focus on making meaningful 
decisions that will likely be useful in the next session, like 
how to prepare encounters to challenge the current player 
goals and how to fuse the generated NPCs, districts and 
factions into a coherent and believable fantasy settlement. 
Aspects of these settlements that can be decided randomly 
are delegated to the NPC and settlement generators while 
giving ﬂavour and description to characters and locations is 
entrusted to players with a little guidance from the 
Descriptive Decks. 
Fantasy city adventures
Fatebenders gives you tools to prepare encounters in 
fantasy settlements of any size - cities for short. Why cities? 
Fatebenders is about the stories that arise when player 
characters struggle to pursue their personal goals. 
Cities are where most people end up when they are 
pursuing some personal goal other than a peaceful rural life 
or a life of treasure hunting or wilderness exploration. The 
treasure hunting and wilderness exploration themes are 
already well covered by many other fantasy RPGs.
Also, when adventuring in a city, all encounters are nearby. 
So when the party wants to switch focus from one 
character's Goal to another, they don't feel like they're 
losing valuable time. More importantly, it helps you show 
your players the consequences of their decisions and the 
impact of their actions when they return to the same 
locations and run into the same characters while staying in 
a city. Players who invested in building relationships with 
NPCs get to see their Attitude shift. 
With all that said, if your players are interested in a location 
outside the city, just let them go there and prepare 
encounters to challenge the Goal that led their characters 
there as you would in a city. 
If your past experience with TTRPGs is mainly about hunting 
treasure in dungeons, then think of attaining PC Goals as 
the treasure. Encounters are the rooms. Conﬂicts with NPCs 
are the traps and monsters and clues that lead the party 
from encounter to encounter are the tunnels and doors.

Supported settings
Fatebenders supports settings with: 
› A level of technological progress anywhere between 
early bronze age and pre-gunpowder. 
› Any number of sentient humanoid species and races. 
They are all treated equally by the rules, so you can have 
any races you want in your world and your players can 
play anyone they want as long as you're okay with 
adding such a species to your world. 
› Stories of more personal than world-changing scale. 
Changing the world is not necessary for a fun and 
engaging adventure. On the contrary, setting up an 
incredible domino eﬀect that will save or destroy the 
whole world or a kingdom and leading the player 
characters to unleash it would not feel like an earned 
consequence of their actions. 
› Magic is prevalent, noteworthy, rare, mythical or absent. 
The game is fun both with and without Abilities. You can 
agree with your players on which Elemental Attunements 
exist in your setting before you start playing. 
› Real or invented gods. There's no divine magic in 
Fatebenders. If gods are real in your world, then their 
every inﬂuence is a miracle. 
› Player characters both rich and poor, both powerful and 
weak. Getting rich and having access to all Elemental 
Attunements doesn't trivialize combat, social, crafting, 
stealth, exploration or any other aspect of the game. The 
party can recruit NPCs but they can't buy loyalty. 
› Both local and travelling adventures. If the players want 
to focus on solving mysteries, building alliances, playing 
enemies against each other or increasing their inﬂuence, 
then having a home in one or few most interesting cities 
of your world is better. On the other hand if players want 
to have less consequences from negative NPC Attitudes 
or explore new wondrous civilizations, then they can 
keep travelling instead of settling down. 
› Civilizations more populous than sparse. Historically, 
ancient city states grew to tens of thousands of people 
thanks to farmland irrigation in a hospitable climate. 
A populous world raises the stakes by making actions and 
decisions aﬀect the lives of more people. It also gives you 
more interesting locations and characters to put in the 
party's way and more ways for the consequences of player 
actions to catch up to them. 
Also many mechanics of Fatebenders work best when the 
party does not face mindless beings, monsters or beasts, 
but instead other NPCs who: 
› Are not obviously evil and can be reasoned with; 
› Desire more than just food, territory or money; 
› Fear other things besides death; 
› Form a lasting Attitude towards the party;
› And use diﬀerent kinds of weapons that add variety to 
their combat tactics. 
A familiar world
Your world doesn't need to be original for you to have fun 
with your friends. On the contrary, it's easier to focus on 
what's important when the unimportant details are familiar. 
When picking up a game, a book or a TV series, you expect 
it to have human-like characters doing human-like things in 
a recognizable world. Certain tropes are simply expected - 
concepts of what people had and could do in diﬀerent 
historical eras and in diﬀerent genres like fantasy and sci-ﬁ. 
Each departure from player expectations takes time to 
explain. An exception is not worth adding to your world 
unless it creates more awesomeness than it takes eﬀort to 
learn and keep in mind during play. 
So avoid global exceptions like a pink sky, blue grass or a 
diﬀerent calendar with new month names that you 
constantly have to remind yourself and your players when 
describing most events or locations. Instead use tropes that 
are familiar to your players and keep your exceptions local, 
like a wondrous landscape, an impossible structure, a 
unique creature or a fascinating new species that only 
inhabits a certain region. 
The only departures from expected reality that Fatebenders 
comes prepackaged with are an ancient or medieval society 
and the magic system of Mystic Attunement. Both build 
heavily on popular tropes and Attunement is in fact 
optional - if you and your players want to play in a historical 
setting, you can simply decide that no one is Attuned to any 
Element. 
Using your favourite settings
Fatebenders doesn't deﬁne a single world for all games to 
take place in. That would be a lot of arbitrary facts for you 
to learn and would make all campaigns a bit samey. Instead 
Fatebenders gives you tools to build your own world, one 
kingdom, settlement and character at a time. 
It bears repeating that your world doesn't need to be 
original to have fun in it. If you like a book, a TV series, a 
computer game, an RPG setting book or a published 
adventure of another RPG, go ahead and salvage what you 
like - names, characters, factions, conﬂicts, creatures, items, 
locations, whole towns, maps, cultures, species or anything 
else. Make them your own and use them in your current and 
future games. 
If you are adapting aspects of a well-known ﬁctional world, 
then it likely has an online wiki detailing these things. When 
reading a wiki, keep in mind that you don't have to stay true 
to canon. You're reading to get inspired, not to ﬁnd "the 
correct answers". In fact it's hard and fruitless work trying to 
avoid changing the world that you are adapting for your 
game. It's your world now.

Example setting outline
Here's an example setting that you can use for your games 
where anyone can become a "god" and where no one and 
nothing is out of reach. Let's call it Kamikoen.
Adventures take place on a planet with one or more 
civilized species shattered into many small kingdoms and 
city states by natural barriers to travel like mountain ranges, 
canyons, rivers, lakes, seas, glaciers and deserts. 
Roads between kingdoms are few and travel is slow and 
perilous. This has caused the cultures of even neighbouring 
kingdoms to drift apart and become diverse, reminiscent of 
ancient historic civilizations. This gives the game a variety 
that a world of large empires could not.
Small kingdoms also bring conﬂicts between factions 
closer to the player characters and make the factions more 
personal and easier for the party to associate with or 
confront locally. 
Kingdoms are mostly ruled by kings or queens of royal 
dynasties and maintain a tenuous peace motivated by 
proﬁtable trade that's peppered by short-lived military 
campaigns for control over a mine, a port city or a castle. 
There are no inherently evil or good creatures, no gods, no 
souls, no afterlife and no undead. 
All creatures are either: 
› Mortals - Any creatures born of reproduction. They need 
sustenance and die of old age, but some species may 
have a very long lifespan. Intelligent mortals can extend 
their lifespan using the Ritual of Immortality (p. 72).
› Constructs - built by mortals using long-lost technologies 
or by other constructs. They require a source of energy 
and are subject to wear.
› Elementals - previously mortal creatures that Attuned to 
an Element, overindulged in manipulating it and willingly 
became one with it. As elementals, they don't age, don't 
need to eat nor breathe and create their own energy. So 
they are immortal, but can still be destroyed. They have 
no will of their own besides the compulsions associated 
with their Element as a force of nature. 
As a consequence - all known "gods" are actually either: 
› Powerful elementals that don't have any agenda besides 
the compulsions of their element;
› Powerful and well-preserved constructs that fulﬁll some 
unknown function with an important long-term impact; 
› Or powerful mortal Mystics who regularly extend their 
lifespan and work toward a long-term goal of 
domination, peace, reform, balance, revenge, freedom, 
hedonism, self-perfection, scientiﬁc discovery, 
exploration or something else. 
Individuals of all three categories are mystiﬁed. Powerful 
Mystics are often found leading a kingdom or a faction that 
spans many kingdoms like a Mystics guild, a school of 
philosophical thought, a merchants guild or a religious cult. 
They are often in conﬂict with each other's ambitions.

Social status of Mystics
It's up to you how prevalent any Elemental Attunement in 
your world is. This also determines whether Abilities are 
publicly used for mundane chores or kept secret and used 
only to gain an upper hand in a decisive situation. In some 
cultures individuals Attuned to a certain Element are 
revered. In others they could be shunned, enslaved, 
imprisoned or even executed. 
Secret arts
Mind and Illusion Attunement are great candidates for 
being the secret arts that are publicly believed to be lost or 
to have never even existed but in reality only a handful of 
powerful faction leaders and secluded hermits in the whole 
world are Attuned to either of them. A hermit would refrain 
from using Abilities of a secret Attunement and would only 
help someone Attune for an important cause. A powerful 
faction leader would either demonstrate these Abilities as 
miracles of a god or a holy man or they would use these 
Abilities in secret to get what they want through deception 
or through an unfair advantage. 
Visits to space
Your setting might have legends of Mystics that visited 
outer space and survived to tell about it, as Continuous use 
and meditation (p. 34) allow high level Mystics to ﬂy very 
long distances, possibly into space, taking their own air 
with them combining Fly and Blow or travelling in a bubble 
of water containing a pocket of air using Flow. 
Mystic Professions
Being Attuned to an Element gives a distinct advantage in 
some professions and gives rise to new professions that 
don't exist in our world. Here are some examples:
› Freezer - A Water Mystic who patrols their city district on 
ﬁreﬁghting duty and chills the food cellars of the houses 
they visit during these patrols. 
› Lighter - A Lightning Mystic who sets alight the evening 
streets of wealthy districts with beautiful colours.
› Warcaster - A Mystic of any kind is valued in the military. 
› Cogniﬁer - A Mind Mystic who provides their lightly 
addictive Focus Ability as a service to nobles and 
scholars. They are also in a tempting position to sell the 
services of fortune telling, to swindle their clients or even 
to become a spy for another faction or kingdom. 
› A Mind Attuned character also has an advantage as an 
emissary, a merchant, a gambling dealer or a charlatan.
› Body Attunement is useful for a surgeon, a jailer of 
dangerous individuals or an elite bodyguard who can 
Block oﬀensive Abilities and Cure Wounds.
› Illusionists could be exclusively Illusion Mystics.
 
› Most famous musicians might also be Illusion Attuned, 
impressing the crowds by amplifying their voice, creating 
absolute silence and unique sounds from all directions. 
› Earth Attunement gives a unique advantage to masons 
and sculptors.
› A sailor with Wind Attunement can make a fortune and 
raise their status to that of a captain.
Attuned creatures
Elemental Attunement need not be limited to civilized 
species. The wildlife, pets and mounts in your setting could 
also be Attuned: 
› Wind Attuned bird species have a signiﬁcant advantage 
in hunting or ﬁghting oﬀ predatory birds by using Blast 
or Blow against them or to ﬂy higher than other birds. 
› Earth Attuned species could have evolved to quickly 
burrow through sand or stone and ensnare their prey. 
› Water Attuned marine species are untouchable in water. 
The same can be said about terrestrial species with Fire 
or Body Attunement. 
› Body Attuned species could have evolved to use the 
Block Ability to prey on other Attuned species. 
› Any species with a big enough brain could be sapient, if 
they are Mind Attuned. They might be able to send 
memories of their senses or emotions and might even 
have developed a visual language that uses geometry, 
patterns and colour. They might live in herds or hives that 
exhibit beautiful synchronicity or collective intelligence. 
› Lightning Attuned marine species would use Shock or 
Flash for self-defense and hunting and Glow for hunting 
in deep dark waters. 
› Illusion Attuned species would often use Show or 
Disguise to seem like a predator when moving and like a 
rock when staying still or using Play to spook predators 
who enter their illusions. They would put up a Show and 
Play for incredibly elaborate mating performances. 
Example Attuned creatures
Your setting could have giant lizards that are Attuned to 
Lightning as a means to shock and stun faster prey. They 
are slower than horses and more dangerous to tame and 
train - but despite all that, they are still in high demand as 
mounts. This is thanks to their ferocity in battle and their 
ability to climb steep cliﬀs and even walls, which gives 
them an unmatched advantage during sieges and in 
mountainous landscapes. 
Your setting could also have pygmy owls or ﬂying squirrels 
that are Attuned to Wind as a means to cross longer 
distances between trees or to escape larger predatory 
birds. As tiny and irresistibly cute as they are, they have 
become highly sought-after as a pet among the nobles.

Smugglers! For a drink they'll tell you what goes on in a port 
across the sea. For a fee they'll take you there. Double the 
fee and they won't sell you as slaves upon arrival. Triple it 
and they'll make sure no one knows of your arrival and what 
you brought with you, unless of course someone pays them 
more. 
If this district is absent from your city, then there is some 
important reason why this city grew in a place with no river 
or sea access. All trade is done over land and this city 
depends heavily on maintaining and securing the roads to 
neighbouring cities that are traversable by wagons and the 
outposts along these roads. Any smugglers work solo on 
these roads and don't form a faction. 
Workshops
This is where master craftsmen and their apprentices 
produce the goods that this city is known for. Here you'll 
ﬁnd their stalls, workshops, warehouses and the crafts 
guild. The crafts guild members settle disputes on quality of 
produce, judge works of apprentices who want to become 
masters and issue permits to sell foreign handiwork in this 
city. Alas they are not as impartial toward potential 
competitors as they say they are. 
If this district is absent from your city, then this city is not 
known for any particular crafts. The few workshops in this 
city don't form a faction and are dispersed between other 
districts, commonly the lower city or the market district. 
 
 
Market
A lot of this city's trade is operated by a foreign merchants 
guild that has opened their guild house in this district. 
If this district is absent from your city, then there are also no 
traders guilds. Instead there's a food market in the lower 
city. If there's a workshop district, it has a wares market. If 
there's an upper city, it has a clothes market. If there's a 
docks district, it has a ﬁsh market.

Here you'll ﬁnd a mixture of temples of all the gods 
worshiped in this city. One of them stands grandest atop

god-fearing folks of all standings. This is where the party 
could request council with the head priest.
If this district is absent from your city, then so are any holy 
relics or landmarks. Any temples are dispersed between 
other districts and are maintained by families rather than 
organized religious orders. In that case traditional religious 
rites are led by the king, magistrate or village chief. 
Castle
Here resides the king or queen with their royal dynasty or at 
least a regent or head of a noble family representing the 
royal dynasty's authority in this city. They hold the authority 
and the duty to command armed forces, collect taxes, pass 
judgment on crimes and disputes, pass new city laws, order 
grand construction projects, attend events and give 
opening speeches at annual festivals. 
If this district is absent from your city, then the city is ruled 
by a council of elders or elected senators who don't have a 
clear leader and gather regularly in a town hall in the most 
populous district. 
Entertainment district 
A criminal organization operates here behind closed doors 
and after sunset. It deals in slavery, gambling, loan 
sharking, armed robbery, blackmail, spreading rumors and 
possibly prostitution or drug traﬀicking depending on how 
grim you want your setting to be. Its leader is a ruthless 
person of humble origins who worked their way up from the 
mud, earning the fear and respect of their peers through 
consistent and level-headed action and more luck than they 
let on. Maintaining this fear and respect is not a matter of 
prestige to them, but a matter of survival.
If this district is absent from your city, then so is organized 
crime and the few taverns, inns and theaters are dispersed 
between other districts.

An academy, gymnasium, dojo or other district for training,

Types of cities
If a city is wealthy or if it has plenty of legal or enslaved 
Earth Mystics, then its roads are paved and houses and city 
walls are made of stone. If the city is poor and Mystics are 
few or banned, then it has wooden houses, mud roads and 
palisade walls. If the city was once wealthy but is now in 
decline or ruined, then its roads are covered in weeds and

variety of species, cultures and goods. Inland cities have 
outdated traditions and a uniform populace wary of 
outsiders. Large cities have two concentric walls: The inner

while the outer wall separates the lower city and the slums 
sprawling along the river, sea or lake shore.

Guilds
The membership of a traders, masters or Mystics guild is

Expensive Lifestyle it is covered by the Lifestyle. Members

price (like 3 Expensive Lifestyle points). 
Crime and punishment
Player decisions and consequences matter in Fatebenders. 
Law enforcement in a fantasy city gives weight to these 
decisions and incentivizes ﬁnding alternatives to violence. 
Minor violations deserve a ﬁne or forced labour. 
Increasingly harsher punishments are ﬂogging, branding, 
exile, slavery and execution. 
In some corrupt kingdoms one can buy their way out of a 
punishment in case their guilt has not yet become public. 
The public always demands justice and rulers are obliged to 
deliver it. Although rulers themselves are above the law, 
only the richest of kings can get away with being publicly 
unjust. But laws are diﬀerent in diﬀerent kingdoms. In 
some, the same crime has a much harsher punishment for 
slaves and a more lenient one for nobles.

Running the first session
Inviting players
Simply tell your friends that you are going to run a 
Fatebenders campaign and ask if they want to join. Players 
usually prefer playing whatever game they are used to, but 
rarely pass up on an opportunity to play. They'll easily get 
over the hurdle of learning new rules during play as long as 
they're into what Fatebenders is about. 
Matching expectations
When inviting players, tell them what they can expect so 
they can decide if they want to join. 
› Is it going to be a short or a long campaign? 
In case of new players it's always a good idea to start with a 
short campaign to see if you enjoy playing together.
› How frequently do you plan to run the game? 
Ideally once a week or every other week. Playing less than 
every other week results in players forgetting what 
happened in previous games together with the excitement 
they had about the game. Playing more than once a week 
on the other hand leaves little time for you to prepare 
sessions and strains your motivation - and it's also hard to 
ﬁnd players willing to invest that much time into one game.
› Will your games be only oﬀline, only online or 
oﬀline when possible? 
› How many players are you looking for? 
Fatebenders works well with one to ﬁve players. Three is 
ideal for keeping everyone engaged. More players means 
less spotlight for each player and more time spent watching 
others play. The amount of encounters you get to play 
through in a session actually decreases with more players, 
so more players doesn't mean more prep per session.
› What is your GMing style?
When a PC attempts something incredible that would be 
unlikely to succeed in real life, do you set a hard DC to 
create a sense of realism and help players stay immersed in 
the fantasy or do you set a reachable DC to create a sense 
of heroic adventure or lighthearted comedy? 
When a PC does something dumb, do they suﬀer realistic 
consequences? Butt-kicker, specialist and power gamer 
type players prefer to have low to no consequences. 
Storytellers prefer games with believable consequences. 
Is it alright for players to use their character as a game piece 
to achieve their goals or do you expect PCs to act in 
character even when it is against their player's interest? 
How weird will your game probably get? Will there be 
encounters meant to shock or scare the players or break the 
fantasy with sci-ﬁ elements? 
Here's a good video from Seth Skorkowsky on player 
compatibility.

When you get together for the ﬁrst time: 
1 If there are new players, ask everyone to introduce 
themselves and name their favourite fantasy worlds from 
games, shows, ﬁlms or books.
2 If you haven't yet matched expectations for the game, 
now's your chance. 
3 Then discuss and agree on the following questions:
»
What creatures, inventions or things do you prefer not to exist 
in the game? 
»
What phobias or mature themes are you uncomfortable about 
being described in detail or encountered at all in the game?
»
Who would like to not receive any tactical advice from other 
players on what to do during combat? 
Tacticians prefer winning over staying true to their 
character. Method actors and specialists enjoy 
staying true to their character more than playing 
optimally. One kind of fun isn't better than the other. 
4
Next, present your setting outline, the starting kingdom 
and city and the "District and faction summary for 
players" generated with the city. 
5
Next, players pick one of the described factions as their 
starting faction. All PCs are members of this faction and 
have been working together for some time. Players 
come up with their party's role in this faction and the 
starting scene of their adventure. There are examples in 
the Starting scene and faction chapter (p. 9). 
6
Finally, players create their characters as described in 
the Character creation chapter of the game rules and 
send you a copy of the front of their character sheet or 
let you keep their sheet in between games.
7
Players discuss and agree on how their characters are

»

»
Players should continue deﬁning new Bonds until

Scheduling campaign sessions
Create a new campaign chat and add players who 
responded to your invitation to play in this campaign. 
In this chat agree on a weekday and time for game sessions 
that suits all the players. A weekday is easy for everyone to 
remember and keep reserved. Weekends are usually harder 
to keep regularly reserved than workday evenings. 
Absent players
The most dynamic and fun sessions happen with 2 or 3 
players, so as long as you and 2 players want to play and 
can make it to the next session, you don't need to cancel 
the session. There's also no need to come up with what the 
PCs of missing players were up to during this session, as 
player scheduling problems should have no bearing on PC 
behaviour. Just ignore their PC. They blink out of existence 
until their player shows up - then they appear back with the 
party as if they've never left. Give the player a recap of what 
happened and just continue with the game. 
Homebrewing Abilities
If a player comes up with an idea for a new Ability or an 
Element and you think the concept is good, then you can 
come up with how it works together. 
Answer these questions: 
› What Forms (p. 33) does the new Ability have? 
› What element does it belong to? If it's a new element 
with all new Abilities, decide on the most appropriate 
Attribute and one or two Mystic Skills that can be used to 
resist Abilities of this element. 
› What eﬀects does the new Ability have on spaces, 
aﬀected characters and their equipment? If the eﬀects 
are lasting, they only last as long as the user spends an 
action per round on this Ability. 
Don't worry about nailing the balance of a new Ability 
perfectly for the next session - If it turns out over- or 
underpowered, bring it up with the player between sessions 
and discuss ways to balance it. 
Principles of Mystic Abilities
When homebrewing Abilities, consider these design 
principles that the existing Mystic Abilities are based on: 
› Abilities can only aﬀect the world within the Mystic's 
reach. This forces them to put themselves in danger by 
getting close to their target and gives them a reason to 
use stealth, a disguise or deception to avoid alerting their 
opponents. It also gives them a good reason to learn to 
defend themselves in case they do get caught. 
› Abilities that make challenging problems trivial, Skills 
obsolete or roleplay unnecessary, are intentionally 
absent from the game, like abilities to divine information, 
force NPCs to divulge information or act against their 
better judgment, alter their memories, handwave survival 
or navigation, etc. Invisibility is left out in favour of more 
creative uses of camouﬂage, Illusion Abilities and social 
Skills. 
› Abilities cause Wounds only through interaction with the 
environment (Blasting a character into Cover) or a 
condition (Shocking a soaked character). It ensures that 
using Abilities is interesting, works better in cooperation 
with other players and doesn't trivialize combat. Instead 
Attacks remain the main source of Wounds. 
»
It's OK to cause Wounds with an Ability indirectly 
through causing Fatigue or setting a condition that 
will cause a Wound, if not cleared within a combat 
round. An example of this is setting a character on 
ﬁre with the Burn Ability, which can be put out using 
the Extinguish combat action. You could reﬂavour it 
to instead create acid and add it as a new Water 
element Ability called Corrode. 
› Abilities that increase or decrease the number of combat 
actions are also absent, like haste, slow, summoning, 
time manipulation and "save immediately or die/become 
excluded from combat" abilities, like banishment, 
polymorph, imprisonment, etc. 
› Abilities that make healing, creation or ﬁxing things as 
easy as breaking them are also left out. Abilities should 
never be able to remove Fatigue, as this would allow 
endless use of Abilities. Creation abilities would make 
the Artisan Profession redundant. Also, creation and 
annihilation of objects wouldn't suit the more primal and 
less arcane low-magic fantasy feel of bending-like 
abilities. 
› The game rules don't deﬁne gods nor any divine abilities. 
Whether any gods or afterlife planes exist is up to you. 
Now that you know the initial principles, feel free to break 
them selectively and knowingly with new Abilities and 
elements to ﬁt your game style and the magic lore of your 
game setting.

Consequences for every action
Nothing makes your world more real and empowering to 
your players than showing the consequences of their 
actions. Important and risky actions having big and lasting 
consequences, but even trivial and simple actions having 
small consequences. These consequences can be expressed 
as an Advantage or Disadvantage on a check, a change in 
the behaviour, livelihood or Attitude of an NPC, a structure 
erected or destroyed, a faction or organization created or 
disbanded, etc. Grateful NPCs might spread the word or 
leave a pleasant surprise. Hateful NPCs might arrange an 
unpleasant one. A solved problem might improve life while 
an untended one might grow out of hand. 
When faced with a choice between a more believable and a 
more interesting, but still plausible consequence, pick the 
interesting one. For example if the enemy advance force 
defeats the party in combat, it might be most believable for 
them to kill the PCs, but that is also the least interesting 
thing that can happen. Taking them in as prisoners is also 
plausible behaviour and puts them in a more challenging 
situation to solve, raising the stakes in case of failure and 
creating material for awesome memories. 
If a player would make their PC act in a dangerous and out 
of character way, they might be missing a detail you think 
should be obvious to their PC. They might have forgotten 
some crucial fact that their character uncovered during past 
sessions. Remind them about that obvious detail or crucial 
fact before asking for any check, so the player has a chance 
to change their mind. Raise your eyebrows really high and 
state what makes the attempt obviously hard or dangerous. 
If they don't change their mind, go with it: drive the scene 
onwards in the most interesting direction you can think of. 
This blog post from Mythcreants has some good examples 
of how to say "Yes and…" to a PC action you wouldn't take. 
If a player describes their attempt in a way that brings the 
scene to life or enriches the world with detail, encourage 
this by awarding them with a Drama token (p. 18). 
Sharing the spotlight
Many players enjoy being in the spotlight. Many players 
also expect the GM to moderate the game and shine the 
spotlight on their character. 
During combat this is taken care of by the rules, where each 
PC has an equal number of actions per turn. 
Outside of combat it is easier to let each player take the 
spotlight as they see ﬁt. It's a more natural way to converse 
and have fun. But in case your group has some eager and 
outspoken players, they might take initiative and enjoy most 
of the spotlight, leaving more polite players quietly 
frustrated and, in the worst case, slogging through 
encounters behind the characters of louder players without 
being able to get a word in. Even the outspoken players 
might start ﬁghting over the spotlight and get frustrated 
with each other. 
One way to alleviate this is to talk about it with the more 
eager players and ask them to help you engage other 
players in the game. 
Another method is the following: whenever a player 
describes what they want their character to do and you 
think that other players might want to try a diﬀerent 
approach or compete for the same objective, ask other 
players to describe what their characters are doing. Then, 
only after you've heard each player's intent, resolve any 
checks and describe the results of these actions. Let the 
actions happen in the order that makes sense to you based 
on player descriptions and check results. 
Handling a split party
Sometimes a PC needs to split from the rest of the party to 
do something or meet someone privately, all with the intent 
of rejoining the party afterwards. Sometimes party 
members want to head to diﬀerent locales in a village or 
town to run some errands before rejoining. 
When this happens ask the players what they intend to do 
next and play out these activities from shortest to longest. 
So if four party members split up in a town and one of them 
gets in a ﬁght, another in a chase, a third does some 
shopping and fourth goes to sleep, then play them out in 
that order so that consequences of shorter actions could 
aﬀect the longer ones but not the other way around. When 
two activities, like the ﬁght and the chase, have the same 
turn duration, play them out turn by turn switching between 
split groups each turn. 
If a player wants their PC to split from the party without 
intention of rejoining, then take a break from the game and 
talk to them privately. Explain that unless they change their 
mind, their character will become an NPC under your 
control and they'll have to make a new PC, if they want to 
continue playing. 
If they are bored with their PC or want the PC to pursue a 
diﬀerent life or become an interesting villain, then talk 
about what character they would continue playing. They 
could take over an existing NPC during this session and 
create a new PC before the next session. 
Otherwise the player might have a conﬂict with another 
player or might have lost interest in the story or the 
playstyle. In that case it's best to end the session early and 
talk to them about it in private before the next session. 
Handling player conflicts
TTRPGs are games where some players invest deeply in 
their characters and that creates memorable and deep 
experiences. But it also increases the chance of conﬂicts 
between players as they reveal new sides of their 
personality during play.
If you notice your players clashing or some other behaviour 
that breaks any table rules you had agreed on, take a break 
from the game and talk to them privately. Explain what is 
ruining the fun for you or another player and look for a 
solution that you both agree to. If they keep breaking the 
agreement, you can either tell them that you would like to 
continue playing without them or end this campaign and 
start a new one without them as there is no reason to 
continue playing if players are not having fun.

Playing out combat
Every time you start a combat, you can choose the most 
appropriate way to simulate it. Your options are described 
in the chapters below.
Theater of the mind
Sometimes combat starts with only a few guards and the 
question is whether they get to raise an alarm or not. 
Sometimes combat starts with all opponents already in 
melee range, so reach of Movement, Attacks and Abilities 
becomes irrelevant. 
In these and other cases where your players care more 
about cinematic action and fast pace than about fairness 
and tactical advantage, save time and expedite play using 
theater of the mind. This means that you play out combat 
by describing what happens without using a combat map, 
tokens or miniatures. Every time a character wants to Move, 
Attack or use an Ability, you decide whether they can reach 
their target without Moving closer. 
Don't use this option against many opponents though, as 
every PC action runs the risk of misjudging the situation 
based on your description while actions of their opponents 
don't. 
 
Combat zones
Sometimes combat starts mid-air or on moving vehicles, 
where the surroundings change constantly and the vehicles 
should move in parallel, not each on their own turn. 
Sometimes combat starts while climbing a vertical cliﬀ, 
being far away from each other, on diﬀerent ﬂoors or in 
some other way that doesn't ﬁt a top view grid approach. At 
other times you might simply have no grid to play on. 
If any of the above is the case, simply split the combat area 
into combat zones: draw a circle or other shape on a paper 
or a whiteboard for each physically separated or distinct 
area. Put a token there for each character in that zone. They 
are all Close to each other, which means they can all reach 
each other with Brawling, Short and Long weapon Attacks 
and Abilities used at reach of 3 or less without Moving. This 
means that leaving this zone or using Ranged weapons in it 
triggers Opportunity Attacks and entering this zone triggers 
Opportunity Attacks from characters in it equipped with 
Long weapons or hunting bows. 
Zone shapes that touch each other are Near and shapes 
that don't are Far. Moving into a Near zone is like Moving 4 
spaces or 8 if the zone you're moving to or from has Rough 
Terrain. If you want a zone to be farther away than just a 
multiple of 4 spaces per zone, then instead of making it 
touch other zones draw it away from them, connect it with a 
line and on the line write how many spaces away the 
characters in that zone are from the connected zone. 
Abilities used at a reach 12 or beyond and Ranged weapon 
and Javelin Attacks can reach zones both Close, Near and 
Far. Other Thrown weapon Attacks and Abilities used at a 
reach anywhere from 4 to 11 can only reach Close and Near.

Combat map
When playing on a physical surface as your combat map, 
you can use a square grid, a hex grid or no grid at all. 
Grid
If you go with a grid, you can buy coloured markers and 
either order a suitable battle mat online, make your own 
mat or, if you don't want to bother cleaning the mat after 
each session, you can buy ﬂip chart paper with 1 or 2 inch 
square grid. 
A square grid works better for combat taking place in most 
buildings, because they tend to have a rectangular ﬂoor 
plan. That way characters don't have to zig-zag when going 
down a corridor, as they often do on a hexagonal grid. 
The downside of a square grid is that a character can move 
much farther diagonally than it can move laterally. To 
correct this skew you can either use a Hex grid for outdoor 
encounters or use an optional rule to "double every other 
diagonal" - Count every other square moved or reached 
diagonally as costing 2 spaces instead of 1. Counting 
diagonals this way is more hassle, but it reduces the unfair 
advantage of diagonal movement being faster than lateral. 
Long weapons would have to be excluded from this rule, 
because it would allow opponents to enter their reach 
diagonally without provoking Opportunity Attacks. 
No grid
The no grid option allows characters to move free of any 
grid lines as in real life, but requires you and the players to 
eyeball the reach or use a measuring tape with inches on 
it whenever you're in doubt whether a character's Move, 
Attack or Ability can reach its target. Why inches? Because 
miniatures usually have one inch wide bases - so to eyeball 
4 spaces of movement, simply "step" with the miniature 4 
times in any direction by approximately the width of its 
base. It's also easy to ﬁnd tokens that ﬁt an inch wide space.
For reach - measure the distance between the centres of 
the target's and attacker's tokens - If it's more than or equal 
to the reach in inches, then the target is within reach. 
Characters
For characters you can use miniatures, toy characters, game 
tokens (if they ﬁt an inch wide space), chess pieces or coins. 
If you have lots of dice, you can keep one next to each 
miniature or on top of the coin or token showing its 
remaining clear Armour points + Hearts. It's okay for players 
to know their opponents' health, because Wounds 
represent actual visible wounds. 
If you're using an upright whiteboard as your combat map, 
then you'll have to use marker blobs or magnets to signify 
characters. 
 
Conditions
There are many conditions that can aﬀect each character. 
During a session use the Conditions reference sheet at the 
end of the book to quickly ﬁnd any lasting condition that a 
character can be inﬂuenced by, what are its eﬀects and how 
long it lasts. 
The best way to track conditions is to mark the character's 
mini with a colour representing the condition. Get yourself 
some coloured rubber bands or other small colourful 
objects that ﬁt on the token or mini and move them along 
when you move the mini. These lack a label to connect the 
colour to a speciﬁc condition, but if you were to use labels, 
you would have to look for the correct label every time you 
want to set a condition. It's easier instead to declare the 
condition that the colour represents each time you use a 
new colour in combat. 
Terrain
If you're a wargamer and you already have little bushes, 
trees, buildings and minis, then making use of these is a 
natural choice for you. 
Otherwise the easiest way to indicate Cover, Obstacles and 
Rough Terrain in the environment is to draw them on a 
whiteboard or a grid mat as your combat map with erasable 
markers of diﬀerent colours and a cloth or a felt eraser. You 
can also draw on paper and spread a new sheet for every 
new encounter. You can draw contour lines to symbolize 
altitude change on steep outdoors terrain and draw ﬂoors 
or levels of the same building side by side. 
Beyond drawing the combat map there are other ways to 
represent the environment in ﬁner detail that require more 
preparation and investment like buying ﬂoor tiles, walls of 
foam, tokens for furniture, statues, containers, and others, 
but they won't be covered in this guide. 
Virtual tabletop
If your group can't gather at the same table, play online 
using a virtual tabletop (VTT). For example a free Roll20 
account has everything you might need like video calling, 
virtual dice and combat map assets.

Extras
Extras are NPCs who are not willing to risk their life when 
confronting the party. The group of thugs in the previous 
example are likely all Extras. 
Extras are often motivated by money or a desire and not by 
a personal stake, loyalty or ideals. So after taking a Wound 
or being Disarmed in combat they lose their will to ﬁght and 
either ﬂee, fall unconscious or fall prone while screaming 
and holding their wound. 
Fleeing sounds like the most logical of these 3 options but 
because they can't actually start ﬂeeing until it's their turn, it 
fails to telegraph that this opponent has given up ﬁghting 
and so player characters continue ﬁghting them. Instead 
describe how the mercenary screams and falls prone or 
how the ﬁre imp theatrically falls over and plays dead. 
When you are not sure whether an NPC is an Extra or if they 
will continue ﬁghting, do a Composure check Opposed by 
the one who Wounded or Disarmed them. If they fail, 
they're an Extra. 
An Extra becomes a full-ﬂedged normal NPC when you 
decide to assign them an Attitude. For example they 
survived a ﬁght with the party, but sustained a Wound and 
are now seeking vengeance. You assign them the Hateful 
Attitude and use them to prepare a new encounter. 
Encounter difficulty
Fatebenders is not a game where players have to be able to 
defeat everyone they meet. It's a game where players need 
to believe that the world is real, that their actions have 
consequences and that there are always other options 
besides combat. So focus on making your encounters 
interesting and let your fantasy roam free without worrying 
about diﬀiculty. Present your world as it is and let your 
players decide which ﬁghts to pick. 
But to make a meaningful decision players need 
information. This is why it's important to let players decide 
if they want their characters to avoid being Spotted with a 
Sneaking check at the start of every encounter. A hidden 
party has a chance to observe what the encountered 
characters are doing, listen in on their conversation and 
gather information that can open up more options aside 
from "ﬁght" or "run". This gives fuel for attempts to trade, 
negotiate, deceive, seduce or intimidate the encountered 
NPCs or try to gauge their NPC level with an Insight check. 
The more dangerous a threat, the more obvious it should be 
from the behaviour of those around them. 
When you introduce potentially hostile NPCs who didn't 
expect to meet the party and who usually assess their 
situation before turning hostile, make it clear that the NPCs 
seem to be assessing the situation, but unless the players 
take action soon, the NPCs will. This gives players a chance 
to avoid combat, but conveys a sense of urgency to prevent 
a long tactical discussion. 
A good way to show urgency and focus player attention on 
an NPC is to make that NPC reveal themselves through an 
unexpected action that is triggered by the party.

Mass combat
If you want to give PCs an opportunity to participate in a big 
battle, think of some important mission they could 
undertake to help their side win and play it out as a series of 
smaller manageable combat encounters against a small 
part of the opposing force. This will be much more fun and 
epic than trying to play out the entire battle in some 
alternate ruleset for unit combat that takes away the 
combat actions and map scale familiar to your players. 
During these encounters you can engage the players' 
imagination by describing important moments of the battle 
that the party witnesses beyond the borders of the combat 
map and you can let opponents and allies run into and out 
of the combat map as you see ﬁt to keep the tension up. 
Playtesting encounters
If you don't feel conﬁdent about an encounter you prepared, 
try playing it out on your own before the session. This will 
give you a chance to think of how an NPC would start a 
conversation and you'll have plenty of time to look up any 
rules that you want to use in this situation. 
Another use for solo play is that you can play out a scene 
among NPCs without the party and keep its consequences 
for the party to discover when they arrive at the scene. 
In addition you can play out faction events that happened 
without the party's involvement to ﬂesh out more believable 
NPCs who actually experienced these events before 
meeting the party and can speak of their experience 
truthfully and in detail. 
Leaving clues
If you have prepared an encounter to challenge a PC Goal, 
but the players haven't found any clues leading to it yet, 
have forgotten or haven't understood the clues or haven't 
realized that the encounter is an opportunity to progress 
towards this Goal, then plant more clues in your other 
prepared encounters. Three clues at three diﬀerent 
encounters should be enough - they might miss the ﬁrst, 
misinterpret the second and ignore the third, but then 
change their minds thanks to some unexpected association 
and visit the encounter anyway. 
A clue could be an NPC's name or nickname, a name of an 
establishment, a map with several marked locations or 
something more challenging like an encrypted message or 
an object left behind at a crime scene. 
Drop additional clues generously when the party 
successfully questions a local or a survivor, or successfully 
interrogates a captured enemy. 
When players leave a city, they are seemingly walking away 
from all your unused prepared encounters, but don't worry - 
your work won't go to waste. Just rename the location and 
the NPCs, reword the way in which the PC goals are 
challenged or rewarded and you've got yourself a brand 
new encounter! 
Maps
Any world is large and full of inﬁnite detail, but practically 
all of it is irrelevant to the adventure that the party is on.
A useful map in a Fatebenders game depicts a settlement, 
outlines its districts and names, or numbers only the 
buildings and locations that the party has visited or is 
interested in. 
If the party buys, ﬁnds or is given a map of an area larger 
than a settlement and you want to hand it to players, then 
make it low in detail and symbolic. A good example are the 
maps of medieval Europe that, back then, were drawn by 
cartographers with limited information and a point of view. 
This way players won't feel like the map already contains 
everything that exists in that area and you can continue 
adding new locations as you prepare new game sessions.
Rewards
Fatebenders game mechanics are focused on your 
character fulﬁlling their goals, improving themselves and 
making friends and enemies in the process. So getting new 
equipment and Lifestyle Points is helpful, but not really 
powerful the way clever decisions, allies and skills are 
powerful.
Still, every NPC wants something. If the party ﬁnds out what 
it is and helps them achieve it, a reward is appropriate. 
If this NPC's Lifestyle is at least as high as the party's, then 
they can award the party with Lifestyle points (p. 19) 
described as coins or some non-perishable valuables that 
the party can easily sell. Otherwise they simply don't have 
enough valuables to increase the party's Lifestyle points. 
An increase in an NPC's Attitude (p. 14) is its own reward, 
where the party helps them enough to gain their favour, 
friendship or loyalty or to reduce their distrust, anger or 
hatred.
An alternative reward could be an item or information that 
can give the party an edge in another encounter that you've 
already prepared. 
An appropriate reward for bigger accomplishments could 
be a faster mode of transport, a title, some trouble-ridden 
patch of land or a base of operations like a stronghold, a 
tavern, a dojo or a guild house. Players grow attached to 
these things and don't want to lose them. This helps players 
care more about consequences of their character's actions 
and about NPC Attitudes. It makes them take sides in 
conﬂicts between factions. These rewards also grow the 
party's renown, give importance to being a member of the 
party and give you new ways to introduce new NPCs and 
deliver information to the party, because now the word 
spreads when they stop at a village or a town, or take 
ownership of a tavern or a keep.

Big projects
If your players decide that they want to found a faction, a 
business or a settlement, acquire a crewed ship, a manned 
fort or achieve some other major goal, ask your players: 
› What do they expect to achieve with that big goal? 
› What are the intermediate PC Goals that their characters 
are planning to reach to achieve that major goal?
› How do they think that these intermediate goals will help 
achieve the major goal? 
Reach an agreement on what will be the intermediate PC 
Goals and their eﬀects so that when the party achieves 
them, then they will achieve the major goal as a reward with 
the consequences that your players intended. 
Then, between sessions, think of what diﬀiculties their 
characters would believably face when trying to achieve 
these intermediate Goals and prepare encounters for them. 
If some of them don't inspire you with ideas for encounters, 
then turn them into an Extended Test where each attempt 
spends one Lifestyle Point and takes at least one day. 
When they reach their goal, let it have the expected impact 
on the world. That settlement, business, ship crew or unit of 
fort guards is now a faction - it has an NPC leader who 
decides what the faction does. In case of a settlement, it 
starts oﬀ with one district and one active faction and gains 
factions as it expands and gains new districts. 
Make it clear to the players that hiring a ship captain or a 
shopkeeper doesn't increase their Attitude - it doesn't buy 
their loyalty. A faction leader with a Neutral Attitude will act 
out of self-interest. But if their Attitude is Backing or higher, 
then they will proactively do what they think would beneﬁt 
the party as described in the NPC downtime events chapter. 
Such favours don't erode their Attitude towards the party 
unless the PCs asked for these favours. 
Skipping in‐game time
Sometimes you'll want to give the party a breather to let 
them recover from nasty wounds or to fast-forward in-game 
time to avoid the unbelievable coincidence of how all 
important and rare events happen in the span of a few 
weeks and how a group of regular people got skilled 
enough to face these events in these same few weeks. 
But usually players are reluctant to just sit around - their 
heroic sense of responsibility to save (Conquer? Burn?) your 
world won't let them. Here are a few ways to skip in-game 
time without taking away player agency: 
› Leave more days between important faction events.
› Make travel between settlements take days or weeks. 
Describe it as a cutscene and ask players to describe or 
talk out one night at the campﬁre. 
› When a PC researches a topic in a library, spies on 
someone, goes spreading rumors or asking locals for 
information, make it take at least a day and, if you think 
their approach could reveal something important, turn it 
into an Extended test where each turn takes one day. 
NPC downtime events
At the start of a campaign there tend to be few, if any, NPCs 
who have strong feelings toward the player characters. But 
as a result of PC actions the number of NPCs with a strong 
Attitude towards the party will grow. 
To bring the consequences of these actions to the party, go 
over each NPC in your campaign notes who has met the 
party during past sessions and survived:
› If you haven't deﬁned their Attitude yet, then deﬁne it - 
add a dropdown from the Insert menu -> Dropdown -> 
Attitude and set its value. 
› If the party met this NPC during the previous session and 
managed to shift their Attitude, then update it. 
› If the party didn't meet this NPC during the previous 
session and the NPCs Attitude is below Exploitative or 
above Supportive, then come up with their downtime 
event - an event in your campaign calendar that resulted 
from this NPC's attempts to help or hinder the party in 
the time that passed during the last session. This event 
should stay within the limits of this NPC's power and 
knowledge and reﬂect their Attitude towards the party. 
For example "Duke Roderick sent a messenger to the Owl's 
Beak inn to warn the party of the inquisitor who has arrived 
in the city and is looking for them." 
You can add more events that could be witnessed by the 
party as an aftermath of the ﬁrst one. Describe each event 
in a sentence, mentioning where the party would have to be 
to witness it. For example "Duke Roderick's messenger 
arrives at Owl's Beak." 
Just skip an NPC if the party is already too far for this NPC to 
help or hinder them, or if the party is so powerful or rich 
that helping them would be too inconsequential or 
hindering them would be too risky for this NPC's Attitude. 
If this NPC is a faction leader, then take into account that 
they have their faction's resources at their disposal. 
The outcome of interactions between NPCs or between 
factions during these downtime events is for you to decide. 
You don't need to roll dice when the party is not involved. 
Whatever you think should happen, happens. Whatever 
assets you think a faction has, they have. Whatever 
methods you think are suitable for the faction to use, they 
use, be it cunning, wealth, force or productive capacity. 
It's hard not to use your knowledge as a GM when writing 
downtime events, but try to keep in mind that not even a 
god in your setting would know as much about the party, 
their location, physical state, Goals, secrets and activities as 
you do. NPCs who have not done any risky spying have such 
a small amount of true knowledge about the party that you 
could ﬁt it in a sentence or two. Hence their downtime 
activity based on this information could probably also be 
described with one sentence. This approach will help you 
avoid overthinking NPC downtime activities.

Player agency
When preparing a session it's tempting to start writing a 
story - planning a sequence of events with mysteries, 
dramatic plot twists and NPC monologues and assume that 
the player characters will participate in these events and 
follow the plot like book characters. But while players might 
be able to guess at the general shape of the plot, it is not 
unlikely that they might want to focus on doing other things.
Players become invested in their character and in the game 
because they have control over their character's decisions 
and feel that they own the consequences of these decisions. 
If the GM would push the party to resolve encounters in a 
certain way and make all other approaches fail, that would 
leave the players feeling like their choices didn't matter. 
Coming up with a plot and then trying to lead the players 
back on track each time they stray from your vision of how 
things should play out is called railroading. Doing so would 
violate players' agency over their characters and can make 
players lose interest in the game. 
Instead, create problems, conﬂicts and interesting choices 
for your players and be mindful of your assumptions or 
hopes in favour of any speciﬁc resolution to these conﬂicts. 
After all, the best roleplaying experiences are born when 
characters do surprising things. 
 
Avoid deus ex machina
There's a dangerous kind of railroading that doesn't look 
like railroading on the surface. 
Let's say you want to show your players that their 
characters have a lot of room for growth, that they're weak 
and that your world is a dangerous place. It might seem like 
a good idea to lure them into an inescapable death trap or 
make them face an overpowering opponent and then save 
them at the last moment by an intervention from an even 
more powerful ally. 
The problem with this is - once the ally saves them, the 
players will realize that you had the solution planned from 
the start and that their attempts to solve this on their own 
were meaningless because the stakes were fake and the 
result would have been the same. 
Of course, in some cases it's fair and even encouraged to 
help your players out. For example when they have 
forgotten something that happened to their characters in 
previous sessions and you think their characters would 
deﬁnitely remember it, then it's alright to just remind them. 
If it's a fact about the world that you haven't told your 
players before, but their characters should know it and it's 
relevant to their current situation, then share it. And if you're 
not sure if the PCs know this fact, then ask for a Skill check 
to see if they do.

Character goal‐driven stories
Playing tabletop RPGs lets players experience another life 
in a diﬀerent world, in a diﬀerent body, with a diﬀerent past, 
diﬀerent relationships, facing diﬀerent challenges. They're 
free to face physical danger, defy authority, break social 
rules, express their thoughts and feelings with conﬁdence, 
splurge wealth, complete laborious projects, accept long-
term commitments, and try anything else with no regard for 
real-world consequences, no fear of losing face or not living 
up to their own expectations. They get to explore 
possibilities that would otherwise remain unexplored. 
Players want to play a character who solves problems and 
attains goals in some awesome way or style that's unique to 
their character. This way or style is often even more 
important to the player than their PC's Goal or the problems 
they create by playing in-character. The key to every fun 
game session is to give each player an opportunity to do 
their thing - to fulﬁll their vision of their character's 
awesomeness in new and challenging encounters. 
Prepares encounters introduce sources of conﬂict - either 
physical obstacles that stand in the party's way or NPCs, 
whose motivations are in conﬂict with a PC's goals or, more 
interestingly, are not yet in obvious conﬂict. The latter 
would mean that such a conﬂict could then be avoided with 
the right approach. 
Use the character sheets
PC Goals help you prepare conﬂicts that players will care 
about and let you anticipate where the PCs will pull the 
party during the next session. Each Goal is a path that a PC 
is willing to follow, if only you reveal it. 
Another source of inspiration for preparing conﬂicts in 
encounters are the PC Motives. Think of how these Motives 
can be used to tempt each PC into trouble or trigger an 
emotional response to an NPC's action. This might inspire 
their player to come up with stronger Goals for their 
character. You'll have to experiment with how hard you need 
to press these buttons that the players have given you to 
engage them without making it feel old or overused. 
Yet another motivation is progression. A PC's highest 
Profession level reﬂects their player's favoured approach 
to problems. It might not be appropriate for the encounter 
you have in mind, but at least you can count on them to 
consider this approach in every conﬂict. 
Know your players' interests
Here are common ways how players have fun in a TTRPG 
(inspired by Robin's Laws of Good Game Mastering by 
Robin Laws) and suggestions on how to prepare encounters 
for them in Fatebenders. Each player might be interested in 
one or more of these. 
Butt‐kicking
If a player enjoys beating opponents through sheer power 
(perhaps the only Profession levels they've taken are 
Soldier and Athlete), then the conﬂict that stands in the way 
of achieving their PC's Goal could be a ﬁght with aggressive 
opponents that give an easy excuse to attack. 
Tactics and optimization
If a player enjoys winning challenging ﬁghts through 
cleverness and coming up with optimal decisions within 
the bounds of the game rules, then the conﬂict that stands 
in the way of achieving their PC's Goal could be a duel 
against an NPC 3 or 4 levels higher than their character's 
weapon Skills or a ﬁght against a group of Extras (p. 59) of 
an equal level who use clever tactics. 
Playing an archetype
Some players just like to always do the one thing in every 
encounter without much care for how it will aﬀect events or 
resolution of conﬂicts. It could be a need to enact a 
character ﬂaw (e.g. lecherous or kleptomaniac) or a wish to 
play a speciﬁc trope from pop culture (e.g. a ninja). This 
doesn't mean you should make all encounters solvable by 
one approach. Instead make it clear ahead of time when it 
looks like a totally bad idea. 
A good way to let this player shine during sessions where 
their approach is otherwise inappropriate is to add an 
encounter with an NPC who has a positive or negative 
reaction to lechers/kleptomaniacs/ninja and will approach 
this player's character when witnessing the PC doing their 
thing or when recognizing the PC from a past encounter. 
Stealth
If a player enjoys sneaking their way into situations 
(perhaps their highest Profession is Thief), gaining unfair 
advantages, stealing, backstabbing, deceiving, disguising or 
having multiple identities, that gives you quite an 
interesting challenge as a GM. Think about what to do when 
this player's grand plan goes awry and their PC gets caught. 
Also, make sure that no single roll will take them from 
ﬂawless success to deadly failure. Instead milk as much 
tension and suspension out of the dangerous situation that 
the PC has put themselves in as you can. 
If they failed to sneak, they hear one person's slow footsteps 
and see opportunities for hiding, a sign that they have other 
options besides ﬁghting and aborting the whole mission. If 
they fail to hide and are found by the guard, switch to 
combat. If the guard is killed or silenced before their ﬁrst 
turn, then they never had a chance to scream. If they alert 
their allies, describe escape routes to adjacent locations 
that have opportunities to hide or mislead the guards. E.g. 
domesticated animals that can be spooked into making 
similar noises or some way to draw attention away, like by 
starting a ﬁre, some clothing to use for a disguise, etc.

Negotiation
If a player enjoys talking their way out of situations 
(perhaps their highest Profession is Performer), then it's 
important that you make it challenging and interesting to 
interact with NPCs. 
Don't just give them allies. Give each potentially powerful 
ally a negative preconception of the party. They might have 
a personal grudge against one of the PCs or see the party as 
an obstacle to some interest of theirs. Or they might be 
convinced that the party's approach to the problem at hand 
is not how things should be handled. 
It would be easy to just tell the players what an NPC thinks 
or wants, but that would break any player's suspension of 
disbelief. Show, don't tell - think of how NPC motives, 
assets and weaknesses would make them act and describe 
these actions or their results. For example "The orc steals 
glances at your belt". Your description will help players 
realize that a motivation exists. If a PC digs deeper and 
succeeds on an Insight check, reveal the NPC's motivation. 
Here's a good video from the creator of ICRPG on what to 
watch out for when running social encounters. 
Telling a story
If a player cares most about their character's story 
progressing (perhaps they're the quickest to jump into 
trouble), they're the engine driving your adventure forward. 
They're happiest when you fast-forward travel and 
downtime activities and keep up a fast pace during combat, 
negotiation and exploration. Then they'll get more chances 
to realize all the ideas they have about what their character 
would do next. 
Such players often start unexpectedly caring a lot about a 
random NPC or goal. You see this from how emotionally 
they react to that NPC, faction or quest. Don't ignore it, use 
it. First think of how that NPC they care about could be more 
interesting to yourself: develop them into an intriguing 
character. Then think of what important role could this NPC 
play in your other encounter ideas: e.g. they're secretly 
living a more complicated life, they fell victim to an 
antagonist, they accidentally found out things they weren't 
meant to or maybe they're a vocal opponent of a powerful 
faction. 
Hanging out with friends
In case of some players you'll feel like they're not really 
invested in the game, but for some reason they still attend 
your games - and yet you're not sure why. The answer tends 
to be easy: they're there because they enjoy the company. 
As long as you also enjoy their company, it's ﬁne. You don't 
need to prepare anything special for their character. Instead 
just help them out with character creation and rules, ask 
other players not to pressure them about playing optimally 
or true to their character, but also don't be afraid to practice 
your authority when they interrupt the game with oﬀ-topic 
discussions. 
Out-of-character discussions like tactical planning, silly 
jokes and oﬀ-topic discussions are a natural part of RPGs 
and might be the main reason why you're gathering. You 
can use this distraction to prepare the next encounter. Let it 
go on as long as everyone's interested. But if you or any 
player gets bored with it, it's time to bring players' focus 
back to the adventure. If it's a player's turn, you can ask 
them to ﬁnish it in 10 seconds to help them decide quickly.
Don't sweat it
Most players have fun in more than one of these ways, so 
it's okay if you don't try categorizing your players and don't 
base your session prep on these player types. But now you 
know that players can bring very diﬀerent expectations to 
your game, and you know how to recognize common 
expectations and the diﬀerent ways you can meet them in 
case you feel like you want to take this extra step.

Setting up interesting decisions
When planning an encounter with an opposing NPC or an 
obstacle in the party's way, try to turn it into an interesting 
decision for the players by presenting a situation that can 
be approached in more than one way and by adding a 
trade-oﬀ or a moral cost to the obviously better approaches 
and a beneﬁt to the obviously worse or harder approaches. 
For example the party comes across a fugitive slave who 
asks the party to help him attain his freedom. So far it's a 
morally good decision to side with him, no room for conﬂict 
and no obvious trade-oﬀs. Turn the party's attention to his 
brand or clothing and, on a successful History check, reveal 
that he is a scribe who has gone through an expensive 
scholarship and that his master is an inﬂuential noble - the 
upper city's faction leader. Now the players know that 
freeing him could worsen the noble's Attitude while 
returning him could improve it. There are now two options - 
a moral one and a convenient one and room for a conﬂict of 
interests. Look to present the party with more alternatives 
and information to make decisions more complicated: on 
their way they run into the crime lord's henchman who 
oﬀers a reward worth two Lifestyle points if they hand the 
slave over and pretend they didn't see anything, but if they 
don't, they will regret crossing her. This adds even more 
trade-oﬀs to trying to free the slave, as a third choice is now 
on the table. 
Taking a path around an obstacle could cost more time or 
maybe have a chance of dangerous encounters. Crossing 
the obstacle on the other hand could require success on an 
Extended Test, possibly risking detection and costing some 
spent equipment and Fatigue. Avoiding a run in with an NPC 
or a group could also cost time and risk detection or cost 
Lifestyle points in the form of a bribe. 
Conflicts from faction events
One source of interesting decisions are the faction events. 
Here are some ways you can turn a faction event into an 
interesting decision: 
› When the party ﬁnds out about the event, they also 
reveal that if they act quickly (no time for preparation or 
planning), then they could prevent its worst 
consequences or reveal evidence of the next event's 
location or time or of the faction behind the events. 
› You pass a paper note or a private message to a player, 
describing how their character found out that there's a 
chance of an unjust or immoral event happening (the 
faction event). But that speciﬁc event has an interesting 
side eﬀect that this player's character knows of - if they 
just let it happen, then they will greatly beneﬁt from it 
personally in some way. 
› One of the party members ﬁnds out that if they prevent 
the next event, the faction behind it will likely punish 
someone they know. 
Ways to obstruct a PC's Goal
› Present a short window of opportunity to safely and 
quickly attain this Goal by immoral means to challenge 
this PC's values and relationships with other party 
members. 
› If the Goal involves crafting, brewing, composing or 
other creation, then it requires a successful Extended 
Test and components or information that can be either 
traded (see #3) or gathered at another location (see #4).
› The NPC who can help fulﬁll the Goal wants something in 
return. For example:
»
They might want a very speciﬁc item that the party 
can ﬁnd, steal, trade or craft for them. 
»
They might want another PC's personal item that 
struck their fancy.
»
They might ask the party to perform a task that is 
dangerous, weird, immoral or outside of the party's 
comfort zone. 
› The Goal can be fulﬁlled at another location and taking 
the most direct path there comes with a cost, a risk or an 
obstacle. For example:
»
The cost could be as simple as spending a Lifestyle 
point worth of coin.
»
The risk could be getting lost and wasting time or 
bumping into a gang of thugs.
»
The obstacles could be walls to climb over.
› Someone else is after the same goal. For example to 
steal, buy, inherit or destroy the thing that the PC is after, 
or to convince, seduce, exile or kill the person that the PC 
is after. 
› This rivalry can play out in steps over several encounters 
and create the much needed time pressure for attaining 
the PC's Goal.
› The rival could be a group of adventurers similar to the 
PCs who are ﬁghting for some long term cause and 
follow some principles regarding what they are willing to 
do to achieve this cause. The cause or the principles 
could be in conﬂict with the party to challenge the party's 
morals or it could be in sync with the party to create 
friendly rivalry and give the PCs an opportunity to make 
friends or allies by compromising on their Goals and 
possibly losing a chance to gain XP. 
› A distraction unrelated to any PC's Goal - Use this 
obstacle once in a while to remind the players that it's a 
living world with accidents, chance encounters and other 
people who also want something. See examples in the 
next chapter.

Example distractions
› An NPC bumps into a PC on the street and either 
attempts to pickpocket them or only acts as a distraction 
while another NPC tries to pickpocket the same or 
another PC. 
› While eating in a tavern or other dining & alcohol 
establishment, a drunken ﬁght breaks out between NPCs. 
If the PCs don't intervene, one of them gets splashed 
with spilled beer and another has to dodge the mug that 
contained it (or a stool, etc). 
› If any of the PCs has fought well in public in this city, an 
NPC approaches them and challenges them to a duel, 
claiming to be a master in the same skill that the PC used 
to ﬁght, be it a Mystic Element, Short weapons, Long 
weapons, Ranged weapons, Thrown weapons or 
Brawling.
› If the party has rid the city of some dangerous threat, a 
teenager approaches the party, asking to join the party, 
showing oﬀ a skill they've been practicing and promising 
to learn fast and not become a burden. 
› An individual takes interest in one of the PCs who hasn't 
done as much talking as the others (to engage the more 
passive player), asking them on a date. 
› A persistent salesman invites the party to their stall, 
promising great discounts and upselling the quality of 
their wares to actually get an above average price. 
Shopping there will cost the party a Lifestyle point unless 
they have the Expensive Lifestyle or are successful in 
haggling with the salesman. 
› A drug dealer signals one of the PCs to follow them into 
an alley. If approached by the whole party, they'll play it 
as a misunderstanding, but if approached by only one PC, 
they'll suggest that they got the best quality stuﬀ in town 
and oﬀer a discount (no Lifestyle point cost for the ﬁrst 
deal, but the stuﬀ is addictive).
› The party witnesses a chase on the streets where the 
pursuers are armed, but do not bear any oﬀicial livery of 
the city guard or militia. They could be goons of a crime 
lord from the entertainment district chasing a rival street 
gang, an escaped slave or a broke businessman who 
owes them money on high interest. 
› A relative or an acquaintance mentioned in a party 
member's Motives comes looking for them - they need 
help. 
Adding suspense
To add suspense to an encounter, add a countdown: 
1 Deﬁne a consequence that the party would likely want to 
prevent. For example:
»
The captives are sacriﬁced for the ritual.
»
The ship crashes into an obstacle on its course.
»
The gates are broken by the ram.
»
Opponent's reinforcements arrive.
»
The alarm bell is rung.
»
The poison takes eﬀect.
»
The ﬁre spreads.
2 Decide on what would trigger a countdown until this 
consequence happens. A typical trigger is the start of 
combat, but it could also be the release of a mechanism 
or a trap or an order from the leader of opponents. 
3 Think of the actions that the party could take to prevent 
or mitigate the consequence and stop the countdown. 
For example:
»
Defeat the ones performing the ritual or rescue the 
hostages held as sacriﬁces.
»
Stop the vehicle or change its course.
»
Destroy the ram or the ones using it, or retreat 
behind the inner wall before gates are breached.
»
Complete your objective and retreat before 
reinforcements arrive or defeat the current 
opponents and prepare an ambush for the 
reinforcements. 
»
Complete your objective and escape before the 
alarm is rung, knock out the guards on their way to 
the bell or even steal the bell clapper. 
»
Retrieve and drink the antidote or complete your 
objective before the poison paralyzes you. 
»
Extinguish the ﬁre or evacuate everyone before the 
ﬁre spreads. 
4
Decide on a number of rounds since the triggering event 
for the consequence to happen. 
»
1 to 3 rounds create enough suspense for capturing 
or knocking out guards. You could roll 1d4 instead to 
determine the number of rounds.
»
3 to 5 rounds (or 1d4+2) create enough suspense for 
defeating challenging opponents in combat. 
»
More than 5 rounds (or 1d4+4) are needed for a 
complex objective like ﬁnding something in a room, 
succeeding on an Extended Test and escaping 
before the countdown ends. 
5
Decide if the consequence repeats until it is stopped.
To play out a countdown:
1 If the countdown is triggered, switch to playing out the 
encounter in combat mode with rounds and turns until 
the consequence happens or is prevented. 
2 Set a countdown die to the number of rounds remaining. 
3 At the end of each round turn the countdown to the next 
smaller number. If it is already at "1", the consequence 
happens. If the consequence is repeatable, then reset the 
countdown and continue.

Using traps
A hidden trap is not fun for players - it's an invisible source 
of Wounds that doesn't challenge players and instead 
causes them to play overly cautiously, slowing down the 
game. 
A better trap is one that lures the party in, conﬁnes them 
and springs danger on them, like:
› An ambush, if the party has leaked their plans of going 
somewhere or has been spotted resting nearby.
› A big and visible trap that occupies the only way forward 
and, instead of blocking the way it harms anyone moving 
through it. For example a wide pit with spikes or snakes, 
a tunnel with boiling water geysers or steam vents or lots 
of blades moved by mechanical moving parts that can be 
disarmed or turned oﬀ using a hidden switch or a 
combination of visible switches. On attempts to disarm it 
describe how the mechanism looks from the outside, 
encourage the players to tinker with it and only ask for a 
check if they have a chance to fail at some step of 
disarming the trap.
› A trap room that imprisons the party and creates time 
pressure to get out by ﬁlling with water, mud, sand, 
spears, heat, cold, etc. The party could then try to: 
»
Pick the lock of the door that trapped them.
»
Clog some holes or hinder movement of a moving 
wall.
»
Create ice or obstacles to stop the trap for a time - 
long enough to try picking the lock one more time.
»
Create a pocket of safety in the room.
»
Create a new exit.
»
Something crazy - players will usually surprise you 
with something crazy. 
Using puzzles
Asking players to solve a puzzle requires player knowledge 
instead of character knowledge, but you don't know what 
players know or remember, so puzzles turn out to be either 
trivial or take way more time than you expect. Both 
scenarios are not much fun. 
One solution is to not use puzzles at all. Another is to turn 
the puzzle into one or more visible traps - one trap for every 
correct answer that has to be presented. If the right answer 
is presented to a trap, the trap disarms itself in a clear and 
visible way. If a wrong answer is presented, the trap is 
triggered, potentially Wounding the one presenting the 
answer and then again disarming itself in a clear and visible 
way. These traps can be disarmed like any other. The most 
important thing here is that both the right and a wrong 
answer allows the party to proceed past the trap. 
Example trap‐puzzle
As a security measure of the royal treasury, its antechamber 
is trapped. The trap can be deactivated at the entrance. 
The entrance is a locked door. Its archway is visibly set with 
three gems - a sapphire, a diamond and a topaz. Each gem 
will activate and glow for an hour if Imbued with an Ability 
of the correct Element. Water for the sapphire, Wind for the 
diamond and Lightning for the topaz. As long as all three 
gems are activated and remain in place in the archway, the 
trap in the antechamber will not activate. 
If a gem is Imbued with an Ability of a wrong Element, the 
Ability is reﬂected back at its user. The user can try to resist 
their own Ability as if someone else used it against them. 
The entrance leads into the antechamber, which is a 6 by 6 
spaces square room with no windows and one locked door 
in the opposite wall. In each corner stands an imposing 
statue of a gargoyle holding a large gem in its toothy maw - 
one holds a topaz, another diamond and the remaining two 
hold sapphires. Each gem imprisons a level 6 elemental 
(see The Ritual of Elemental Imprisonment, p. 72). 
If the entrance door is opened without deactivating the trap, 
then after thirty seconds the trap activates - for the next 
hour, every 6 seconds (or each combat round) the gem in 
each gargoyle's mouth ﬁlls the whole chamber with a Cone: 
1 Spray Cone from one of the sapphires to soak everyone 
in the room. 
2 Shock Cone from the topaz to cause a Wound for soaked 
characters. 
3 Freeze Cone from the other sapphire to freeze over the 
wet ﬂoor and cause a level of Fatigue for soaked 
characters. 
4
Blast Cone from the diamond to cause a Wound by 
blasting characters into the walls. Attempts to resist it 
have a Disadvantage due to the now slippery ﬂoor.
So, if the party triggers the trap, they face the choice of 
retreating, destroying the trap or trying to open the locked 
door while under ﬁre from the gargoyles.

An overwhelmingly large amount of adventures published 
for roleplaying games already exists out there for you to be 
inspired by in creating your own. But in order to run a 
published adventure you ﬁrst need to read the whole 
adventure and learn its locations, NPCs, factions and 
conﬂicts to make them your own and decide how you will 
play out the encounters described there. This usually takes 
more time and eﬀort than simply preparing your own 
encounters. 
If you're not a professional writer, then your encounters 
won't be as stylishly written as in the published adventures, 
but for new GMs, less time spent on preparation is 
generally more important than more stylish descriptions 
and those won't be presented to your players anyway 
unless you read them aloud during the game. And reading 
aloud for more than one sentence usually is a good way to 
lose the attention of your players. 
Many published adventures are about killing monsters and 
taking their stuﬀ, in which case they're lacking negotiable 
opponents with motivations and a variety of weapons. So 
when you reuse their encounters, it's worth substituting 
such opponents for intelligent ones and of all the monsters 
only keep unique specimens and make them actually 
monstrous, unknown and scary to the players.

(Not) using random encounter tables
Random encounter tables don't mesh well with 
Fatebenders. They are usually full of monsters whose sole 
purpose is to ﬁght, so they will tilt your session towards 
resolving conﬂicts with combat, which also take longer than 
encounters resolved without combat. 
Secondly, they have no connection to PC Goals, so they can 
feel like ﬁller episodes that the players have to grind 
through to get to the prepared encounters that matter to 
them. Random encounters also tend to feel more repetitive 
due to monsters not utilizing weapon mechanics and not 
having any distinguishing motives, weaknesses or assets. 
 
Adapting magic from published settings
You can adapt ideas from low-magic fantasy or sword and 
sorcery books to your Fatebenders game as long as you 
manage to translate their spells, curses, magical item 
eﬀects, racial and bloodline abilities or substance abuse 
powers into new Abilities (p. 52) and deﬁne whether they 
can be learned and Attuned to or not. 
In fact, as long as a magical eﬀect can only be produced by 
a unique item or individual and can't be learned or acquired 
by others, it's ﬁne if it ignores a few rules of Elemental 
Abilities. It's up to you to explain why this one ring, talking 
hat, egg-shaped amulet or notebook with names of dead 
people in it is special. If you're going for a more mysterious 
feel, you don't even need to know who made it or how it 
works. Just consider what will happen if the players get 
their hands on it. How would it change their approach to 
combat, exploration and negotiation? 
Many great and personal stories can be told in worlds 
where some magic is so mysterious and rare that it's not 
used regularly as a tool, not studied as a science, not 
understood nor maybe even believed to exist by the public. 
You can introduce such magic to your game not as a new 
Ability nor a bendable Element, but as a unique magical 
creature, event, location or item and use it as a lure for the 
party, to create mystery and to make the adventure unique. 
Adapting items from published settings
To adjust Fatebenders to the level of technology of some 
published setting that you like, go over the Items chapter 
(p. 30) and rename, add and remove items as you see ﬁt or 
substitute them with homebrew items or items converted 
from another game. 
To convert items: 
› Deﬁne what price ranges in the source game correspond 
to Cheap, Aﬀordable and Expensive in your world. Use 
cheaper ranges for consumables and services than for 
items, as they're bought more often. 
› Deﬁne melee weapons as Short if they are wielded with 
one hand and Long if they are wielded with two hands. 
› If you add a new Ranged weapon type, make sure you 
add a Disadvantage or some other limiter to its Attacks 
to make up for its superior range compared to Thrown 
weapons. 
In case your group wants to play with simpler combat rules, 
you can agree to ignore all weapon properties, considering 
only each weapon's type and reach. But then restrict all 
Ranged weapons to only 1 Attack per turn for balance.

Follow below steps to create a new species or a unique 
creature or to convert any creature from any TTRPG

creature can only use this Ability once per that many 
rounds or has that many less allies. 
› If you want a large creature to pose a threat to the party 
on its own, then you can make its body parts occupy 
multiple spaces in any conﬁguration and give it more 
than 2 Combat actions per turn. Assign the creature's 
ability to see, hear and to use each of its Combat actions 
and abilities to a diﬀerent body part. 
»
Assign one or two Heart points to each body part so 
that when these Hearts are wounded, the body part 
is mangled and the creature becomes blinded, 
deafened or can't use the extra Combat action or 
ability associated with this body part. 
»
If the body part remains attached to the main body, 
it will continue to occupy the space as an Obstacle 
moving along with the main body, but attacks at it 
won't inﬂict Wounds on the main body. Tell your 
players about it, as it is also apparent to their 
characters. 
»
Give the body parts dangerous abilities, so players 
will prioritize them and turn it into a ﬁght with 
multiple phases. 
Any special abilities, immunities, resistances and 
vulnerabilities that you ascribe to the creatures you create 
will work as exceptions to the rules. Otherwise all game 
rules that refer to characters also apply to all creatures. 
Creature behaviour
Pick the behaviour patterns that suit your creature: 
› Preying creatures are often good at Spotting prey (p. 23), 
Concealing themselves (p. 23), sneaking and preparing 
an ambush (p. 22) or a trap (p. 24) like a spider web.
› Creatures with strong arms or tentacles tend to Hurl or 
Grapple (p. 26), then lift and drop or pull into water. 
› Aggressive opponents tend to Intimidate (p. 26) or, if 
intelligent or dumb enough, Taunt (p. 26) the party or 
wreck the surrounding structures in a ﬂashy way. 
› Certain scaly creatures can breathe ﬁre, acid, cold, 
lightning or poison, being immune to it themselves. 
For that use the Burn (p. 37), Freeze (p. 38), Shock (p. 39) 
or Poison (p. 41) Ability in Cone Form. 
› Quadrupeds are stable, giving Disadvantage to attempts 
to Shove or Hurl them (p. 26). If they have horns, they 
tend to ram and if they're carnivorous, they tend to

knocks its target prone if it does a Move action and then 
a successful Shove. 
› Acid can also work as the Burn Ability, except aﬀected 
characters are covered in acid instead of being set on 
ﬁre. If by the end of the aﬀected character's next turn it's 
not washed oﬀ, it deals 1 Wound or removes 1 Armour

remaining point, then the armour is destroyed. 
› Very large creatures could destroy structures or 
otherwise interact with the combat map. 
› Some marine creatures can Shock you on Hit while being

› Biting and stinging creatures can be venomous,

› Some creatures are able to regenerate - heal 1 Wound at

› Sneaky opponents can steal your equipment and, if 
caught, use it against you or use dirty tactics, like

hinder your movement before escaping. 
› Lonesome opponents who have much to lose tend to 
ﬂee on sight, which is enough reason for most parties to 
give chase.
› Leaders can Command (p. 25) and Inspire (p. 26) their 
subordinates. 
› An intelligent opponent:

Blood rituals (optional rules)
Blood rituals are secret rituals which grant beneﬁts 
unobtainable by other means, but involve hours of 
Continuous use of Abilities of diﬀerent Elements and 
require sacriﬁces. Blood is usually not enough - someone 
has to lose their Attunement, grow much older during the 
course of the ritual or even die. Most societies actively shun 
and persecute practitioners of blood rituals, but the desires 
of individuals have kept these rituals from fading into 
oblivion. The long term subjective beneﬁt of blood rituals 
and their high cost creates an immoral motivation and gives 
NPCs a believable and relatable cause for dramatic conﬂict 
with the player characters. 
All blood rituals have lasting eﬀects, unknown principles, 
steep cost of experimentation and an air of secrecy, so 
every time a new practitioner learns or steals a ritual, they 
are unable to distinguish the required actions from the 
habits and aesthetic choices of their master and copy both 
with equal fervour. As a result every blood ritual comes with 
a list of superstitious rules about location, timing, 
appearances and sacriﬁcing which diﬀer from one 
practitioner to another. 
Every blood ritual has a minimal duration, a list of 
associated Elements that need to be controlled by Mystics 
to perform the ritual, some sacriﬁced characters and 
possibly some beneﬁtting characters. 
› For the whole duration of any blood ritual all the 
sacriﬁced and beneﬁtting characters have to stay in reach 
of the Forms of Continuously used Abilities - one Ability 
for each Element listed in the ritual's description. These 
Abilities can be used in every Form and they have no 
eﬀect of their own, but they still follow all the rules of 
using Abilities with the exception that characters can't 
resist them. 
This rule ensures that there's a Mystic Attuned to each 
required element participating in the ritual and makes sure 
that these Mystics are skilled and determined enough to 
complete a ritual of the scale and duration that they set out 
to do despite all the Fatigue they gain in the process. 
As there are 600 combat turns in an hour and Continuous 
use of an Ability requires to spend an action per turn to use 
that Ability, even the most skilled Mystics would die of 
Fatigue from Critical fails of Ability checks alone before they 
could ﬁnish even an hour-long ritual. But if they meditate on 
an Ability one minute per hour during the ritual, they can 
reuse one Ability check for an hour. If that Ability check fails, 
they get a Fatigue level, but they can redo this check every 6 
seconds until they fall unconscious or get a success and 
start reusing the successful result. 
This forces ritual practitioners to ﬁnd and train apprentices, 
choose the smallest Ability Form that still covers all the 
targets and the shortest ritual duration that they can get 
away with while still attaining their greedy goal. 
Feel free to come up with your own rituals that ﬁt your 
setting.
