Story Mechanics: Incremental Antimagic
Interjection Games
We've all been there. Somewhere in the middle of a
campaign setting is that blasted wasteland, the remnants
of an elder race, or the prison of some long­forgotten god.
Around this place, spellcasters mewl in frustration as spell
after spell simply fails to work for some reason that likely
isn't sufficiently explained. What makes these sorts of
mechanics particularly frustrating is that they typically
take no effort to distinguish between a 1st level caster and
a 20th level caster. While this can very well be perfectly
okay for the prison of a long­forgotten god, the residual
antimagic of the equivalent of an arcane nuclear bomb
really has no place shutting down Archibald, master
wizard extraordinaire, but poor stuttering Avery over
here in the corner rather deserves it.
Incremental antimagic is a combination of three pieces:
the difficulty, the consequences, and the radius. This
system can be applied to locations, creatures, inanimate
objects... Really, it can be applied to anything that can
have a radius drawn around it.
Part 1: The Difficulty
Not all attempts to shut down magic are created equal. As
such, incremental antimagic supports a simple tiering
system. Simply select a difficulty tier. In order to
successfully overcome a form of incremental antimagic, a
DC (Selected tier + spell level) Concentration check is
required.
Table: Incremental Antimagic Difficulty
Part 2: The Consequences
Again, not all attempts to shut down magic are created
equal. Some simply destroy the weaves of magic, making
them dissipate like a dream in the waking world. Others
delay magic, pressing against the mind of the caster until
the caster is forced to subject himself to the very
devastation he was trying to throw upon others. Still
others hinder magic, requiring the caster to buffer his
spell with more of his magical ability in order to get it to
function. Add one of the following styles of incremental
antimagic to your selected difficulty.
Chaotic ­ This style of antimagic causes strange things to
happen should the caster fail his concentration check.
Rather than cast the spell the spellcaster intended to cast,
he instead casts a random spell among spells he knows of
that same spell level. If the original spell was a single
target and the new spell is an area of effect, the area of
effect is centered upon the original single target. If the
original spell was an area of effect and the new spell is a
single target, the target of the spell is the center of the
original area of effect. Spells with a range of Personal can
affect other creatures in this manner. Should the range of
the new spell be incapable of reaching the original target,
the spell fires off in the direction of the original target
regardless of this fact.
This system is thematically intended to simulate the
effects of fairy circles and other areas of fae influence. An
interesting side effect of this system is that casters who
only know a single spell of a particular spell level, such as
a sorcerer who just broke into a new caster level, are
essentially immune to this effect, thus allowing them to
shotgun a limited selection of spells at will. Wizards, on
the
other
hand,
sob
into
their
spellbooks.
Delaying ­ This style of antimagic causes a spell to
backfire
into
the
mind
of
the
caster
on
a
failed
concentration check. On the caster's next turn, he must
choose one of two courses of action. First, he may spend a
standard action to attempt to cast that spell again using
the same spell slot as before (A new spell is NOT
expended to attempt this. The old spell is still active.). If
his concentration check is successful, the spell is cast,
albeit with a duration shortened by how long it was
delayed by the antimagic. Secondly, he may simply let the
spell go as a swift action. Whatever the spell effect is, it is
now centered on the caster and ONLY affects the caster if
the spell usually has the ability to choose who is affected.
Compulsory targeting, such as the fireball spell, remains
in effect.
This
system
is
thematically
intended
for
use
by
dominators, knowledge deities, and other individuals who
would rather see a creature squirm than simply be
rendered impotent. An interesting side effect of this
system
is
it
really
doesn't
harm
those
who
buff
themselves, thus allowing a combat cleric and others of
that general archetype to overcome this form of antimagic
quite easily.
Eliminating ­ This style of antimagic actually means
antimagic. Should a spellcaster fail his concentration
check against eliminating antimagic, the spell simply fails
to cast. Period.
This system is intended to behave like every other
antimagic that has been seen in the past and doesn't really
have any intent other than continuity.

Hindering ­ This style of antimagic gives the caster a
choice should he fail his concentration check. The caster
may either expend a spell slot of one level lower (or
higher) than the spell whose concentration check failed,
thus casting the spell even though the check was failed, or
allow the spell to fail.
This system is thematically intended to make low level
spells feel safe and stable, as they carry with them a lower
cost of failure than one would experience with a higher
level spell. An interesting side effect of this system is that
1st level spells simply shrug and burn a limitless cantrip
should the concentration check fail. This allows hindering
antimagic to be used as a plot device early in a campaign
without crushing the spellcasters in a party.
Part 3: The Radius
For creatures, select a radius for your incremental
antimagic in increments of 30 feet. This radius should
never be more than 30 feet for every 5 CR the creature
possesses.
For locations, go wild! Perhaps a faint antimagic field is
present miles out, but slowly builds to overwhelming
force as the party approaches. Just remember to use the
current
field
strength
for
calculation
of
CR
below.
Calculation of CR
There are two means by which incremental antimagic can
be applied to combat that takes place with it in play. First,
the incremental antimagic can be part of the terrain itself,
thus adding to the CR of one creature in the encounter to
properly reward the party for defeating the encounter
with the antimagic in tow. Second, the incremental
antimagic can simply be part of a creature and be
calculated in its own CR.
Consult the following table to determine whether or not to
add to the CR of a creature for having a form of
incremental antimagic in tow. For excessive cases, add +2
at the creator's discretion.
