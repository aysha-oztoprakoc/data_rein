W
elcome to the world of Oath 
Hammer! You and your allies 
have been charged with restoring 
Osric Isle to the dwarven people 
who once called it home. Courage 
and steel will be necessary for this 
journey, as the evil forces that have 
conquered this land will not part with 
it easily.
Tone
Characters in Oath Hammer are 
heroes who embody virtue. These 
adventurers are expected to oppose 
evil or suffer the dire consequences 
of dereliction. Heroes are more than 
forces of destruction. While delivering 
ruin to the wicked, characters rebuild 
corrupted lands; fallen cities are 
restored, and new shining beacons 
of civilization founded. 
Roleplaying
Oath Hammer is a roleplaying 
game directed by a Game Master 
(GM). The other players take on 
the roles of heroic characters. Each 
character is driven to adventure by 
noble convictions rather than petty 
ambitions.
The GM creates a world and fills it 
with people, factions, and nations. 
Players interact with this world, 
their decisions often changing it in 
meaningful ways. While the players 
attempt to roleplay their characters, 
the GM roleplays as any non-player 
character (NPC) the party contacts, 
such as a magistrate or shopkeeper. 
The GM is also responsible for 
interpreting the rules and calling for 
dice rolls when determining certain 
outcomes.

Dice Pool
Sometimes it is not obvious what 
the outcome of an action will be. 
In such cases, a dice pool is rolled, 
which involves multiple dice being 
thrown at once. The number of dice 
rolled is determined by a character’s 
attributes, equipment, and skills. If 
enough successes are rolled on the 
dice, the action succeeds.
Oath Hammer dice are always six-
sided (d6). Some rolls may require a 
d3, but in such situations, a d6 is still 
used. When rolling a d3, divide the 
d6’s result in half and round up (1-2: 
1, 3-4: 2, 5-6: 3).
D66
When rolling a D66, the player rolls 
two dice. The first roll represents 
the “tens,” and the second roll is 
the “units.”
Example: The first roll has a result 
of 2, and the second roll has a result 
of 4. The value of the roll is thus 24.
Gaming Aids
Players will need to keep track of 
their character’s attributes, abilities, 
and equipment; this is most easily 
done with a character sheet. 
Miniatures, dry-erase boards, and 
other visual aids can also be used 
to convey movement and position. 
Many rules were designed with a 
grid in mind, though miniatures can 
be moved 1 inch for every tile they 
would ordinarily travel when a grid 
is not available. 
Setting
Osric Isle is a land within the world 
of Mornholm, once controlled by a 
collection of dwarven clans. While 
elves jealously guarded the island’s 
darkest forests and firbolgs claimed 
the swamplands, all other challenges 
to dwarven authority were crushed. 
Overconfident from years of peace, 
the dwarves were unprepared to 
resist a looming northern threat.
Black-sailed longships delivered 
orcish reavers to the island; their 
great numbers overwhelmed any 
force that stood against them. Aided 
by monstrous beasts, the orcs soon 
conquered much of the land. Mighty 
holds, once believed impregnable, 
fell to dark magic and attrition.
The few who survived the invasion 
fled to the continent. The humans 
of Nockland welcomed the exiled 
dwarves, who found work on the 
continent as masons, mercenaries, 
and smiths. Never truly finding 
peace, the dwarves prepared for a 
day when they would reclaim Osric 
Isle from their hated enemies.  
It has been nearly a century since 
the orcs invaded Osric Isle. While 
orcish raids along the coast had been 
a common occurrence, black sails 
have not been sighted in Nockland 
for over a year. Many speak of some 
ill fate that has befallen the orcs, 
and several expeditions have been 
launched to investigate. Dwarven 
warriors, eager for vengeance, pour 
onto the isle in growing numbers.

Getting Started
Players will need to form a company 
before beginning the campaign; this 
involves selecting a name, coat of 
arms, and colors. Creating a company 
will establish early on that the 
characters are aligned as one and 
that their party is meant to confront 
the world collectively.
Starting Location
Most campaigns will begin with the 
company preparing for their trip to 
Osric Isle. It is suggested that the 
characters begin in Whitehill, the 
capital city of Nockland, and the 
nearest port to Osric Isle. Some 
campaigns may begin on Osric Isle, 
the company having already landed 
on the isle or having never left.
Expedition
Assuming the characters sail for 
Osric Isle, they will have to plan their 
expedition. The characters will need 
to secure funding and transportation.
Patrons
There are figures and factions with 
an interest in subduing Osric Isle: 
Dwarven thegns eagerly await the 
opportunity to reclaim their stolen 
lands. The King of Nockland fears 
reavers returning to his land and 
would gladly support any adventuring 
company willing to attack the orcs. 
Agents of the Velathi Empire skulk 
in Whitehill, promising coin to 
any who will deliver them magic 
items. Patrons are unlikely to offer 
support freely and will often expect 
something in return for their aid. 
Saltgate
The King of Nockland has established 
a stronghold on Osric Isle known as 
Saltgate. This fortified colony was 
hastily constructed and receives 
new visitors daily. Saltgate is the 
base of operations for adventuring 
companies on Osric Isle, a secure 
location to rest and resupply. While 
characters will probably rely on 
Saltgate early on as a safe haven, 
they will struggle to find adventure 
if they linger too long.
Ships regularly leave Nockland for 
Saltgate, and the party will have no 
difficulty finding a vessel willing to 
transport them. These warships 
have been directed to secure the 
waters north of Nockland and 
deliver armed adventurers to 
the island. Adventurers will not 
be able to return to Nockland 
via these ships, as they have been 
ordered to deliver warriors to the 
island, not bring them back.

haracters in Oath Hammer 
are daring heroes who live for 
adventure. These characters may 
be chivalrous knights, hardened 
soldiers, or wizened wizards.
Players have a variety of options 
when creating their characters. After 
choosing a lineage, the player should 
name their character and assign 
ranks to their six attributes. Finally, 
the player chooses three oaths and 
a class for their character. The order 
of character creation isn't important, 
so long as each one of these steps is 
completed.

•  Determine name.
•  Assign attribute ranks.
•  Declare three oaths. 
•  Select a class. 
Attributes
A character’s physical and mental 
abilities are measured through their 
attributes. Each attribute ranges from 
rank 1 (poor) to rank 4 (excellent). 
Characters begin with one rank 4 
attribute, two rank 3 attributes, two 
rank 2 attributes, and one rank 1 
attribute. Players determine how they 
want to assign these ranks. 
Example: Roland is an intelligent 
character who easily grasps academic 
concepts. Roland’s impetuous nature 
often results in fights that test his 
impressive brawn and resilience. 
These fights are often won because 
Roland is fairly dexterous and lucky.
To represent this, Roland assigns 4 
ranks to Intelligence, 3 ranks to Might 
and Toughness, 2 ranks to Agility and 
Fate, and 1 rank to Willpower.

Characters
Characters


Might (M)
This attribute measures a character’s 
brawn and physical prowess. Might 
determines a character’s likelihood 
of accomplishing feats of strength 
and ability to inflict damage with 
melee weapons. 
Toughness (T)
Toughness represents a character’s 
physical endurance and durability. 
Toughness determines how much 
damage a character can sustain 
before succumbing to pain.
Agility (A)
This attribute governs a character’s 
reflexes, balance, and the precision 
of their handwork. Agile characters 
move more gracefully and can avoid 
dangerous attacks.
Willpower (WP)
This attribute is a measurement of 
a character’s wisdom, focus, and 
force of personality. Willpower also 
determines mental fortitude and the 
ability to perform under pressure.
Intelligence (I)
This attribute represents a character’s 
ability to accumulate knowledge and 
perform cerebral acts. 
Fate (F)
A character's good luck is reflected by 
this attribute.  Heroes often rely on 
good fortune to survive impossible 
odds and perform incredible feats.
Grit Points
Grit Points (GP) measure how much 
physical punishment a combatant can 
endure. A character’s total Grit Points 
are determined by adding their ranks 
in Resilience and Toughness.
Luck Points
Characters possess a number of 
Luck Points equal to their ranks in 
Fate. Spending a Luck Point allows 
the character to gain a +2 bonus to 
any roll. Characters may choose to 
spend a Luck Point after making a 
roll and may use multiple Luck Points 
on a single roll. Spent Luck Points are 
lost for the remainder of the gaming 
session. Luck Points are fully restored 
at the beginning of the next session.
Lineages
At character creation, players choose 
from one of the following lineages: 
dwarf, firbolg, halfling, high elf, 
human, or wood elf.
Bonus attribute ranks granted by 
lineages can allow attributes to reach 
rank 5.

Oaths
Characters declare three oaths that 
guide their conduct while pursuing 
adventure. These may be formal 
pledges sworn in public or secret 
vows that hold deep meaning for 
the character. 
Boons & Banes
If a character adheres to the tenets 
of their oath, they benefit from the 
oath’s boon. Should a character 
violate their oath, they lose the oath’s 
boon and incur its bane instead.
Redemption
Characters who violate an oath 
will have to perform a heroic deed 
or act of self-sacrifice to redeem 
themselves. Redeemed characters 
no longer suffer from the oath’s bane 
and have the boon restored.
Oath of Courage
While you are not expected to act 
impetuously, you must oppose evil, 
thwarting the wicked when possible. 
You can carefully choose your fights 
but cannot surrender to evil foes. 
Boon

checks and cannot be Frightened.
Bane
You incur a -2 penalty to Discipline 
checks. You also incur a -1 penalty to 
Leadership checks.
Oath of Diligence
When tasked with a responsibility, 
you must strive to complete it as 
quickly and efficiently as possible. 
You should embrace hard work and 
industry while rejecting sloth.

knight, mage, priest, scout, soldier,

outcome. Proficiency in a skill is 
measured in ranks and governed by 
an attribute. When resolving a skill 
check, the character rolls a number

Dice Color
White dice are standard and succeed

be negatively influenced so that red

Dice explode when they result in 
a 6, allowing the player to roll an 
additional die and add the result. 
If the player rolls a second 6, they 
roll again and add the result to the 
total, continuing to do so until they 
fail to roll another 6. Exploding dice 
allow characters to perform heroic 
acts and keep combat dangerous. 
All dice explode.
Example: Roland rolls four dice 
with results of 1, 3, 5, and 6. Roland 
has two successes and rolls an 
additional die because he rolled a 
6. This additional die roll result is a 
6, adding a third success to the skill 
check and granting a further die roll. 
Roland rolls again with a result of 5, 
granting a fourth success. Because 
Roland did not roll another 6, he will 
not roll again.

Standard Checks
Standard checks involve a Difficulty 
Value (DV), which is the number of 
successes necessary to pass the skill 
check. A character attempting a DV1 
skill check passes if they roll a single 
success or more. A skill check with 
a Difficulty Value of 5 (DV5) would 
be significantly more difficult and 
require 5 successes to pass. 
Example: Roland attempts a DV2 
skill check and rolls three successes. 
Because Roland rolled more than the 
two required successes, he passes 
the skill check.
Opposed Checks
These checks involve opposing dice 
rolls, and the character with the 
greatest number of successes is the 
winner. The net successes rolled for 
an opposed check is known as the 
Success Value (SV). 
Example: Roland and Zacharias 
participate in an arm wrestling 
contest. Both characters perform 
an Athletics check. Roland rolls four 
successes while Zacharias rolls one. 
Therefore, Roland wins the contest 
with a Success Value of 3 (SV3). 
Tied Contests
Opposed checks that end in a draw 
have no clear resolution and neither 
party gains an advantage over the 
other. The opposed skill check can be 
attempted again on subsequent turns 
until a clear winner is determined.
Dice Modifiers
When a character receives a bonus to 
a roll, they roll an additional number 
of dice indicated by the bonus. For 
example, a +2 bonus would grant 
the character two additional dice. 
Penalties reduce the number of dice 
the character may roll. A penalty can 
never reduce the number of dice 
rolled below 1.

Attack and Defense Rolls
Combatants can target and attack 
one enemy on each of their turns. 
Bows and thrown weapons have 
a range, measured in feet, which 
dictates how close the attacker 
must be to their target to perform an 
attack. The range of a melee weapon 
is referred to as reach. 
When an attack is performed, the 
attacker’s Fighting or Shooting skill is 
opposed by the defender’s Defense 
skill.  If the attacker wins the opposed 
roll, they hit the defender; otherwise, 
the attack fails. 
The Fighting or Shooting check is 
referred to as the attack roll, while 
the Defense check is referred to as a 
defense roll. The dice used for these 
rolls may sometimes be referred to 
as attack dice or defense dice.
Diminishing Defense
Combatants attacked by multiple 
enemies during the same turn incur 
a penalty to their defense rolls. Each 
defense roll performed after the 
first incurs a cumulative -2 penalty. 
Diminishing Defense allows weaker 
combatants to overwhelm more 
competent foes.
Example: Zacharias is attacked by 
three goblins. The first defense roll 
Zacharias performs during the turn 
does not incur a penalty. However, 
the second defense roll would incur a 
-2 penalty, and the third defense roll 
would incur a -4 penalty. Zacharias 
no longer incurs a penalty to defense 
rolls on the next round of combat.
Damage  Dice (DD)
If an attack hits, the attacker 
makes a damage roll. The base 
number of damage dice (DD) rolled 
is determined by the attacker's 
weapon. The damage roll also 
receives a bonus equal to the attack's 
Success Value, reflecting the lethality 
of more accurate attacks. For every 
success rolled on a damage die, the 
target loses 1 Grit Point.
Nonweapon Damage
Some forms of damage deal a fixed 
number of damage dice. In such 
cases, the number of damage dice 
rolled will be indicated.
Example: If an attack deals 3DD, 3 
damage dice are rolled.
Armor
If the defender is wearing armor, 
they may roll a number of armor dice 
equal to their Armor Value. Every 
success rolled in this manner reduces 
the damage received by 1. This roll 
to reduce damage is referred to as 
the armor roll.
Armor Modifiers
Unlike other rolls, negative modifiers 
can reduce an armor roll below 1 
die, effectively bypassing the armor. 
Some attacks may ignore armor and 
prevent the target from making an 
armor roll. Various forms of armor 
reduction stack with one another.
Example: Roland ordinarily rolls 5 
armor dice. Because he is hit with an 
AP1 weapon, he rolls 4 dice instead.

Combat Example
Roland attacks a goblin. Roland rolls 
3 successes on his attack roll. The 
goblin rolls 1 success on its defense 
roll, resulting in an overall Success 
Value of 2 (SV2). Roland hits the 
goblin with his attack.
Roland is wielding a longsword that 
deals Might + 2 damage. Because 
Roland has 2 ranks in Might, his base 
damage is 4. The total number of 
damage dice rolled is therefore 6 
(base damage 4 + SV2). Roland rolls 
three successes for damage.
The goblin ordinarily rolls 3 armor 
dice, but Roland's longsword is AP1 
and thus imposes a -1 penalty to 
the goblin's armor roll. The goblin 
rolls a single armor roll success and 
reduces the damage it receives by 
1. The goblin loses 2 Grit Points as a 
result of Roland’s attack.
Actions
Combatants have a variety of options 
during their turn but are limited to 
one action. Combatants may also use 
a single auxiliary action on each of 
their turns in addition to their action. 
Attack
As an action, a combatant may 
perform a melee attack against a 
target within reach or a ranged attack 
against a target within range.
Cast Magic
As an action, a combatant may 
perform a miracle or cast a spell. 
Combatants must be able to see 
the target of their miracle or spell. 
Complete rules for magic can be 
found in the Magic Chapter.
Disarm
A combatant may attempt to disarm 
an enemy by requiring them to 
make an opposed Fighting check. If 
the attacker wins, they force their 
target to drop one weapon that lands 
somewhere within 5 ft. Combatants 
armed with two-handed weapons 
gain a +1 bonus to their opposed 
Fighting check.
Hold Action
A combatant may forgo acting during 
their turn so they may perform an 
action later. The combatant must 
declare what action they intend to 
perform and the trigger that initiates 
it. If the necessary trigger does not 
35
35


occur before their next turn, the 
combatant loses their action. If 
a combatant uses Hold Action to 
prepare a melee attack, their attack 
occurs simultaneously with any 
attack their target performs.
Example: Roland has a crossbow 
trained on a door. He announces that 
he intends to fire his crossbow when 
the door is opened. A goblin opens the 
door on its turn and is greeted with a 
crossbow bolt to the face.
Reload
Some ranged weapons require 
an action to load and reload. A 
combatant may move no more than 
5 ft in the same turn they perform a 
Reload action.
Run 
The combatant may use an action to 
move an additional 25 ft.
Shove
A combatant can force an opponent 
to make an opposed Athletics 
check. If the attacker wins, 
they may move their 
target into an unoccupied 
space within 5 ft. 
Shoving cannot provoke 
Opportunity Attacks.
Trip
A combatant can force an opponent 
to make an opposed Athletics check. 
If the attacker wins, the defender 
falls prone.
Use Item
Using an item, such as a magic potion, 
requires an action. Interacting with 
an object, such as pulling a lever or 
picking something off the ground, 
also requires an action.
Use Skill
Using a skill, such as Heal, requires 
an action during a combat encounter.
Drawing Weapons
Unsheathing one or more weapons 
requires an auxiliary action. Held 
items can be dropped while drawing 
a weapon. Combatants incur a -1 
penalty to attack rolls if they attack 
with a weapon drawn during the 
same turn.

Unarmed Attacks
Unarmed combatants receive a -2 
penalty to attack rolls when targeting 
armed combatants. Similarly, melee 
weapon attacks performed against 
unarmed combatants receive a +2 
bonus to the attack roll. 
Natural Weapons
Some creatures possess natural 
weapons, such as talons and fangs. 
Creatures with this trait do not suffer 
any disadvantage for fighting armed 
opponents, nor do armed opponents 
gain a bonus for attacking combatants 
armed with natural weapons.
Grappling
Rather than making a normal 
attack, combatants may attempt a 
grappling check against an enemy 
within 5 ft. This attack is resolved 
as an opposed Athletics check. 
If the attacker wins, the target is 
Restrained and may not perform 
actions other than attempting to 
break free of the grapple on their 
turn. Targets possessing the huge or 
large special trait cannot be grappled 
unless attacked by a similarly sized 
combatant.
As an action, a grappled combatant 
may attempt another grappling 
check. If the grappled combatant 
succeeds, they break free and are 
no longer Restrained. 
Combatants may attempt unarmed 
or natural weapon attacks against 
their grappled target on rounds 
following a successful grappling 
check. These attacks receive a +2 
bonus to the attack roll and impose a 
-1 penalty to the target’s armor roll. 
Outside melee attacks made against 
any combatant involved in a grapple 
receive a +2 bonus to the attack roll.
Polearm Brace
Combatants armed with weapons 
possessing the repel trait may brace 
their weapons against cavalry as 
a Hold Action. When a mounted 
enemy combatant enters a braced 
combatant's reach, they provoke an 
Opportunity Attack that gains a +2 
bonus to the damage roll.

Charge
Combatants may move an additional 
10 ft before performing a melee 
attack. This movement may not 
be performed if the combatant is 
already in melee. Combatants gain 
a +1 bonus to attack and damage rolls 
during the same turn they charge.
Flying
Some combatants are capable of 
flight and may choose to fly on their 
turn rather than use their normal 
movement. While flying, combatants 
may ascend or descend in elevation. 
Flying combatants ignore obstacles 
while navigating the battlefield. If a 
flying combatant loses the ability to 
move while airborne, they plummet 
to the ground and take falling 
damage.
While flying, a combatant cannot 
perform melee attacks against 
combatants on the ground or be 
the target of melee attacks from 
the ground. Flying combatants can 
perform melee attacks against other 
flying combatants, however. Flying 
combatants that charge must end 
their charge on the ground and are 
no longer airborne.
Prone
A combatant may choose to go prone 
or rise from a prone position as an 
action. Ranged attacks against a 
prone combatant incur a -1 penalty, 
while melee attacks receive a +2 
bonus.
Dual Wielding
Combatants may carry a weapon in 
each hand, though they may only 
choose to attack with one of them 
on each turn. Dual wielding in this 
manner allows a combatant to 
quickly switch between weapons or 
benefit from the defensive properties 
of an off-hand weapon. 
Combatants may retain a two-
handed weapon with one hand while 
attacking with another weapon or 
unarmed attack.
Movement 
A combatant may move up to 25 
ft during their turn, though some 
traits and items may increase a 
combatant’s speed. Movement can 
be broken up so that a combatant 
moves before and after an action. 
Tracking the position of combatants 
can be done with a grid, every square 
tile representing a 5 ft x 5 ft area. 
Entering an adjacent tile on the grid 
would be the equivalent of moving 
5 ft.
Humanoid combatants occupy a 
single tile on the grid. Some creatures 
possess traits indicating they are 
larger than a typical humanoid and 
occupy more than a single space. 
Combatants cannot end their turn in 
a tile occupied by another combatant 
or obstacle. Combatants may pass 
through a space occupied by an ally 
but cannot enter a tile occupied by 
a hostile combatant.

Obstacles
Combatants passing through a space 
occupied by an obstacle must make 
an Athletics check. Jumping over a 
small crate may only require a DV1 
check, while quickly bounding over 
a low wall would likely require a DV3 
check. If a combatant fails to bypass 
an obstacle, they fall prone and 
cannot move or perform actions for 
the rest of their turn.
Intercepting Attacks
Combatants can intercept charges 
and ranged attacks directed at 
their allies. Interceptors must be 
standing between the attacker 
and their target, though they may 
move up to 10 ft to appropriately 
reposition themselves. Combatants 
shielding their allies in this manner 
become the attacker’s new target. 
Combatants may intercept a charge 
or ranged attack once per round and 
cannot intercept an attack if they are 
within reach of an enemy.
Ranged Attack Interception
Combatants that intercept ranged 
attacks are automatically hit and 
suffer the attack's base damage. This 
damage imposes a -3 penalty to the 
defender's armor roll.
Interceptors armed with a weapon 
possessing the block trait are not 
automatically hit and are permitted 
to make a defense roll against the 
attack roll; this represents the ability 
for shields to safely catch missiles.
Ranged Attacks 
Ranged attacks usually involve the 
use of bows and thrown weapons. 
Ammunition
Attacking with a bow exhausts one 
projectile. If a character has access 
to multiple forms of ammunition, 
they must declare which they are 
using before attacking. A weapon 
that is thrown can only be recovered 
following the encounter.
Line of Sight
Ranged attackers must be able to 
see their target, and some terrain 
imposes penalties to ranged attacks. 
Ranged attackers often need to find 
appropriate angles and elevation to 
avoid these penalties.
Concealment
Concealment is terrain that partially 
obscures a target, and it imposes 
a -2 penalty to ranged attack rolls. 
Multiple forms of concealment do 
not stack. 
Cover
Terrain that can obscure a target and 
deflect missiles is considered cover. 
Cover imposes a -3 penalty to ranged 
attack rolls. Multiple forms of cover 
do not stack.
Moving and Shooting
Ranged attack rolls incur a -2 penalty 
if the combatant moves in the same 
turn before shooting.

Opportunity Attacks
Melees are chaotic affairs that must 
be carefully navigated. Combatants 
within the reach of an enemy are 
considered threatened. Threatened 
combatants may provoke Opportunity 
Attacks if they perform actions that 
trigger them. 
Movement
Combatants perform Opportunity 
Attacks against enemies leaving their 
reach. These Opportunity Attacks 
are resolved before the threatened 
combatant may move. 
Entering the reach of an enemy’s 
melee weapon does not provoke an 
Opportunity Attack,  nor does moving 
while staying within an enemy’s 
reach.
Magic & Shooting
If a combatant performs a Magic 
check or ranged attack while 
threatened, they provoke an 
Opportunity Attack from every 
enemy that threatens them. These 
Opportunity Attacks are resolved 
before the threatened combatant 
may perform their action.
Opportunity Attack Limits
Combatants may perform no more 
than one Opportunity Attack each 
round.
Example: Two orcs leave Roland's 
reach. Roland may only make a single 
Opportunity Attack and thus must 
choose which target he attacks.
Damage
Opportunity Attacks automatically 
hit their target, the attacker dealing 
their base number of damage dice. 
The target incurs a -3 penalty to their 
armor roll against this damage. 
Disengage
As an auxiliary action, combatants 
may make a DV3 Acrobatics check to 
safely disengage from combat. On a 
success, the combatant may not be 
targeted by Opportunity Attacks. On 
a failure, the combatant may choose 
to remain in melee combat or incur 
Opportunity Attacks as normal. 
Alternatively, a combatant may 
spend an action to automatically 
disengage from combat instead of 
making a DV3 Acrobatics check.
Ranged Weapons
Opportunity Attacks cannot be made 
with ranged weapons. A combatant 
must be wielding a melee weapon 
to perform an Opportunity Attack.

Damage & Injury
Adventuring is not without risk, and 
characters will likely endure wounds 
during their journeys.
Damage Types
An attack may interact with its target 
in different ways depending on the 
type of damage it deals. Below are 
the various damage types:
Flaming
Fiery attacks deal flaming damage. 
A combatant damaged by a flaming 
attack becomes Ignited.
Frost
Freezing attacks deal frost damage. A 
combatant damaged by a frost attack 
becomes Enfeebled for 1 turn.
Holy
Demons, fey, and undead may not 
perform armor rolls against attacks 
that deal holy damage.
Lightning
Shocking attacks that deal electrical 
damage are considered lightning. 
This damage ignores armor.
Magic
Attacks that deal magic damage have 
no inherent properties other than 
their ability to harm some creatures 
that are resistant to physical damage.
Physical
This is the default form of damage 
that all attacks are assumed to deal 
unless otherwise specified. 
Poison
A target damaged by a poison attack 
must pass a DV4 Resilience check or 
become Poisoned.
Subdual
A combatant reduced to 0 Grit Points 
by subdual damage becomes Stunned 
for 1 minute but does not die.
Damage Immunity
Combatants immune to a damage 
type cannot be harmed by an attack 
that deals that damage type.
Damage Resistance
Combatants impose a -3 penalty to 
damage rolls if they are resistant to 
that type of damage. 
Example: Dragons have flaming 
resistance, meaning flaming attacks 
against them incur a -3 penalty to 
damage rolls.
Damage Vulnerability
Damage rolls gain a +3 bonus if the 
target is vulnerable to that damage 
type.
Example: Vampires have flaming 
vulnerability, meaing flaming attacks 
against them gain a +3 bonus to 
damage rolls.

Subduing Enemies
Combatants who wish to neutralize 
an enemy without killing them can 
attempt to deal subdual damage, 
even if they lack a suitable weapon. A 
combatant may deal subdual damage 
with a weapon that ordinarily only 
deals physical damage, though 
doing so imposes a -2 penalty to 
the damage roll. This simulates the 
combatant using the flat of the blade, 
pommel, or haft to strike their target.
Suffocation
Characters can hold their breath for 
30 seconds per rank in Toughness. 
When a character can no longer hold 
their breath, they must make a DV5 
Resilience check at the beginning of 
each turn until they can breathe once 
more. On a failure, the character 
suffocates and dies.
Death & Injury
When a character is reduced to 0 Grit 
Points, they are injured and must roll 
a D66 on the Critical Injury Chart to 
determine which injury they incur. 
Characters reduced to 0 Grit Points 
may continue moving and acting but 
must roll on the Critical Injury Chart 
each time they receive additional 
damage. Characters may spend 1 
Luck Point to reroll on the Critical 
Injury Chart. Non-player combatants 
immediately die when reduced to 0 
Grit Points.
Multiple Injuries
Characters can sustain multiple 
different injuries in a single combat 
encounter. If a character rolls on the 
Critical Injury Chart and suffers the 
same injury twice during a single 
encounter, they die. 
Damage Types
When a character is critically injured 
by holy, magic, or physical damage, 
they roll on the standard Critical 
Injury Chart. If another damage type 
causes the critical injury, locate the 
appropriate chart.
Feign Death
Characters reduced to 0 Grit Points 
may fall prone and feign death. If 
there are active threats on the 
battlefield, combatants will typically 
ignore a character they believe has 
already been incapacitated or killed. 
This can be a dangerous gamble, as it 
will not always deter attackers.

Morale Checks
Combatants will rarely fight to the 
death and may be required to make 
a morale check, which is resolved as 
a DV3 Leadership check; the enemy 
with the highest Leadership skill 
makes this morale check.
Enemy combatants must make a 
morale check at the beginning of 
each turn once half or more of them 
have been reduced to 0 Grit Points. If 
the morale check is failed, the enemy 
will flee from the characters.
The GM can impose a morale check 
at any time if they believe it makes 
sense to do so. A band of cowardly 
goblins may break and flee after only 
a single one of them falls in combat. 
Undead and other creatures that do 
not experience emotions never make 
morale checks and may continue 
fighting until they are destroyed.
Coordinated Attacks
If three or more combatants target 
the same character, the GM can make 
a single Coordinated Attack for the 
group rather than resolving every 
individual attack. This represents a 
single decisive blow made after a 
series of distracting attacks. The GM 
chooses which of the attackers makes 
the attack roll and then imposes a -2 
penalty to the target's defense and 
armor roll. There may be times when 
the GM believes combat will benefit 
from making an attack roll with every 
combatant, though Coordinated 
Attacks will result in faster combat.
Devastating Hits
If the SV for an attack roll is double 
that of the target's remaining GP, the 
attack is considered a Devastating Hit 
and automatically kills the target. This 
is an optional rule the GM may use to 
speed up combat. Devastating Hits 
cannot be made against characters.

Conditions
Combatants may be affected by 
one or more of the following 
conditions as a result of an attack 
or as a consequence of their own 
actions. The penalties from multiple 
conditions are cumulative.
Blinded
Blinded combatants can no longer 
see and incur a -3 penalty to defense 
rolls. Blinded combatants may not 
perform ranged attacks or cast spells, 
but may swing wildly with a melee 
weapon. Blinded combatants incur a 
-3 penalty to their melee attack rolls.
Confused
Confused combatants have difficulty 
processing the blur of combat. At the 
beginning of each turn, a Confused 
combatant must make a DV2 
Discipline check. On a failure, the 
combatant may not move or perform 
actions.
Dazed
Dazed combatants cannot perform 
Magic checks or move more than 10 ft 
during their turn. Dazed combatants 
also incur a -1 penalty to their attack 
and defense rolls. 
Deafened
Deafened combatants can no longer 
hear. Due to the verbal component 
necessary to perform miracles and 
cast spells, Deafened combatants 
incur a -3 penalty to Magic checks.
Healing
Characters must have access to a 
healing kit if they wish to perform 
Heal checks. 
First Aid
Following a combat encounter, a 
character may attempt a DV3 Heal 
check to treat a wounded ally. On a 
success, the target regains 1d3 lost 
Grit Points. A character cannot regain 
more GP through first aid than they 
began the encounter with. Only a 
single Heal check may be made to 
treat an injured character following 
an encounter.
Natural Healing
Characters resting for 8 hours or more 
recover 1 lost GP. This rest cannot be 
significantly interrupted or involve 
strenuous activity. 
Mending Wounds
Characters can attempt a DV2 Heal 
check to mend the wounds of an ally. 
On a success, the treated character 
will recover an additional 1d3 Grit 
Points the next time they rest, up to 
their maximum allowance. This Heal 
check may only be attempted on a 
character once per day. 
Neutralize Poison
Characters may attempt to treat a 
Poisoned ally by performing a DV4 
Heal check. On a success, the target 
is no longer Poisoned.

Demoralized
Demoralized combatants incur a -1 
penalty to Discipline checks and may 
not perform Leadership checks until 
they are no longer Demoralized.
Diseased
Diseased combatants lose 1 rank in 
all of their attributes. The combatant 
can be treated with a DV5 Heal 
check once per day. On a success, 
the combatant is cured and regains 
their lost attribute ranks.
Enfeebled 
Enfeebled combatants incur a -2 
penalty to attack and defense rolls. 
Enfeebled combatants cannot move 
more than 10 ft during their turn and 
cannot perform the Run action. 
Fatigued
Fatigued combatants incur a -1 
penalty to attack and defense rolls. 
Fatigued combatants also incur a -1 
penalty to Resilience checks. A full 
night’s rest removes this condition.
Frightened
Frightened combatants cannot 
approach enemies. As an action, 
Frightened combatants may attempt 
a DV2 Discipline check. On a success, 
the combatant ends the Frightened 
condition and cannot be Frightened 
for the remainder of the encounter. 
Frightened combatants cannot 
perform Leadership checks.
Ignited
Ignited combatants suffer 1DD 
flaming damage at the end of 
each round that ignores armor. 
Ignited combatants can remove the 
condition as an action, but doing so 
immediately ends their turn with the 
combatant in the prone position. 
Inspired
Inspired combatants receive a +1 
bonus to Discipline and Leadership 
checks.
Invisible
Invisible combatants cannot be seen 
by ordinary means, nor can they be 
targeted by magic or ranged attacks. 
Melee attack rolls against Invisible 
targets incur a -3 penalty, and defense 
rolls against Invisible attackers also 
incur a -3 penalty; these penalties are 
not cumulative with Blinded effects.
Poisoned
Poisoned combatants suffer 1DD 
poison damage at the end of each 
round that ignores armor. This 
damage is rolled on a black die. 
Restrained
Restrained combatants cannot move.
Stunned
Stunned combatants may not speak, 
move, or perform actions. Stunned 
combatants also incur a -3 penalty 
to their defense rolls.

Movement
The following rules govern movement 
in treacherous locations.
Climbing
Combatants can climb up to 10 ft 
each turn. An Athletics check must 
be made each turn the combatant 
climbs.  The DV of the Athletics check 
depends on the surface climbed.
Falling
Combatants falling from a height of 5 
ft must pass a DV1 Acrobatics check 
or suffer 1DD that ignores armor. 
The DV and damage each increase 
by 1 for every additional 5 ft the 
combatant falls.
Example: Roland fails an Athletics 
check while climbing up the side of a 
tower. Roland was 20 ft high when he 
failed the check and therefore makes 
a DV4 Acrobatics check. If Roland 
were to pass this Acrobatics check, he 
would fall safely. If he fails the check, 
he suffers 4DD that ignores armor.
Jumping
Vertically jumping a distance of 5 ft 
requires a DV1 Athletics check. The 
DV increases by 1 for every additional 
5 ft of distance jumped.  


Leaping 
Leaping a distance of 5 ft requires 
a DV1 Athletics check. The DV 
increases by 1 for every additional 
5 ft of distance leaped.  
Swimming 
Combatants may only move up to 10 
ft while swimming, though they may 
only move up to 5 ft per turn while 
wearing medium or heavy armor. 
While swimming, ranged attacks 
cannot be performed, and melee 
attack rolls incur a -2 penalty.
Locks
Characters with access to a burglar's 
kit can make Dexterity checks to open 
locked objects. Most locks require 
a DV3 Dexterity check to open, 
though the most complex dwarven 
mechanisms require a DV7 Dexterity 
check. If a character fails to pick a 
lock, they must gain another rank in 
Dexterity before they can attempt to 
pick that same lock again. 
Traps
Some locations are protected with 
traps; these devices can be quite 
crude or mechanically advanced. 
Acrobatics Checks
Characters targeted by a trap must 
make an Acrobatics check to avoid 
the trap's effects. The DV for this skill 
check is determined by the type of 
trap that is triggered.
Dexterity Checks
Characters may make a Dexterity 
check to disarm a trap within 5 ft. The 
DV for this skill check is determined 
by the type of trap being disarmed. 
If the Dexterity check fails, the trap 
is triggered. 
Perception Checks
Players must declare that they are 
actively inspecting a suspected trap 
or looking for hidden traps before 
rolling a Perception check to locate 
nearby traps. The DV for this skill 
check is determined by the type of 
trap being searched for.
Triggers
Traps can be triggered in a variety of 
different ways. Pressure plates that 
activate traps when stepped upon 
are common, and these typically 
occupy a 5 ft x 5 ft space. Some traps 
are triggered when a chest or a door 
is opened. More complex triggers 
can involve levers and puzzles, while 
magic traps can be activated by the 
presence of light and sound.

Darkness
While in darkness, characters are 
Blinded. Combatants obscured by 
darkness cannot be targeted by 
ranged attacks or magic. 
Monsters & NPCs
It is assumed that monsters are not 
affected by darkness and can see 
normally without light. NPCs are 
similar to player characters in that 
they are dependent on light to see 
and will need to use their own light 
sources while navigating dark places.
Light Sources
Light sources, such as torches, allow 
the characters to create light (page 
95). Monsters are drawn to light 
sources and will try to snuff them 
out. If a light source is dropped, a 
combatant can extinguish it as an 
action. Characters must spend an 
action activating a light source with 
a tinderbox or open flame.
Timer 
Once activated, a light source only 
lasts for 30 minutes (real time). It is 
important to track each active light 
source with a timer. It can be deadly 
if all light sources extinguish during 
an encounter. If the light is lost on 
the monster's turn, they continue 
their turn while the characters are 
Blinded. If the characters lose light 
during their turn, they immediately 
end their turn, Blinded.
Dwarf Holds
There are seven known dwarven 
holds on Osric Isle. These once mighty 
fortresses are now dark places filled 
with monsters.
Layout
Each mountain stronghold is unique, 
though there are a few things they 
share in common. While holds may 
contain dozens or even hundreds of 
rooms, each has at least one armory, 
hall, and vault.
Armory
Every hold maintained an armory 
filled with the finest dwarven steel. 
Armories maintain enough weapons 
and armor to supply several hundred 
warriors. Evil creatures have raided 
these armories, leaving little behind.
Hall
Dwarven kings would spend much of 
their time in spectacular halls with 
huge columns and vaulted ceilings. 
Halls are designed to impress visitors 
and demonstrate the king's wealth. 
Thrones now sit empty in the holds, 
no king having held court or feast in 
over a century.
Vault
Royal clans hoarded massive sums of 
wealth in colossal vaults. Each vault 
possesses a pair of massive rune-
inscribed doors that are impervious 
to magic. Only a key or DV7 Dexterity 
check can open a vault.

Equipment
Equipment
C
haracters begin with equipment 
and coin determined by their 
starting class. Characters may spend 
their starting coin to buy further 
equipment upon character creation. 
Other equipment may be awarded to 
a character upon creation with GM 
approval. For example, a player may 
request equipment for their character 
that reflects a previous occupation or 
social background.
Once the characters depart for 
Osric Isle, purchasing equipment will 
become more difficult. Characters will 
need to find friendly settlements or 
wandering merchants if they wish to 
buy new items.
Characters can also create their 
own equipment. Items crafted by 
characters can be customized with 
special properties, allowing craftsmen 
to create unique equipment.
Availability
When characters visit a new town or 
contact a traveling merchant, they 
may need to roll to determine what 
items are available for purchase. 
Characters attempting to purchase 
an uncommon item must make a 
Fortune check with a DV equal to the 
item’s Rarity. On a failure, the item 
is not available for purchase. Only 
one Fortune check may be made per 
item type, regardless of how many 
characters search for it.
Currency
One gold piece (gp) is worth ten silver 
pieces (sp), and one silver piece is 
worth ten copper pieces (cp). Coins 
may have culture-specific names. 
For example, dwarves refer to gold 
pieces as crowns.

Haggling
Merchants will often sell their wares 
for inflated prices and buy items for 
less than their worth. Characters can 
attempt to haggle with a Diplomacy 
check, gaining a 5% price adjustment 
in their favor for each success.
Item Slots
The amount of equipment a character 
can carry is measured in item slots. 
The weight and size of an object 
determine how many item slots it 
occupies. Small items do not occupy 
item slots. Characters can aid one 
another in carrying an object, evenly 
dividing its item slots.
Item Slot Limit
Characters begin with 10 item slots 
and gain 2 additional item slots for 
every rank they have in Might.
Example: Roland has 2 ranks in 
Might and therefore has 14 item slots 
(base 10 + 4 additional from Might).
 
Weapons
Weapons are divided into proficiency 
groups. Weapon proficiency is often 
determined by a character's class and 
special traits. If a character attacks 
with a weapon they lack proficiency 
with, they incur a -2 penalty to the 
attack roll.
Armor Penetration (AP)
A weapon’s AP value indicates the 
penalty it imposes on armor rolls.
Cost
This is the average fair market price 
for the item. 
Damage (DMG) 
This is the base number of damage 
dice the weapon deals on a successful 
hit. Melee weapons deal a number of 
damage dice equal to the character's 
Might + a modifier. The base damage 
for bows is not modified by Might.
Example: A warsword deals M+2 
damage, meaning the character 
rolls a number of damage dice 
equal to their ranks in Might plus 
two additional damage dice. The 
character’s attack roll Success Value 
is also added to this damage.
Slots
This is how many item slots the 
weapon occupies. Weapons do not 
occupy item slots while held.
Range/Reach
The range or reach of a weapon 
indicates how far away it can target 
a combatant. Ranged weapons have 
a short and long range. 
Long Range Attacks
Attacking with a ranged weapon 
beyond its short range imposes a -1 
penalty to the attack roll. 
Maximum Range
Ranged weapons cannot be fired 
beyond long range with any accuracy.

Magic
Magic
M
agic is inherently enigmatic, 
its origin often debated by 
scholars. Many desire to harness the 
arcane energy that flows through the 
world, bending it to their will through 
the formation of spells. 
Spells
Spellcasting requires hand gestures 
and incantations. Only a single hand 
is needed for hand gestures, and a 
character may temporarily remove 
a hand from a two-handed weapon 
to perform a Magic check. 
Difficulty Value
Spells vary in complexity, and each 
has a DV listed in its description. If 
a character’s Magic check meets or 
exceeds this DV, they successfully 
cast the spell. 
Duration
Some spells remain active after they 
have been cast. These spells have a 
duration measured in rounds, turns, 
minutes, hours, or days. Spells that 
last for an encounter remain active 
for the entire encounter in which they 
are cast. Active spells immediately 
end if their caster is slain.
Range
Range refers to the distance in feet 
that a spell can be cast. Spellcasters 
must be able to see their target 
unless the spell specifies otherwise. 
Spells with a touch range can only 
be cast upon targets the caster can 
touch, while magic with a self range 
can only be cast upon the caster. AoE 
spells affect a location within range.

67


Spell Save
Combatants may resist harmful spells 
by making a skill check known as a 
Spell Save. The skill used to make this 
Spell Save, along with its DV, is listed 
in the spell’s description. If the Spell 
Save is successful, the spell has no 
effect on its target.
Arcane Stress
Each time a character rolls a 1 while 
making a Magic check, they incur 1 
point of Arcane Stress. Only Magic 
checks related to spellcasting can 
cause Arcane Stress. Exploding dice 
cannot cause Arcane Stress.
Stress Threshold
Spellcasters have a Stress Threshold 
equal to their ranks in Willpower plus 
their ranks in Magic. If a spellcaster’s 
Arcane Stress meets or exceeds their 
Stress Threshold, they may no longer 
cast spells.
Removing Stress
Spellcasters lose all of their Arcane 
Stress after a full 8 hours of rest.
Dice Pools
Spellcasters may choose to roll 
fewer dice than their maximum 
allowance for a Magic check  to avoid 
accumulating Arcane Stress.
Ritual Magic
Rituals require 1 hour of incantation. 
A ritual may only be attempted once 
per day. A failed ritual cannot be 
attempted again for 1 week.
Magic Missiles
Some spells are considered magic 
missiles; these arcane attacks can 
be intercepted similarly to mundane 
ranged attacks (see page 51).
Grimoires
Spellcasters must record their spells 
within a tome known as a grimoire. 
If a spellcaster loses access to their 
grimoire, they incur a -2 penalty to 
their Magic checks. 
Characters can learn spells from 
grimoires they acquire by making a 
DV3 Academics check for each spell 
contained within. On a success, the 
character learns the spell without 
the need to spend EXP and records 
it within their personal grimoire.
Spell Damage
Spells deal magic damage by default. 
Some spells may deal other forms of 
damage, though are still considered 
magic. Spells that deal damage over 
multiple rounds only deal damage at 
the end of each round.
Spellcasting & Armor
While wearing medium or heavy 
armor, a spellcaster incurs a penalty 
to Magic checks equal to the Armor 
Value of their armor.
Sorcerous Traditions
All spells derive from the following 
traditions of arcane magic: Elemental, 
Illusionist, Imperial, Infernal, Runic, 
and Stygian.

Loss of Power
A character who defies their deity's 
will may temporarily or permanently 
lose access to miracles. Violating a 
deity’s tenets will likely result in a 
character losing their magical abilities 
for a day. A character who commits 
significant sacrilege will permanently 
be stripped of power.
Divine Traditions
Divine magic reflects the nature of 
its origin. All miracles belong to one 
of three divine traditions.
Druidic Magic
Druids usually worship a pantheon 
of gods, each manifesting an aspect 
of nature. Some scholars believe that 
elemental sorcery is derived from the 
study of Druidic Magic.
Profane Magic
Evil deities and powerful demons 
bestow their followers with Profane 
Magic. This power comes at a cost; 
evil priests are expected to perform 
vile acts if they are to maintain access 
to Profane Magic.
Sanctified Magic
Priests who serve righteous deities 
command Sanctified Magic. Miracles 
belonging to this divine tradition are 
benevolent by nature. Sanctified 
Magic is often used to protect the 
vulnerable or heal the sick. This holy 
magic may also be called upon to 
destroy evil creatures.
Miracles
Some magic-users borrow divine 
power from the deities they serve. 
This magic manifests in the form of 
miracles. Paladins and priests are 
well-known for their access to divine 
miracles.
Miracles function similarly to spells, 
though they are not considered 
spells. Unlike spells, miracles do not 
cause Arcane Stress, and armor does 
not affect their casting.
Magic Checks
Divine power is sacred and should 
be reserved for dire needs. The 
first Magic check performed during 
the day has a Difficulty Value of 1. 
The DV increases by 1 for every 
additional miracle cast during the 
same day. When a character fails a 
Magic check, they cannot perform 
miracles for the remainder of the 
day. Characters regain their ability 
to perform miracles the following 
morning, and the Difficulty Value for 
Magic checks is reset to 1. 
Ritual Miracles
Divine rituals follow the normal rules 
for ritual magic, and a character must 
have a holy book to perform them. 
Unlike other miracles, rituals have a 
set DV. Performing a ritual does not 
increase the DV for other miracles 
cast in the same day, and they can 
be attempted even if  the character 
cannot otherwise perform miracles.

sometimes terrifying creatures.

Identifying Monsters
Characters encountering a monster 
for the first time may attempt a 
DV3 Academics or Folklore check to 
identify it. A skill check that barely 
succeeds will only reveal basic 
details, though a highly successful 
one will be more enlightening. 
Stat Blocks
Every creature is provided with a 
stat block that displays how many 
dice the creature rolls for each skill 
check. The stat block also contains 
other important information.
Weapons
Each creature has its weapons listed 
in the stat block. Weapons have their 
properties detailed along with how 
much damage they deal. A creature's 
Might is already calculated into a 
weapon's damage. The total number 
of damage dice a creature rolls for an 
attack is equal to the Success Value 
of the attack + its weapon's damage.
Example: A goblin makes an attack 
roll with a success value of 2. It rolls 4 
damage dice because its spear deals 
an additional 2DD (SV2 + 2DD).
Special Traits
Monsters often possess unique 
and sometimes bizarre traits. Some 
of these special traits may impose 
penalties to enemy dice rolls. 
Multiple penalties imposed by the 
same special trait are not cumulative.

Orcs
Orcs are short and sinewy, appearing 
like hairless green chimps. While 
awkwardly hunched over, an orc's 
lean arms nearly touch the ground. 
The largest orcs are built like great 
apes and are just as strong. An orc's 
wide mouth is filled with jagged teeth 
and a pair of short tusks. Orcs have 
short lifespans, and that is perhaps 
why they live so recklessly.
Mistheim Archipelago 
North of Osric Isle is a chain of islands 
known as the Mistheim Archipelago; 
it is this frozen hell that the orcs call 
home. Orc tribes are ever at war 
over with one another, wrestling for 
control of these lands. Some warlords 
refuse to quarrel over the archipelago 
and instead cast their covetous gazes 
to neighboring lands. Reavers who 
set sail from Mistheim rarely return 
to their miserable homeland.
Society
Orcs live within tribes, and each tribe 
is ruled by a chieftain known as a jarl. 
If left unchecked, orcs will reproduce 
at a rapid and unsustainable rate; as 
a consequence, only a tribe's jarl is 
permitted to take wives. An orc that 
wishes to become a jarl must either 
murder his father or subjugate others 
in the formation of a new tribe. There 
are no taboos in orc society other 
than weakness.
Orc craftsmanship is crude and 
rarely dependable. Orc tribes depend 
on pillaging when it comes to securing 
both food and supplies. The precious 
longships that orc reavers sail the 
world in are constructed by goblins 
who are forced to perform such labor.
Religion
Orcs are children of Mologa and 
often assert themselves as being 
the greatest of their sire's progeny. 
While orcs are proud of their unholy 
ancestry, they rarely offer Mologa 
praise, as the imprisoned god has no 
real power to offer. 
The demon prince, Baal-Bolg, has 
claimed the orcs as his disciples and 
jealously hoards their worship. Orcs 
that devote themselves to the Blight 
Crow are blessed with infernal power 
and dark magic. Some orc tribes obey 
other demon princes, though they 
must do so in secret or be destroyed 
by those loyal to Baal-Bolg.
